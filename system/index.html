Why it is called a clutch

An organization is a drivetrain. Something generates the power, something chooses the gear, something decides the direction, and something transfers the force to the wheels. When an organization runs badly, people usually blame the parts. It is almost always the connections between them.

A clutch is the only component in a car engineered to slip. Everything else is built to grip. It exists so that two things spinning at completely different speeds can meet without shattering each other. Engine at four thousand rpm, gearbox not turning at all: take away the part in between and you do not get movement, you get metal filings.

That part is emotional intelligence. Not a soft skill, a mechanical necessity, and the reason the book is called CLUTCH rather than something about vision.

If you have ever driven a manual, you already know how this is learned. Nobody hands you a chart of engine speeds. You learn to hear it. You feel the moment come up through your foot, and you shift before any gauge tells you to, because the car told you first. That ear is the whole of it: reading the load, sensing the moment, knowing when to change gear and when to hold the one you are in. And the first few weeks you are terrible at it, which is worth remembering about the people you are asking to lead.

Which is also why it is not one of the four. CREATE, VOICES, the 6C Compass and DELEGATE are frameworks, each with a method you can actually run on a Tuesday. Emotional intelligence is not a step in that sequence and does not get a framework here, because it is the medium the other four move through. It has a chapter to itself in the book, and it is in every other chapter whether it is named there or not.

The four, in one line each

  • VOICES chooses the gear
  • The 6C Compass sets the direction

Emotional intelligence is the fifth thing and the first one. Not a framework, and what lets the other four meet without shearing.

The engine

CREATE

Six cylinders. An organization generates capacity the way an engine generates torque, and when one cylinder stops firing the whole thing runs rough long before anyone names why.

An engine down one cylinder still runs. It just runs rough, burns more, and gets slower in a way nobody can quite point at. Most teams diagnose the symptom. The engine light tells you which cylinder.

Ask yourself

Name the last thing that took three times longer than it should have. Which of the six was it?

If you cannot answer that in ten seconds, that is the answer.

The gearbox

VOICES

Six leadership presences, shifted by context. You do not have a leadership style. You have one gear you never shift out of, and your team has built its habits around it.

A gearbox does not choose where you are going. It decides how much of the engine’s power actually reaches the road right now. The worry leaders raise first is whether changing presence will confuse people. It does the opposite. Teams do not need you consistent. They need you legible.

Ask yourself

What gear were you in for your last three hard conversations?

Same gear all three times is a gearbox stuck in third. It will get you along a motorway and nowhere up a hill.

The navigation system

The 6C Decision Compass

Who decides, how, and when. Six modes, named out loud, so nobody has to reverse-engineer decision rights from your tone.

Satnav does not drive the car. It tells you which turn belongs to you, before the junction rather than in it. Command is not domineering, it is simply expensive, and most organizations spend it like it is free.

Ask yourself

Map your last ten decisions. How many were actually yours to make?

If the answer is seven, you do not have a trust problem. You have an architecture problem.

The handoff system

DELEGATE

Eight steps for transferring real ownership, so a handoff develops someone instead of dumping a task on them.

You can hand someone the keys and still keep a foot on the pedal. That is most delegation. Define the container, not the contents: the why, the what and the by-when. The how belongs to them.

Ask yourself

Think of the last thing you handed over. How many times has it come back to you since?

Once is a handoff finding its feet. Three times was never delegation. It was a loan.

The distinction most delegation dies on

You keep the title. They get the license.

Title

Final accountability. It stays with the leader, and pretending otherwise is how people end up carrying weight they were never given authority to carry.

License

Operational ownership. It transfers, completely, and the boundary gets said out loud rather than assumed.

Most delegation fails because these two get conflated. The leader believes they handed over the work. The team believes they were handed a test. DELEGATE separates them and makes the line explicit, which is the difference between delegation that sticks and delegation that comes back with interest.
