The problem

A dashboard with no lights on can mean the engine is fine.Or that the bulbs are out.

Teams rarely go quiet all at once. Problems start arriving later, smaller and better polished. Questions that used to come up in the meeting come up in the hallway afterward, or not at all. Nothing looks wrong, which is exactly the trouble.

Leaders usually believe their team is safer to speak up in than it is. Not from vanity: from the head of the table, the room looks fine. The person a team stops telling things to is the last to find out it has stopped. That blind spot is what this check is built to measure.

What the research says

“… the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking.”

Amy C. Edmondson, defining team psychological safety, Administrative Science Quarterly, 1999

Silence is common, it costs performance, and the people at the top feel it least

  • 85%

    of employees interviewed in one study had, at least once, stayed silent with their boss about an issue they thought was important.

    Milliken, Morrison and Hewlin, Journal of Management Studies, 2003 (40 interviews)
  • 51

    work teams in Edmondson's original study. Teams with more psychological safety did more learning – asking for feedback, testing ideas, discussing errors – and that learning is what carried through to performance.

    Edmondson, Administrative Science Quarterly, 1999
  • 22K+

    people across 136 samples and nearly 5,000 groups. Psychological safety was linked to task performance, learning, engagement and information sharing, at both the individual and the team level.

    Frazier et al., Personnel Psychology, 2017 (meta-analysis)
  • 1,440

    clinicians in 23 neonatal intensive care units. Higher-status professionals felt significantly safer than the people they worked with, and inclusive leaders narrowed that gap.

    Nembhard and Edmondson, Journal of Organizational Behavior, 2006
  • 180

    teams studied at Google: 115 in engineering, 65 in sales. Of five dynamics that set effective teams apart, psychological safety ranked first. Who was on a team mattered less than how it worked together.

    Google re:Work, Project Aristotle · industry research, not peer reviewed

The clinician finding is the reason for the leader view. The person at the top of a team is the one most likely to feel safe, which makes their own read of the room the least reliable one in it.

What you get

An honest read, and a way to tell whether you fixed it

  • Your team's results on a published research scale, with the comments de-identified and grouped into themes.
  • The gap between your view and theirs. Where you expected one answer and your team gave another, that is the finding that matters most.
  • A 30-minute debrief with me, ending on the first thing to redesign, not a list of everything that could be better.
  • A 90-day re-run when we keep working together, so you have evidence of whether the change worked instead of a feeling that it did.

How it works

From the first call to the first change

  1. We talk for thirty minutes

    About your team, your timing and whether this is the right tool. If it is, the survey is set up within a week of the call, with the email you send your team and the links for you and them.

  2. Your team takes the survey, and you take your own version

    They answer Amy Edmondson's published scale, with room for an optional comment. You rate the same scale the way you expect them to answer, before you see a single result.

  3. I de-identify the comments

    I read every comment myself and remove anything that could point to who wrote it. You get what people meant, not who said it. Results are shared only when at least five people respond.

  4. We debrief for thirty minutes

    Within a week of the survey closing, we go through the results and agree the first thing worth redesigning.

  5. If we keep working together, we run it again

    Ninety days later, same team, same scale. The second round shows whether what you changed made a difference, or whether the lights are still off. Rounds usually run in October and late January, or late January and late April for teams starting mid-year.

Where the scale comes from

I didn't write this scale. It is Amy Edmondson's, published in 1999 and used in team research ever since. It measures whether a team shares the belief that it is safe to take interpersonal risks: to raise a problem, ask for help, admit a mistake or try something new without it being held against you. What I add is the way it's run: the leader view, the de-identified comments, the debrief and the second round.

Who it's for

Any intact team with one leader

A school faculty

A whole faculty under a head of school or division head, where the people closest to students are often the last to be asked what they're seeing.

A school leadership team

The heads, deans and directors who set direction for everyone else, and who are most likely to agree in the room and disagree in the parking lot.

A company leadership team

A founder or executive and the leaders who report to them, especially once the company has grown past the point where the leader hears everything directly.

Built for teams of up to fifteen people, all working together under the same leader. Larger teams can run it too, quoted separately on a call. It isn't built for cohorts drawn from different organizations, because there is no shared team for them to describe.

Three ways to use it

Start an engagement, open a workshop, or build it into a 360

To start a consulting engagement

When I take on a consulting client, this is one way we begin. It gives us a baseline of how safe the team feels before anything changes, and a second reading at ninety days to show whether the work is landing.

To open a workshop

Several of my workshops start with it. The team takes the survey before the session, so the room begins from how people actually experience working together, not from a guess about it.

How this fits CLUTCH

Low safety is a design outcome, not a personality problem

CLUTCH is leadership by design, not personality. Silence is a design problem too: a team that won't speak up is responding sensibly to the system around it. In CREATE, safety starts in Culture and runs through every cylinder. In VOICES, the Encourager builds it and an unchecked Sculptor crushes it. In the 6C Compass, Consultative becomes fake listening when input goes nowhere. In DELEGATE, Agree on Checkpoints fails when people hide what isn't working. Emotional intelligence, the clutch, is how a leader reads the gap between how they expect the team to answer and how it does. This survey shows which part to redesign.

For invited teams

Take the survey

Think about the team you work with most closely and rate how much you agree with each statement. It takes about two minutes, there are no right or wrong answers, and the form doesn't ask for your name or email.

What happens to your answers

  • Your leader never sees individual answers. They see team results only, and only when at least five people respond.
  • I read every comment myself and remove anything that could identify who wrote it. Your leader never sees a comment as written.
  • After ninety days, I delete the data. The one exception: if your team and I keep working together, I keep it for as long as we do, so each new round can be compared with the last. When the work ends, the ninety-day clock starts.
I have a team code – open the survey

Enter the team code from your leader or from me so your answers are counted with your team's. Leaders: use the link I sent you, which opens the leader version.
