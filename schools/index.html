Who this is for

Where you sit changes the problem

Three altitudes inside a school, and one cohort that cuts across all three.

The school

Heads and senior administration

The head, the associate head, and the chiefs: enrollment, philanthropy or advancement, finance, operations, human resources, communications, equity, athletics. Division heads sit at this table too. A cabinet that agrees in the room and diverges by Thursday. Decision rights, meeting design, and the difference between a team that advises the head and a team that runs the school.

The division

Division heads, principals and directors

Head of Upper School at one school, Upper School Principal at the next, Upper School Director at the one after. The same job under three names, and the only seat that sits in both rooms: cabinet on Tuesday, your own building on Wednesday. With the deans of students, of academics, of faculty, this is the altitude where institutional strategy either becomes daily practice or quietly does not.

The department

Chairs, coordinators and team leaders

Department chairs, grade-level leads, program coordinators. Look at any school’s published leadership page and this layer is not on it, which is most of the reason it goes untrained. Promoted for being excellent at the job they no longer do, then made accountable for adults they did not hire, without the authority to replace them. Responsibility without authority, and the hardest seat in the building.

Cuts across all three

Emerging leaders, early in the career

Assistant and associate directors, new deans, the teacher who was handed a program to run in June. Real responsibility, no direct reports, and no formal training before somebody handed them the keys. This is a stage rather than a level: an emerging leader can be sitting at any of the three altitudes above.

Why this is different

Consultancy-grade analysis, from someone who has made the phone call home

Rigor is purchasable. A school can hire a firm and get real analysis, benchmarking and a clear set of recommendations. What is harder to buy is that same rigor from someone who knows what the recommendation will cost the person who has to carry it out on Monday morning.

  • I have made the call. To a parent, about a discipline matter, at 4:40 on a Tuesday, knowing the whole family will be at the game on Friday.
  • I have written the comments. A full load of report card comments in the same week as exams, where the honest sentence and the useful sentence are not always the same sentence.
  • I have rolled out the change. Divisional and school-wide, to faculty who did not ask for it, on a calendar that had no room in it.
  • I have run the professional development that did not stick. Then had to work out why. Most of this operating system came out of that question rather than out of the sessions that went well.

That is the difference between a recommendation and a plan. A deck can name what a school should do. It takes somebody who has sat in the chair to price what doing it will actually cost in goodwill, calendar and faculty attention, and to design the rollout so it survives contact with a Tuesday.

What a school can actually buy

Every one of these runs on the same operating system. The names below are the ones that make sense in a budget meeting.

  • Executive coaching for school leaders

    One leader1:1, ongoing

    One leader, a fixed cadence, and a working agenda that belongs to them rather than to me. Most engagements start with whatever keeps landing back on their desk and end somewhere structural: the way decisions get made, the way work gets handed off, the way the senior team behaves when the head is off campus.

    It always starts with the OBD. Every engagement, without exception, because a first session spent working out who this leader is is a session the school paid for and did not get. Then six 1:1 sessions. CliftonStrengths® can run alongside it in any of three forms:

    • The assessment itself, for the leader.
    • A 1:1 strengths session on what the results mean for how they actually run their week.
    • The whole administrative team together, so the cabinet has one vocabulary for why they keep colliding in the same three places.

    Two modes, and the leader always knows which one they are in. COACHING is theirs to lead: they set the agenda and find the answer. CONSULTING is mine: I bring the pattern and a direct recommendation. A division head's presence in a hard faculty conversation is COACHING. A cabinet that keeps colliding over who decides is CONSULTING. Most of it sits at the seam, and we name which one we are doing rather than blurring the two.

    Always one to one. It can be bought two ways: the school engages me for a leader, or the leader engages me themselves. The work, the cadence and the confidentiality are identical either way, and so is the price. What the board or the head hears is that the engagement is active, not what was said in it, and nothing moves to a sponsor without the leader's written consent. The engagement runs to the ICF Code of Ethics and is re-contracted at the midpoint.

    Every session runs the same arc. The leader opens by naming what they want out of the hour, and that becomes the agenda. We follow that thread. We close on what they are committing to before we next meet, so accountability is built in rather than added on. About a tenth of the value lands in the room; the rest shows up in the decisions they make over the following three weeks.

  • GoodeStart, the first ninety days

    Newly promotedNinety days

    For the chair, dean or division head who started the job in July and is now finding out what it actually is. Thirteen weeks, cohorts of five to ten, two to three hours a week. A dual-assessment baseline, four individual coaching sessions, and a curriculum that runs alongside the term rather than pretending the term is not happening.

    Less survival guide, more execution plan: what to do in the first fortnight, what to leave alone until October, and how to set the habits before the bad ones harden into how this person leads.

    If you have handed a new chair a copy of The First 90 Days, this is the version of that idea that happens in a room. High touch and in person, built on where this particular person is strong and where they are exposed, and on how your school actually makes decisions rather than how an org chart says it does. A book cannot tell a new division head which of your three loudest faculty members to answer first. That is the part they need.

    Timing is flexible, and usually straddles the summer. The common shape is a scoping conversation in the spring when the promotion is announced, the assessments and the first individual sessions over the summer while there is room to think, and the cohort work running through the fall term when the job is actually happening to them. Finished before the winter review, so the first formal conversation about their performance is not also the first time anybody has talked to them about how to do the job. Off-cycle promotions can start whenever the promotion did.

  • Building the Bench

    Emerging leadersCohort, a year

    A year-long cohort, in person or hybrid, of five to ten participants: the people you can see stepping into a team lead, chair or dean role next. The question behind it is not whether your leaders are any good. It is how many deep you are at each position, and what happens the week one of them resigns in April.

    Schools hire externally more often than they need to. Wharton research on six years of personnel data found external hires are paid 18 to 20% more than internal promotions, review significantly worse for their first two years, and leave sooner. Developing the person already in the building is the cheaper answer and usually the better one.

    Scheduling is flexible by design, because the people who most need this are the hardest to release during the day. Evenings, professional development days, or whatever gaps the calendar actually has.

    The value is partly the content and mostly the room. It is the only place most new leaders can describe a real problem out loud to people who understand the job.

    This is the school version, and it is deliberately not the corporate one. A year rather than six months, because that is how a school budget and a school calendar actually work, and the cases are drawn from divisions, departments and faculty meetings rather than from functional teams.

  • Leadership assessment and debrief

    One leader1:1, one round

    Two ways in, depending on what the school is comfortable with.

    • Instrument-based. The OBD. CliftonStrengths® and the EQ-i 2.0, both reports issued in full and unaltered, plus a written read in my own words of what the two together suggest about how this person operates under pressure, and a 1:1 debrief. Codes are bought on the participant’s behalf and the results are theirs to keep. For division heads and above, and always with at least a 45-minute coaching consult.
    • Conversation-based. No instrument at all. Structured, confidential conversations with the leader and a set of colleagues who see them work, then a written profile: where intention and impact match, where they do not, and what to do about it. For evaluation cycles and board contexts where a personality instrument is the wrong object to put on the table.

  • Decision rights and governance

    Senior teams6CWorking session

    Most school conflict that looks like a personality problem is a decision-rights problem wearing a costume. Nobody said whether this was the head’s call, the cabinet’s call, a consultation, or a genuine consensus, so everyone assumed the version that suited them and somebody left the meeting feeling overruled.

    A working session, then a written map: which decisions belong where, between board and head, head and cabinet, cabinet and department. Decided before the meeting rather than during it.

  • Delegation and role clarity

    Chairs and directorsDELEGATESeminar and review

    Schools accumulate responsibilities the way attics accumulate boxes. Someone agreed to run the thing in 2019 and nobody has opened the question since.

    Two pieces, usually bought together. Hand Over the Keys, a seminar on how to hand work over so it stays handed over, aimed at chairs and directors. And a role-clarity review that puts every recurring responsibility in the division against a name, an authority level and a deadline, which is usually the first time anyone has seen the whole list.

  • Strengths-based advisory design

    AdvisorsOWNDesign and training

    The one item on this page that is not about leadership, and the one nobody else is offering.

    Advisory already has the time. Roughly a hundred and twenty hours with the same adult across four years. What it usually lacks is a vocabulary, and a recognition that advising is not the same skill as teaching. Your advisors were hired as content and pedagogy experts. Sitting with a fifteen-year-old who will not say what is wrong is a different job, and almost nobody was trained for it.

    Built on the OWN framework and made for the fifteen-minute conversation an advisor actually has on a Tuesday rather than the workshop nobody has time for. Your advisors deliver it. Training, prompts organized by grade and behavior, a parent evening, and language students can use in essays and interviews that is genuinely their own.
