I Have Loved You Since 1989

A letter to Arsenal, on the occasion of becoming champions of England again.

Matt Goode22 May 202626 min read Get new pieces by email

# Off the clock

This isn’t my usual lane. The Leadership Lab is for founders, operators, and the people building organizations that grow people faster than they grow capability. This post is none of those things. This is a letter to a football club on the occasion of becoming champions of England again, written by someone who has loved them for thirty-seven years.

If you have zero interest, I understand entirely – skip it. I’ll be back to leadership and infrastructure next week.

Some things you just have to put into the universe. The Champions League final is in nine days. This one could not wait.

I have loved you since 1989. Thirty-seven years later, you are champions of England again.

I did not jump on a bandwagon. There was no bandwagon. Following Arsenal from the United States in 1989 was not a thing most Americans would have understood as possible. The NASL had collapsed five years earlier. MLS would not arrive until 1996. The 1990 World Cup was a year away and the 1994 one that finally gave American football its language was still half a decade out. ESPN treated soccer as a curio that aired at three in the morning if it aired at all.

The way you watched English football in 1989 was that you knew where the British pub was, or you got lucky with a syndicated highlights package on a low-channel cable station, or you waited for the FA Cup Final once a year on PBS. That was it. No streaming. No league pass. No app on a phone that did not yet exist. There was a kid in front of a television, late at night because of the time difference, watching whatever fragment of English football had crossed the ocean. A grainy broadcast. Michael Thomas charging through the midfield at Anfield, the title up for grabs, the last minute of the last game of the season, against the defending English champions and four-time European champions, at the fortress they had built that dynasty on, six weeks after Hillsborough in a fixture only rescheduled because of it, a city still in mourning, Brian Moore’s commentary cracking with disbelief.

If you want to understand the mania of that moment and what it does to a person, read Nick Hornby’s Fever Pitch, or watch the Colin Firth film if you must. The book ends on the Thomas goal – Hornby alone in his flat near Highbury, hearing the streets erupt, writing the closing sentence that every Arsenal supporter of a certain age can recite.

By the time I moved to London for graduate school you were already mine. I chose a hall in Marylebone because it was the closest to Islington. I was within walking distance of Highbury before Arsène Wenger was hired, which is to say, I was there for headlines: Who? I remember chestnuts roasting on the North Bank, the smell of them more than any goal, because that is how memory works. The big moments blur. The small ones stay.

Then I came home, and the internet slowly grew into a thing that could carry a football match across an ocean. By 2010 you could watch all of them, in HD, on a phone you carried in your pocket.

The same infrastructure that finally let me follow Arsenal from a Baltimore suburb let everyone else find them too. Supporters’ clubs in Lagos and Nairobi and Bangkok and São Paulo and Mumbai and Jakarta, packed pubs at four in the morning local time, kids in red and white in countries Wenger’s Arsenal had never visited. Football was the seed. The internet was the soil. By the time the streaming was clean enough to follow a match in real time across an ocean, there were already millions of us doing it. The congregation arrived right around the time the football had stopped being good, which meant most of them found us in the lean years, the trophyless years, the fourth-is-a-trophy years. They loved us anyway. That is a different kind of love than the love of the supporter who watched Michael Thomas at the time. It is no less real. It might in some ways be more honest, because nothing about the football was selling itself in 2014.

Supporting a club in England is a generational thing in a way Americans, with our mobility and our short attention spans, do not really understand. An English boy is born into a club the way he is born into a parish. His grandfather took his father to the ground; his father takes him. You do not pick a club because you saw a good goal on television. You inherit it. You stand where your father stood and your father’s father stood, in the same end, sometimes in the same seat the family has held for forty years. There is a continuity to it that American sport simulates but does not actually possess, because American fans move cities and switch loyalties and arrive in new markets ready to wear whatever colors the new market sells. The English do not move. The seats do not move. The clubs barely move either. When Arsenal relocated approximately 500 meters from Highbury to the Emirates in 2006, still in Islington, still in the N5 postcode, the supporters mourned for years and Wenger himself eventually said we left our soul at Highbury. When West Ham left the Boleyn Ground in 2016 for the London Stadium – three miles east, after 112 years on the same site, into a converted Olympic venue with a running track between the fans and the pitch – their supporters never accepted it. Half a kilometer is a betrayal. Three miles is sacrilege. American franchises move two thousand miles, change their names, change their colors, and the fans go with them. English fans do not. A kid who is born in Islington is born an Arsenal supporter the way another kid is born Catholic, and there is not really a moment of decision in it. Only a moment of recognition.

I am the wrong kind of supporter for that tradition. I picked Arsenal from a grainy broadcast in Maryland. There was no grandfather at the Clock End. There was no family seat. There was a kid and a television and a goal in injury time. So I gave the inheritance backwards. I took my dad to his first match. QPR on Boxing Day, when he came to visit. He played fullback in high school and taught me my love of the game. Some kids played catch with their dad; my dad took PKs with me in goal. Later I moved from goalie to striker, so I am equally happy keeping a clean sheet or scoring one, so I get 1-nil to the Arsenal. He understood the game before I did, but I think I gave him Arsenal. That is a thing a son can give a father. Not many. In England it would not have been mine to give. In America it was mine because nobody else had it. Sometimes the wrong kind of supporter finds the right kind of gift.

Then came the glory.

Wenger arrived in October 1996 from Japan, of all places. A French manager nobody had heard of, with a degree in economics and the bearing of a university lecturer. Pasta and chicken and stretching and the kind of nutrition science the rest of the league laughed at until it stopped laughing. He brought in Patrick Vieira and Emmanuel Petit from France. He kept Tony Adams and Martin Keown and the back four George Graham had built and put them behind a French midfield and a Dutch number ten in Bergkamp. And then he found Thierry Henry.

You cannot explain Henry to someone who did not watch him in his prime. The footage does not do it. Henry on a Saturday afternoon at Highbury, drifting in from the left, twenty yards out, opening his body half a step before the defender realised he was about to shoot – it was a category of football that the Premier League had not seen before and may not see again. He scored two hundred and twenty-eight goals for Arsenal across eight seasons. He played football the way certain pianists play Chopin. Like the instrument was invented for him.

Wenger won the double in his first full season. Won it again in 2002. By then the football had changed shape. Wengerball was not really a system; it was a temperament. The belief that you could win the league while playing better football than anyone else. That there was no contradiction between style and substance. That the most attractive team could also be the hardest team.

Then came the Invincibles.

Thirty-eight league games, May 2003 to May 2004. Not one defeat. Twenty-six wins. Twelve draws. The first English top-flight side to go unbeaten across an entire season since Preston North End in 1888-89, when the league had twenty-two games and most of the players also worked day jobs. We went to Old Trafford and drew nil-nil and Ruud van Nistelrooy missed a penalty in injury time that would have ended the run at seven games, and then we did not lose another league game for the rest of the season. We won the league at White Hart Lane, of all places. Vieira scored. Henry assisted. The Tottenham faithful stayed to watch us lift the title in their stadium because they had to. Some things in football are too rare to leave the building for.

Forty-nine games unbeaten in total, across two seasons. Twenty-two years later it is still the longest unbeaten run in the history of English top-flight football. Nobody has come within ten games of it. There is a 49 tattooed on my forearm. It sits in a grid with other numbers that matter to me. Birthdays, an anniversary, the dates that mark a life. The Invincibles are in there next to my children. That is what kind of fan I am.

We had been building a stadium the whole time. We had to. Highbury sat thirty-eight thousand. Old Trafford sat seventy-five. By the late nineties the gap between matchday revenue at a 38,000-seat ground and a 60,000-seat ground was the difference between competing for the league and not. Arsenal made the only decision a modern football club could make. We mortgaged ourselves to build the Emirates. Three hundred and ninety million pounds of debt, secured against ticket revenue we had not yet sold, paid down across the years we were supposed to be defending the title we had just won unbeaten. The Invincibles were not the start of a dynasty. They were the down payment.

What happened next was not a slump. It was a haunting.

Roman Abramovich bought Chelsea in the summer of 2003. By 2004 he had spent two hundred million pounds on players. By 2005 Chelsea had won the league. The market we had just mortgaged ourselves against had been replaced, almost overnight, by a market in which the price of a player was whatever a Russian oligarch felt like paying that morning. Wenger had a phrase for it. Financial doping – he said it out loud in 2004, while everyone else in the league was still pretending the money was clean. He was mocked for the phrase at the time. Every Financial Fair Play case since has been the league quietly conceding he was right.

It started with Ashley Cole. He was a Hale End boy, raised in our system, the best left-back in the world and in his prime, and we could not pay him what he was now worth because we were paying for our roof. Chelsea offered him ninety thousand a week. Arsenal offered fifty-five. In January 2005, José Mourinho and Chelsea’s chief executive met Cole and his agent in a London hotel without telling Arsenal. The Premier League fined all four parties for illegal tapping up. Cole left for Chelsea the following summer in a swap deal that, with admirable structural irony, sent William Gallas the other way.

We did not just lose a player. We lost the precedent. From that point forward, any of our best players could be tapped up by anyone with enough of Abramovich’s money to make it worth their while. And John Terry, Chelsea’s captain, stood in the middle of it all. Captain, leader, legend, the man whose entire public career was a tutorial in how to dress out self-interest as devotion to the badge. I have hangnails I have liked more than that man.

Everything that came next traces back to that summer. The football did not get worse. The money got dirtier.

And then it got dirtier still. If Abramovich was the first act, City was the second, and the second act was worse because the money was bigger and the laundering was state-sponsored. A nation-state bought a football club. Sheikh Mansour bin Zayed Al Nahyan, brother to the President of the United Arab Emirates, acquired Manchester City for around £200 million in September of that year, and then proceeded to spend more money on it than any owner has ever spent on any football club in history. The official transfer figure since the takeover is somewhere north of £2 billion. That is just what is on the books. The 115 charges – brought by the Premier League in February 2023, alleging financial misconduct across the 2009 to 2018 seasons – are still unresolved. The hearing finished in December 2024. The verdict has been expected soon for over a year. Whatever the commission eventually rules, the money that is alleged to have been moved is the money that built the team that beat us to three consecutive league titles. The football was downstream of the accounting.

And then, having built the platform, they went out and hired the best football coach of the modern era and handed him the biggest annual budget in the league for ten years running. Pep is a genius. Nobody is disputing that. But Pep with the resources of a petrostate behind him is not a sporting competition. It is a category error. We finished second to that machine three years in a row, and those second-place finishes were not failures. They were what it took to even be in the same conversation.

But after the Invincibles came the banter years.

I was there for those too. Twitter sucked. The leg breaks. The 8-2. Wenger sent to the stands at Old Trafford. Wenger memes of his sleeping bag coat zipped to the chin. He was a man whose religion had stopped working. Top four as a trophy. William Gallas wearing the number ten – the number Dennis wore, Dennis – and somehow we handed it to a man who sat down on the pitch in tears when we needed a captain.

Post-Invincibles, Wenger built his team around small, deft, technical footballers. Hleb, Cesc as a teenager, Rosický, Cazorla, Wilshere. Players who saw a pass other players did not see, players you could not catch unless you stopped them by other means. And the league did. Dan Smith broke Diaby’s ankle at Sunderland in 2006 – a giant, silky midfielder intended to replace Vieira, and we never got him back. Martin Taylor broke Eduardo’s leg at Birmingham in 2008. Do not google that video. Ryan Shawcross broke Ramsey’s leg at Stoke in 2010. Three career-altering injuries in four years to three of Wenger’s most gifted young players, all from challenges that should have ended in long bans and did not. The league was a hunting ground, and our players were the prey, and the men with the whistles looked the other way.

But the Invincibles were not soft. The Alan Davies podcast – It’s Up For Grabs Now, named after Brian Moore’s call from Anfield in 1989 – has a piece of received fan wisdom that says you need seven players in a squad who will rumble, or the league will eat you. The Invincibles had nine. Vieira would fight anyone. Petit would fight anyone. Keown would fight anyone, sometimes during games and once memorably after one. Cole would fight Gary Neville on principle. Henry would not fight you, but he would humiliate you in front of fifty thousand people, which was worse. The softness came later, after we had to sell those stalwarts to clubs we could not afford to match.

Cesc went home to Barcelona. Henry followed Cesc to Barcelona. Overmars before them. Van Persie went to United and won the title under Ferguson in his very first season in red, which is the kind of insult only football can devise. Nasri to City. Adebayor to City – and then, in his first game against us in sky blue, scoring at the Etihad and running the entire length of the pitch to celebrate in front of the Arsenal away end, knee-sliding to a stop in front of the supporters who had sung his name. Clichy to City. Touré to City. Sánchez to United. Song to Barcelona. Hleb to Barcelona.

We were a selling club because we were paying for our roof. The cruel joke is that the destinations have not aged well. Barcelona – more than a club – turned out to be less than solvent, technically bankrupt by their own audit in 2021, selling their stadium’s best seats thirty years into the future to register the players they could no longer afford. City are under investigation for financial irregularities. United are an ongoing tragedy. Chelsea are a hedge-fund experiment in real time. Twenty years on, the buyers look worse than the seller.

I was there in the early online days, when the community was small enough to feel like a pub. Arseblog before he was an institution – I was in the first couple hundred who followed. I drew the redhead logo for Gingers for Limpar. Twitter was still good then. The jokes were ours. The misery was ours. We made our own warmth because the football was not giving us any.

Arsenal essentially pioneered the modern football fan ecosystem online. The blogs and pods. The match-day Twitter accounts. The community of supporters who built something the club itself was too slow to build. Other clubs caught up. None of them had the head start. We were first because we needed it most.

Then the ecosystem turned. The same building blocks that gave us Arseblog gave us Arsenal Fan TV. Faces more famous than half the squad, monetised by being more upset than anyone else in the queue. Outrage paid better than analysis. We became the meme we used to be writing around.

talkSPORT figured out the radio version around 2010. An Arsenal fan calling in to cry about Wenger filled an afternoon. So they booked us. Every loss, every draw, every win that should have been by more. Claude from north London with the voice of a man who had not slept since Eduardo’s leg. The hosts laughed. The producers cued it up. The rest of the league learned to think of Arsenal as the club whose own supporters could be relied on to deliver the post-match indictment, free of charge, on a competitor’s airtime.

Don’t shitpost your club. Supporters are allowed to complain. They are not allowed to monetise the complaint.

The shirt is not a microphone. The badge is not content.

And now, after the win, the same supporter ecosystem has produced its mirror image: the celebration police. Twenty-two years without a title and there is a genre of content whose job for the week is telling us how to feel about the one we just won. Calm down, it’s only the league. Have you forgotten the Invincibles? This squad isn’t that good. You won by points, not style.

Predictably, the celebration police are mostly former United players and the occasional sycophantic journo. Wayne Rooney – the dive in match fifty that ended the Invincibles’ run at forty-nine – watched our players celebrate reaching the Champions League final with the fans on the pitch after the Atletico second leg and criticised them for it. Reaching the final. Arsenal legend Ian Wright had to publicly tell Arsenal fans to ignore the celebration police – his phrase, not mine. They should put a statue of Wrighty outside the Emirates already. Roy Keane – former United hardman, never met an Arsenal player he did not want to kick – has been at this for over a decade. In 2014, after we reached the FA Cup final, he said our players needed a reality check for daring to celebrate at the final whistle. Last season they celebrated finishing fourth and now they celebrate beating a Championship side on penalties – we are talking about Arsenal FC here. Jamie Carragher – Liverpool legend, never won a league, current Sky Sports moralist about teams that do – told Martin Ødegaard to “get down the tunnel” in 2024 for the crime of taking a photograph after we beat Liverpool. In March of this year, with us still top of the table, he wrote that neutrals were rooting against us and called our online presence hysterical. The week before the Etihad in April, he predicted that an Arsenal defeat would make Pep Guardiola champion in May. When neither his prediction nor his rooting interest landed, he wrote a follow-up explaining why everyone else dislikes Arsenal. The follow-up is always there. The follow-up is the job. Gary Neville – also of United, who spent the 2004 fixture at Old Trafford kicking José Antonio Reyes every time he touched the ball, has spent two full title races publishing variations on the same column: Arsenal are not good enough, the squad is not deep enough, the title will be City’s, the title will be Liverpool’s, the title will be anyone else’s. He was wrong every week and back on air every week. The job is not to be correct. The job is to keep the suspicion of Arsenal in the air.

You see this on no other club. You did not see it for City the first time, or the fifth. You did not see it for Liverpool in 2020. You did not see it for United for two decades. The genre exists only for Arsenal, because the genre that preceded it – the AFTV meltdown, the talkSPORT call-in – trained the rest of football to think of our joy as suspicious. The world learned to hold us to a standard it holds no one else to. We taught them how.

And then there is the manager.

Mikel Arteta came to Arsenal as a player in 2011 from Everton. He captained the side from 2014, lifted two FA Cups, retired in 2016, and the following summer was on the bench at Manchester City as Pep Guardiola’s assistant. He stayed three years. He won two Premier League titles on Pep’s bench. Pep noticed something his other assistants did not do – when City scored against everyone else, Mikel jumped; when they scored against Arsenal, he stayed seated. Pep said it out loud years later: that guy likes Arsenal.

In 2018, when Arsène finally left, Arsenal passed Mikel over and hired Unai Emery instead. Eighteen months later, with the club in tenth place and the wheels coming off, they finally called him. He left City. He came home.

His first season we finished eighth, our worst league position since 1995. He won the FA Cup in August anyway – the first Arsenal manager to win a major trophy in his first season since George Graham in 1987. The second season we finished eighth again. Three painful years of teardown followed. Aubameyang exiled. Özil exiled. Mustafi and Xhaka shipped out. The backline rebuilt from scratch. By the time he was done, Arsène’s last side had been entirely sold or retired and the new spine was eighteen, twenty, twenty-two years old. Saka. Saliba. Martinelli. Ødegaard. Rice.

And then the title races came. Every one of them, every spring for four years, against the same man. Pep on one touchline, Mikel on the other. The teacher who had taught the student, the student who had spent three years studying the teacher’s notebook from inside the classroom. Each season closer than the last. Each second-place finish to the same team that had once been Mikel’s classroom. The fourth time of asking, the apprentice got him.

And here is the part nobody outside the family understands.

February 2008. Five points clear at the top. Martin Taylor broke Eduardo’s leg at St Andrew’s, we drew that match conceding a 95th-minute penalty, William Gallas sat down on the pitch in tears at the final whistle, and we won three of our next twelve. We finished third. Eduardo never played for us at that level again. In 2018-19 Liverpool took ninety-seven points – still the highest second-place total in Premier League history – and lost the title to City by one. They lost only one match all season. Klopp said it on the way out: as long as City had the money, no team was passing them easily. And then there was the refereeing. The penalties that did not go to us against City and did go to them against us. The yellows that should have been reds. The two-point margin in 2024 was inside the margin of decisions a neutral observer could not have called the same way twice. We did not need a conspiracy. We needed an explanation, and the explanation was the same explanation Liverpool had been given, and Eduardo, and Diaby, and Ramsey. The machine finds a way. The universe pays the bill in a currency the table does not record.

So we did not say it. Not in February when we were level. Not in March when we went a point clear. Not in April when the maths started to bend our way. Amy Lawrence wrote about this unique Arsenal psychology (trauma) for The Athletic in the spring of 2023 – trukke trukke, a phrase she picked up from Roma supporters chasing a Scudetto in 2001. The point of trukke trukke is that you do not name the thing you want, because to name it is to risk it. You call it the run. You call it what is happening. You do not call it the league. Amy wrote that piece in April 2023, when we were top and the title looked ours and you could feel it slipping at the edges. City won twelve in a row from February. We knew the rules even then. We talked around the word. We lost it anyway. We talked around it for two more seasons after that, and lost those too.

So this year we did not talk about it at all.

The reason we did not is that we had all seen the same horror movie too many times. The machine in sky blue. The petrodollar infrastructure. The genius on the touchline. And the giant robot at the centre of it all – Haaland, six-foot-five and built in a factory we cannot afford, the late-game equaliser that always seemed to be coming. The Etihad, ninety-eighth minute, Stones rising to head in the goal that stole us two points and then Haaland in Arteta’s face at the final whistle – telling our manager to stay humble. BTW, Haaland has not won the league since.

Sitting on the Arsenal bench that night, unused, was an eighteen-year-old kid called Myles Lewis-Skelly. Hale End since he was eight. Raised in Islington, a stone’s throw from the Emirates, born into the parish. He was wearing the forty-nine on his back because when they offered him a first-team number he turned it down. He knew what forty-nine meant to Arsenal. He wanted to keep it. After the final whistle Haaland got in his face too – an unused substitute, a kid he had never heard of – and shouted who the fuck are you? The kid took the insult and said nothing.

But, the next time Arsenal played City, the kid was starting at left-back. Sixty-third minute, Arsenal already 3-1 up, the score on its way to becoming 5-1. Lewis-Skelly turned in the box, slotted past Ortega for his first senior Arsenal goal, ran to the corner flag, and sat down cross-legged in Haaland’s meditation pose with the entire Emirates rising behind him. Haaland saw it. Haaland did not like it. The Emirates already had a chant – forty-nine, forty-nine, undefeated – for the Invincibles run. After that goal they added a new verse. Forty-nine, forty-nine, Lewis-Skelly. He’s one of our own.

And then there was Bernardo Silva, with two games to go, with us two points clear, with the title we were about to win sitting on the table between them and us. Told The Athletic, on the record, that we needed to man up. About the team that had spent five months at the top of the table while his own had been chasing. The contempt was institutional. We were the soft team. The team that did not want it enough. The team that did not bottle it because we never had the bottle in the first place. There was a meme that did the rounds all year – a City supporter caught on camera drinking out of an Arsenal water bottle in his own stadium, drinking out of us, with the running joke underneath: bottlers, bridesmaids again, here comes another second place. They were so sure. We were so sure they were right.

You watch enough of those and you stop trusting the table. You stop trusting the lead. You stop trusting the maths. The fixture list looks fine and you do not believe it. The gap is six points and you do not believe it. Because somewhere out there is the machine, and the machine is going to find a way. It always finds a way. It found a way in 2022 when Aubameyang was put in a fridge and we collapsed in the final weeks. It found a way in 2023 when we went top in January and City won twelve straight to catch us. It found a way in 2024 when we put up the highest second-place total in Premier League history and still finished second. You learn, after enough seasons of that, that the table is a trick the league plays on you. The table is a thing the universe is preparing to take away. Do not name it. Do not look at it directly. Do not say the word.

The thing that surprised me about this season was not that we won it. It was that, somewhere around March, I noticed I had stopped flinching. The body learns to expect the wound. And then one week it does not come, and the next week it does not come, and you realise you are watching a team that is going to do this. But I still did not say it. Right up until the maths made it impossible to lose, I did not say it.

And then, somehow, the bill came due in our favour. The machine did not find a way. The giant robot stayed in his factory. Bernardo Silva flew home with no medal. The City supporter drinking out of our bottle watched us lift a different one. And Myles Lewis-Skelly – in the team room where the squad watched City drop points against Bournemouth to mathematically hand Arsenal the title – held up a magnum of champagne and said it to a camera: they called us bottlers, but now we have the bottles. The kid said it. The maths held.

Fourteenth league title. Champions of England.

Nobody outside England fully understands what that means in 2026. The Premier League plays through Christmas. Boxing Day. New Year’s Day. There is no winter break in any meaningful sense. PSG took two full weeks off in December and came back to a fifth-tier cup tie. Bayern took most of January. We played Wolves away on a cold, dark, wet Tuesday while PSG players were sunning themselves on a beach in Dubai.

PSG are another state-sponsored machine. Owned by the Qatari state, just as we were pushed by Abramovich’s roubles and Abu Dhabi’s petrodollars before. The difference is that PSG do not have to work as hard as City, because they have no domestic competition. Ligue 1 is a one-club league. PSG have won eleven of the last thirteen titles, and the team chasing them this year was Lens. Their reigning Ballon d’Or winner, Ousmane Dembélé, has started nine league matches all season. Nine. The best player in the world does not play every game because there is no game to play. Luis Enrique rotates because he can. Arteta rotates because somebody is injured.

Riccardo Calafiori – our gorgeous, oft-injured left-back – has logged nineteen hundred competitive minutes in the league and in Europe this season, and that is the version of him that keeps breaking. Dembélé has played a third of that time and is the best player in the world. The fixture list is not the same fixture list. The calendar is not the same calendar. We are not playing the same sport.

The league below us is the deepest in the world by every honest measure. The hardest top to bottom. Wolves at home is a fixture other top clubs in Europe simply do not have on the calendar. Brentford away in December is a fixture you can lose. We do not get to take our feet off the pedal. And then there are the London derbies. Tottenham twice. Chelsea twice. West Ham twice. Crystal Palace twice. Fulham twice. Brentford twice. London is a city of six top-flight clubs and the emotional weight of that calendar is something only London clubs carry. Manchester City have one rival in their own city and that rival has been a sub-Champions League team for over a decade. Ferguson left and United fell apart, slowly, then all at once. Arsène left and we wobbled. We tried two managers who could not hold it together. And then we built it again, properly, with Arteta and an actual football operation behind him, and we are about to walk into a Champions League final having played a calendar that PSG would not recognise.

PSG eliminated us in the semi-final last year. They are our opponent in nine days, in Budapest.

Nobody knows what will happen against PSG. That is not the point of the letter. The point is that for the first time in a long time the bill came due in our favour. And we did not have to name it for it to arrive.

One more thing.

Eberechi Eze grew up an Arsenal supporter. Boyhood Gooner – in his case the cliché was true. Released from Hale End at 14, he bounced around, before finally becoming Crystal Palace’s talisman and leading them to win the FC Cup last year. Tottenham tried to sign him last summer. He could have gone. The wages would have been there. The minutes would have been there. The route was open. He did not want to go. Famously he called Areta, then drove in circles around the Emirates Stadium until the deal was confirmed. BTW, he scored five against Spurs this year. The boy who loved Arsenal, from a family of Gooners, who was released from the club, eventually got to grow up and play for them and win the league with them, all in one career. That does not happen often. It almost never happens. Except this year.

If there is a moral to thirty-seven years of this, it is that one. Sometimes the wait is the whole of it.

Trust the process.

Jump on the fun boat.

Sometimes it works out.
