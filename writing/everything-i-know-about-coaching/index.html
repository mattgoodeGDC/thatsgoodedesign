Everything I Know About Coaching, I Learned from Ted Lasso

Why a fictional football coach reveals the shift teachers – and advisors – need most

Matt Goode9 December 202514 min read Get new pieces by email

# Coaching# Schools

A quick note before we begin…

I’m currently supporting the faculty at an independent school as they integrate CliftonStrengths into their advisory program. They’re rolling it out thoughtfully – year by year – and right now, only the freshmen have taken the assessment and know their strengths.

Which means my training isn’t about memorizing 34 strengths or understanding domains. It’s about helping teachers make a fundamental distinction between two very different skill sets: teaching and coaching.

Because even without strengths language, teachers need to understand how coaching works – and more importantly, how it’s different from what they already do brilliantly in the classroom.

Today I was putting the finishing touches on my slide deck, and found myself typing two words: “Stay curious.”

The moment I hit enter, something clicked. That phrase – so simple, so central to coaching – opened a creative floodgate. I realized I’d seen that exact principle embodied perfectly in a show I’d resisted watching for years.

That’s what this essay is really about.

I’ve spent twenty years as a teacher, advisor, and middle school dean – followed by years designing leadership systems at USAID and coaching teams through complex human dynamics. With that background, you’d think I would have learned everything I know about effective coaching from formal trainings, PD sessions, or credentialing programs.

But the clearest, most accurate example of great coaching I’ve ever seen didn’t come from a workshop or a textbook. It came from a fictional, mustachioed football coach who didn’t even know the offside rule.

Before you roll your eyes – hear me out.

Because here’s the part I don’t usually admit: I almost never watched Ted Lasso at all.

The character debuted years ago in an NBC commercial where Ted becomes the new coach of Tottenham Hotspur – a detail that, as a lifelong Arsenal fan, was enough to shut it all down immediately. Spurs? Absolutely not. Interest terminated on sight.

I take my football seriously. Football is life.

So I skipped the show entirely. I didn’t watch a single episode while it aired. Only after the entire series had concluded – and after enough people insisted, “Matt, you of all people need to see this” – did I finally give in and binge it.

And to my surprise, beneath the biscuits, the mustache, and the Premier League loyalties I’d rather not discuss, I found something else entirely:

The most accurate, human, psychologically grounded depiction of authentic coaching I’ve ever seen on television.

Because there’s a moment that plays out inside every school: a student hovers in your doorway before asking a question; another blurts out an answer a beat too quickly; another “forgets” an assignment with a look that signals something deeper than laziness. Young people rarely narrate their inner lives directly. Their truth shows up first in behavior – brief, flickering signals adults must learn to read with care.

Teachers respond the way they were trained: They interpret. They analyze. They jump to meaning.

Teaching is about clarity.
Coaching is about curiosity.

In a classroom, interpretation is essential. It helps teachers hold patterns over time and guide learning with intention.

But in coaching – and in advisory – that same instinct becomes the very thing that shuts down a student’s voice before it ever has the chance to emerge.

Teaching is interpreting behavior.
Coaching is inviting meaning.

And this is where Ted Lasso, of all people, becomes such an unexpected teacher. Not because he uses coaching vocabulary – but because he embodies the posture, presence, and restraint that authentic coaching requires.

And the show gives us example after example.

But First: A Critical Distinction (and a Lesson in First Impressions)

Let’s be clear about something: Ted Lasso is a terrible football coach.

He doesn’t know the game. He can’t diagram plays. He doesn’t understand offsides rule. When his team needs tactical expertise, he turns to Nate, Beard, and Roy – people who actually know football.

And when I first saw him in that NBC commercial, he seemed like a clown. A joke. Comic relief about American ignorance of the beautiful game.

But here’s the thing about first impressions: they’re often wrong.

Ted looked like a clown because he didn’t have football expertise. But that’s precisely what made him brilliant at something else entirely.

Because Ted is a brilliant people coach.

  • Athletic coaching is about expertise: knowing the game better than your players, teaching technique, designing strategy, and making tactical decisions.

  • People coaching is about believing in their capacity, asking questions that unlock their own wisdom, creating conditions for growth, and holding space for self-discovery.

Athletic coaches need to know more than their players.
People coaches need to believe their clients are creative, resourceful, and whole.

Ted knows nothing about football. But he knows people. And that’s why the show works – it demonstrates what happens when someone chooses presence, curiosity, and belief over expertise and interpretation.

This is also a lesson in avoiding snap judgments – which is exactly what coaching requires.

When a student shows up at your door acting like they don’t care, or asks fifteen questions about an assignment, or rushes through their work, your first impression might be: They’re being difficult. Again.

But coaching requires you to pause at that behavior long enough to ask: What if I’m wrong? What if there’s another story here?

Ted looked like he didn’t belong. That first impression was accurate – he didn’t belong as a football tactician. But he absolutely belonged as a people coach.

The same is true for your students. Their behavior might look like one thing. The meaning behind it might be something entirely different.

This is the exact shift teachers need to make in advisory work.

  • In the classroom, you’re the athletic coach: you know the content, you design the learning, you guide the strategy.

  • In advisory, you’re the people coach: you trust students to discover their own answers, you ask rather than tell, you believe rather than fix.

Here are seven lessons from Ted that show what that looks like – and how they map to the Behavior → Meaning → Action framework students are craving:

1. Establishes and Maintains Agreements (Season 1, Episode 1 - “Pilot”)

The Scene: Ted’s first team meeting. The players are skeptical, the press is mocking him, and everyone expects him to perform confidently. Instead, he says, “I’m gonna fail. Probably fail a lot.”

The Lesson: Most leaders walk into new situations trying to prove they belong. Teachers do this too – especially early in the year when you’re establishing authority and credibility.

Ted does the opposite – he establishes authentic agreements about what he WILL bring (curiosity, belief, support) and what he needs from them (patience, honesty, effort). No false expertise. No hidden agendas. No pretending he knows the offside rule.

He’s admitting upfront: I’m not here because I know football better than you. I’m here because I believe in people.

Ted calls it “being straight with folks.”

The Connection to Your Framework: Ted isn’t interpreting their skepticism as a sign of disrespect or resistance. He’s creating the conditions where honest meaning can emerge later. He’s saying: I won’t jump to conclusions about your behavior. Let’s start with what’s real.

The Insight: Co-creating the relationship from day one creates safety. Pretending creates suspicion. In advisory, especially, students can smell inauthenticity instantly. You don’t need to pretend you have all the answers about their lives. You need to be clear about what you’re actually offering: invitation before interpretation.

2. Cultivates Trust and Safety (Season 1, Episode 4 - “For the Children”)

The Scene: Richmond has just been humiliated on the field. The locker room is silent, defeated. And Ted walks in and hangs the “BELIEVE” sign – not after a win, not after progress, but after they’ve gotten demolished.

The Lesson: Here’s what most teachers miss in advisory: trust isn’t built through competence or perfect advice. It’s built through unconditional positive regard, even when students are struggling.

The team thinks he’s delusional. But what Ted’s actually doing is creating a container of belief that exists independent of results. That’s psychological safety – the conviction that your value isn’t contingent on your performance.

An athletic coach might hang that sign after a win – as a reward, as validation.
Ted hangs it after a loss – as a foundation, as faith.

This is the advisory equivalent of saying: “I see how hard today was. I believe in you anyway.”

The Connection to Your Framework: Ted sees the behavior (the loss, the defeat). He doesn’t interpret it as a lack of effort or character. He doesn’t rush to fix it. He holds space for them to find their own meaning in the struggle – and he believes they will.

This is what it looks like to stay at the door long enough for students to step into the room.

The Insight: Trust comes from believing in students before they give you evidence that they deserve it. Not after. That’s what creates the foundation every coaching relationship – and every advisory relationship – needs. Results follow trust, not the other way around.

3. Maintains Presence (Season 2, Episode 8 - “Man City”)

The Scene: Ted freezes during the match. His vision narrows. He can’t breathe. He has to leave the sideline – the one place a coach is supposed to stay.

The Lesson: Here’s the dirty secret of coaching – and teaching – when YOUR stuff interferes, you can’t serve the student. This is the competency most teachers violate while pretending they’re fine.

Ted’s panic attack is the moment he realizes he needs help. And critically, he gets it. He doesn’t push through. He doesn’t “lean in.” He tells Dr. Sharon, “Something’s really wrong with me,” and he does the work to fix it.

Most teachers? They keep showing up broken and call it “being dedicated.” They confuse authenticity with bleeding on students who didn’t cut you.

The Connection to Your Framework: You can’t help students access meaning when your own unprocessed meaning is flooding the room. Ted recognizes his basement behavior (anxiety, disconnection, panic) and does the work to understand what it means – so he can show up from his balcony again.

The Insight: Teaching requires stability. Coaching requires presence. And presence requires self-awareness. Knowing when your internal state is no longer safe for someone else’s growth – and stepping back – is sometimes the most responsible act.

Coaching doesn’t require honesty. It requires a safe space where it is possible.

4. Listens Actively (Season 1, Episode 2 - “Biscuits”)

The Scene: Ted asks Nate – the invisible kit man everyone ignores – “What do you think we should do about our set pieces?” Then he waits. He leans in. He nods. He takes Nate’s answer seriously when it finally comes.

The Lesson: This scene is perfect because it shows Ted doing what athletic coaches almost never do: admitting he doesn’t know and genuinely asking someone else.

An athletic coach would design the set pieces themselves – that’s their job. Ted asks Nate – and then actually listens.

Active listening isn’t just hearing words. It’s believing the quietest person in the room has something valuable to say.

This is coaching presence: Ted doesn’t rescue Nate. He doesn’t fill the silence. He doesn’t soften the question. He stays at the door – at the behavior – long enough for Nate to step into the room.

And when Nate does, it changes everything. That one question changes Nate’s trajectory from the margins of the club to the strategic heart of it.

The Connection to Your Framework: Ted notices the behavior (Nate watches everything, knows the game deeply). He doesn’t interpret it (”Nate’s shy” or “Nate lacks confidence”). He invites Nate to make meaning of what he’s been observing. And Nate chooses the action – the strategic insight that helps the team.

Behavior → Meaning → Action.

This is what happens in advisory when adults stop supplying meaning and start inviting it.

The Insight: Most teachers ask questions but don’t wait for answers. They’re just reloading their own interpretation while students talk. Ted waits. He receives. He believes the student has something worth saying. That’s the difference between listening and performing listening.

5. Evokes Awareness (Season 2, Episode 1 - “Goodbye Earl”)

The Scene: Roy Kent’s retirement party. He’s adrift – no longer a player, not sure who he is anymore. Ted asks, “What do you want to do next?”

The Lesson: Ted doesn’t give Roy advice. He doesn’t fix his problem. He doesn’t say, “You should coach!” or “Have you thought about commentary?” He asks the question that makes Roy think differently about his future – a future Roy hasn’t really considered because he’s been too busy being angry about his past.

An athletic coach would tell Roy what to do – they’d have a plan, a strategy, a next step. Ted asks Roy to discover his own answer.

That’s coaching – evoking awareness in the client, not downloading your wisdom onto them.

This is the critical distinction for teachers moving into advisory work:

Teaching (athletic coaching): Here’s what I think this means.
Coaching (people coaching): What does this mean to you?

Roy eventually discovers the answer on his own – and that’s why it sticks.

The Connection to Your Framework:

“This is where agency lives. This is where coaching starts. Access meaning through inquiry – not interpretation.”

Ted sees Roy’s behavior (anger, drift, disconnection). He doesn’t interpret it or fix it. He asks Roy to make sense of it himself. And that meaning leads to Roy’s own action.

The Insight: The best coaches – and advisors – aren’t the most intelligent people in the room. They’re the ones who ask questions that make students feel like the smartest person in the room. One creates dependency. The other creates agency.

6. Facilitates Growth (Season 1, Episode 10 - “The Hope That Kills You”)

The Scene: After Richmond chokes on penalty kicks, Ted designs practice around the thing they failed at. The team practices penalties. Over and over. Missing. Trying again. Missing. Trying again.

The Lesson: Here, Ted actually does function like an athletic coach – he designs the practice, sets up the conditions, and creates the structure.

But notice what he doesn’t do: he doesn’t rescue them. He doesn’t take the kicks for them. He doesn’t soften the difficulty.

Growth doesn’t happen in comfortable spaces. It happens in the repetitive, frustrating, unglamorous work of trying the thing you failed at until you don’t fail anymore.

Ted creates conditions where they can learn from failure. He holds the space. He watches them struggle. He lets them build the muscle memory of resilience.

This is advisory in its purest form: creating conditions where students confront difficult emotions safely, without being saved from discomfort.

The Connection to Your Framework:

“Coaching ends with a future-oriented choice. Tiny. Doable. Chosen by the student.”

Ted doesn’t tell them how to fix their penalty kicks. He creates the conditions for them to practice the action over and over until they develop their own mastery. The action is theirs.

The Insight: Facilitating growth means designing experiences that stretch students, not shielding them from discomfort. If you’re protecting students from hard things, you’re not developing them – you’re infantilizing them. Love doesn’t mean easy. It means believing they can handle hard.

7. Empowers Through Curiosity (Season 1, Episode 8 - “The Diamond Dogs”)

The Scene: The dart game at the pub. Rupert is mocking Ted, the crowd is laughing, and before Ted throws the winning dart, he tells them all: “Be curious, not judgmental.”

The Lesson: This might be the whole game. This is the line that separates athletic coaching from people coaching.

Athletic coaches make judgments – they have to. This player is better than that one. This strategy works; that one doesn’t. That’s their job.

But people coaching isn’t about having answers – it’s about asking better questions. When you approach every interaction with genuine curiosity instead of judgment, you unlock what the student already knows. You create space for them to discover their own wisdom rather than adopt yours.

That’s the coaching standard in seven words. It’s also a coaching doctrine for teachers in advisory.

Judgment collapses possibility.
Curiosity keeps it open.

It’s the same instruction I give faculty when we work on neutral observation:

Interpretation (athletic coaching): “You seem disengaged.”
Curiosity (people coaching): “I noticed you stayed quiet. What was happening for you?”

The difference is everything.

The Connection to Your Framework: Curiosity allows you to stay with the behavior without jumping to interpretation. It’s what invites the student to make meaning. It’s what creates the conditions for them to choose their own action.

Be curious.

Invitation before interpretation.

Choice before correction.

The Insight: Curiosity is a discipline, not a personality trait. It’s a choice you make in the moment when your brain wants to judge, fix, or solve. Ted chooses curiosity. Every time. Even when judgment would be easier, faster, or more satisfying. That’s what makes him a people coach instead of just a nice guy with opinions.

Why These Scenes Matter for Teachers

Schools already have the architecture for coaching: advisory groups, small communities, and a relational culture.

But what many lack is the skill set Ted models so consistently – and it’s not about football expertise. It’s about:

  • Noticing behavior without jumping to interpretation

  • Inviting meaning instead of supplying it

  • Holding space for students to choose their own action

  • Staying curious instead of certain

  • Believing students are creative, resourceful, and whole

This is why faculty wrestle with coaching work: Interpretation is how teachers survive the classroom. It is a professional reflex, a point of pride. It’s what makes you a great athletic coach in your subject area.

But advisory work requires a different muscle: staying with the behavior long enough for the student to offer the meaning.

Ted Lasso succeeds as a leader because he never confuses interpretation with understanding. He knows that meaning belongs to the person living it – not the person observing it. He knows he doesn’t have football expertise – so he gets out of the way and lets the people with expertise (Nate, Beard, Roy) do that work, while he focuses on what he does know: people.

And that is the lesson teachers are perfectly positioned to embrace.

Students are craving adults to help them make sense of themselves.

In the classroom, you’re the athletic coach: you know the content, you design the learning, you guide the strategy.

In advisory, you’re the people coach: you trust students to discover their own answers, you ask rather than tell, you believe rather than fix.

Teaching guides learning.
Coaching guides self-awareness.
Advisory lives at their intersection.

When teachers learn to pause at the behavior – to notice without assuming, to ask without steering, to hold the moment without rushing – students step into the room with more clarity and confidence.

They stop performing the identity adults assign them.
They begin discovering their own.
