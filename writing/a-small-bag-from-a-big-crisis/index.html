A Small Bag from a Big Crisis

Matt Goode3 February 20265 min read Get new pieces by email

# Off the clock

There is a small bag I keep with my travel kit in my home in Ellicott City. It was gifted to me by Maggie, a member of our Bureau of Humanitarian Assistance Nutrition Team, a group of subject-matter experts whose sole mission was to turn American taxpayer dollars into calories that kept children alive in places most Americans will never see.

I keep it as a reminder of a project I learned about during my time at USAID’s Bureau of Humanitarian Assistance, before the agency was gutted and I was shown the door. It is not an expensive bag. It is not a particularly beautiful bag, truthfully, and ironically, it’s a little too small for “all my cords.” I use it nevertheless to carry my cords when I travel – a small, practical thing filled with chargers and adapters. But it was made by hand, in a refugee camp, from materials that would otherwise have clogged a drain or poisoned a water source. Every time I unzip my bag in an airport lounge or a hotel room, I am holding proof of what humanitarian aid can become when someone decides to stop treating people like problems.

I think about that pouch more than I probably should.

Watch this short video story about the bag and the people who made it.

A year ago this month, I was RIF’ed from USAID’s Bureau of Humanitarian Assistance. I’ve written about that experience elsewhere – an essay called “One Year Later: What USAID Meant, and What Remains” that has gone somewhat viral, at least in the humanitarian and development world.

The other day, I received a LinkedIn connection request from Maung Myint Swe – a Rohingya refugee advocate, human rights activist, and freelance journalist covering security issues, migration, and the refugee crisis. A man who lives the story I’m about to tell you.

To understand why a recycling project in a refugee camp matters, you need to understand who lives there.

The Rohingya are a Muslim ethnic minority who have lived in Myanmar’s Rakhine State for centuries – some trace their presence back to the fifteenth century. Despite this, Myanmar has never recognized the Rohingya as one of its 135 official ethnic groups. A 1982 citizenship law rendered them stateless – stripped of the right to vote, to move freely, to attend university, to exist as full human beings in the country of their birth. The United Nations has called them one of the world’s most persecuted minorities.

In August 2017, the persecution became genocide. Myanmar’s armed forces launched what they called “clearance operations” in Rakhine State – a campaign that included mass killings, systematic rape, the burning of entire villages, and the murder of children. At least 6,700 Rohingya were killed in the first month alone. Over 750,000 people – half of them children – fled to Bangladesh, most on foot through jungles or by sea across the Bay of Bengal.

They arrived in Cox’s Bazar, where 33 camps were constructed, forming the world’s largest refugee settlement. Today, more than a million Rohingya live in Bangladesh, the vast majority in and around Kutupalong and Nayapara camps. The shelters are bamboo and tarp. Monsoon season brings floods and landslides that repeatedly destroy homes. Fires are common and devastating in the cramped conditions – a single 2021 fire displaced 45,000 people.

These are not temporary camps. The Rohingya have been there for eight years now. They are not going home. Conditions in Rakhine State remain too dangerous for safe return, and Myanmar’s military shows no sign of accountability. The world’s largest stateless population lives in the world’s largest refugee camp, and the funding is drying up – roughly 22% of the UN’s response plan has been funded as of mid-2025.

That is the context. That is who we are talking about when we use the term “aid.”

Now picture 860,000 people living in the camps of Teknaf and Ukhiya. They survive on relief. Food comes in packaging. Packaging becomes waste. Waste – polythene, non-biodegradable materials – piles up in a place with no municipal services, clogs drains, fouls roads, and creates the conditions for disease in a settlement already stretched past capacity. The physical residue of aid becomes its own kind of hazard.

ICCO – a Dutch humanitarian organization now part of Cordaid, and on the ground in Cox’s Bazar since the day the Rohingya crossed the border in 2017 – saw something different. They saw a resource problem disguised as a waste problem. With funding from the World Food Programme, technical guidance from UNDP, and upcycling expertise from GARBAGEMAN, a Bangladeshi social enterprise, they launched the Circular Food Assistance project at a recycling and skill development center in Camp 15, Jamtoli.

Not an international NGO parachuting in with a prefab solution. A Bangladeshi company is teaching Rohingya refugees how to turn the camp’s own waste into sellable goods. Today, roughly 500 people are involved – collecting, sorting, and upcycling plastic waste into rucksacks, laptop pouches, bracelets, bin bags, and wallets. Workers are paid by the WFP. GARBAGEMAN provides the technical training. Cordaid/ICCO manages the center and runs skills development. But as Shakeb Nabi, Cordaid/ICCO’s country representative in Bangladesh, put it: “The driving force is the Rohingya community. They do the work and run the place.”

Women who had been defined entirely by what was done to them began to define themselves by what they could make.

That is where Maggie’s pouch comes from. Not a factory. Not a charity warehouse. A refugee camp where someone decided that displaced women were not just mouths to feed but hands that could build.

A year on, I am no longer at USAID. The agency I served has been hollowed out by people who understood the cost of everything and the value of nothing. The programs we supported – the ones that kept human beings alive in places the news cameras left years ago – are fighting for scraps of funding against a political environment that treats compassion as waste.

But I still use that bag. And somewhere in Camp 15, a woman is still sorting polythene into something worth having.

The humanitarian sector has always been better at saving lives than at asking what comes next. The Circular Food Assistance project is a small-scale answer to that enormous question. It does not solve the Rohingya crisis. It does not undo genocide. It does not replace citizenship, rebuild villages, or bring back the dead. But it does something the aid world too often forgets to do: it treats displaced people as capable adults with agency, not just populations to be managed.

When we talk about the impact of organizations like USAID and WFP, the numbers are real and staggering – millions fed, millions alive who would not be otherwise. But the truest measure of humanitarian impact may not be the number of calories delivered. It may be the moment when aid stops being something done to people and becomes something built with them.

People do not want to be saved. They want to be equipped.

I know this because there is a small bag that proves it.
