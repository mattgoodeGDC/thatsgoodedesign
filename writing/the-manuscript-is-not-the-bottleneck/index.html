The manuscript is not the bottleneck

The second job

Matt Goode6 October 20267 min read Get new pieces by email

# Everything runs through me# Off the clock

I recently finished a novel that was fine. Competent sentences, a plot that moved, a few scenes I would have cut. Before the author had even finished the manuscript, it had sold film rights. It was a first novel.

I closed it and asked the question every self-published writer asks eventually, usually late at night: how did that happen? Or more precisely: why hasn’t that happened for me?!

Luck? Skill? Both, in proportions nobody publishes.

I’m not asking out of bitterness. Well, not only. If you read this Substack, you know my lane: I help founders build the systems their companies run on. That’s my day job, as I call it. But self-publishing, under my own name and under my pen name, has turned out to be almost like starting a second company. OK, it is starting a second company. Maybe a third.

I’ve put two books into the world through my own imprints, and a third is finished and waiting on its first readers. What I’ve learned is the thing the writing workshops skip. Self-publishing doesn’t make you an author. It makes you a founder. The book is the product. Everything after the last page is distribution, operations, and sales, run by a team of one. Last week I wrote about how I systemized my own work, and this is why, in addition to growing Goode Design & Consulting, I accidentally started a second (or third) company: my nascent publishing empire. I needed a system to run on.

The Martian started as a blog

Andy Weir is the patron saint of people like me. He was a software engineer whose earlier attempt at a novel went nowhere with agents. So he posted The Martian on his own website, chapter by chapter, for free. Readers asked for an ebook. He put it on Kindle for 99 cents, the lowest price Amazon allowed, and it climbed the charts. Then an agent called. Then Crown. Then Fox. Then Matt Damon.

Notice what his readers were doing along the way. They weren’t just reading. They were emailing him corrections to his orbital math. By the time a publisher noticed, a crowd of people already had a stake in that book. That isn’t discovery. That’s a founder with early customers.

People tell Weir’s story as proof that quality rises. Read it again. He built an audience, and the audience did the discovering. The publisher wasn’t betting on Weir. It was buying a result.

The billionaire version

This week The New Yorker reviewed MacKenzie Scott’s new novel, Last Days in Two Nations, which she published in installments on Substack. Plenty of people have wondered aloud why she skipped Amazon. I don’t think it needs much of an explanation.

The review was rough on the craft. But The New Yorker reviewed a self-published, serialized novel, which is more attention than most of us will get in a lifetime. When your name already does the distributing, you can afford to walk away from the biggest bookstore on earth.

The review’s sharper point is that she couldn’t fully escape it anyway. Kindle Direct Publishing built the road most self-published fiction now travels, and even a writer stepping around Amazon is walking through the landscape it made. I’m on that road too. So is every indie author I know. The difference is that most of us don’t get to choose.

Each book taught the next one

Here’s what the founder’s job has looked like, one book at a time. Each one taught me something I used on the next.

  • Balance taught me that a reader is not a review. My first novel, published as m kirk, found good early readers. Then I learned that Amazon doesn’t treat every review the same, and a free early copy can quietly cost you one, no matter how much someone loved it. I had about 20 early readers for Balance, and most of their reviews didn’t show up – they shared them with me – because they never bought the book. I gifted them a copy. Amazon tracks it. I also learned I didn’t own the book’s ISBN, the thirteen-digit number bookstores and libraries use to find it. I’d taken Amazon’s free one, which can’t leave Amazon. Getting the paperback into bookstores and libraries now takes a second distributor and a second ISBN. None of that is writing. All of it decides whether anyone reads.

  • CLUTCH is where I applied those lessons, and found new ways to trip. It’s my leadership book, published under my own name. This time I bought my own block of ISBNs from Bowker, the agency that issues them, so the book’s identity belongs to me, not to Amazon. I launched it at $9.99 to get copies and reviews moving. Then I went live before registering the ISBN, so for its first week on sale, CLUTCH was missing from the catalog booksellers search. I didn’t know I’d missed a step until I went looking. On Amazon, the publisher line reads “m kirk,” my fiction pen name, because of one field Amazon locks the moment you hit publish. Changing it means a support case. That mistake sent me down a road I hadn’t heard of a few months ago: imprints, the publisher name printed on a book’s copyright page and carried in every bookseller’s catalog. I now run two. Tramaridge Press publishes the books under my own name, and The Fern publishes the novels under m kirk, so the two names stop bleeding into each other. Plus copyright. I registered it for CLUTCH. Balance went out before I knew to, so its paperwork is a tangle: the words are mine, but proving it on paper is something the lawyers will need to sort out before Anne Hathaway signs on to play Ellie in the movie. And there’s no Kindle edition of CLUTCH, on purpose: the book asks you to work in it with a pencil. That was a design choice, and it costs me readers every day. I’m OK with that; it was meant to be written in, and an ebook just doesn’t have that kind of vibe.

  • The Wrath of Archie Ellis gets everything the first two taught me. It’s a novel of Passchendaele, 1917, and it’s finished. The ISBN is mine, and this time it gets registered, and the publisher field checked, before I hit publish. I’m also building readers before launch, not after, which is Weir’s lesson. That starts with advance reader copies: free print copies for people willing to read early and post an honest review. On Instagram, I set up an automation: comment YPRES on my pinned post, or message me, and a reply arrives with the request form. Everything I post there also goes to a Facebook author page, and I joined a bunch of historical fiction and WW1 history groups. I have even (gasp) started a TikTok. I hold my nose every time I open it. The US company that now runs it still lists ByteDance as its largest single shareholder, and I’m not naive about where my data goes. But if it sells books, I’ll post.

Writing the books was the part I’d do for free. This is the rest of the job.

I think my novels are good. Not Great American Novel good, but better than some books I’ve read that came with film deals. I mean, they’re at least worth a Booker Short List, though. That matters less than I’d like. A good book nobody finds and a bad book nobody finds sell the same number of copies.

Talent is not a distribution strategy

I see the same pattern with the founders I coach. The best idea in the meeting doesn’t win. The idea with a path does: a sponsor, a forum, a route through the people who decide. Founders who say “the product speaks for itself” are usually the ones whose product nobody has heard of.

Quality is a precondition, not a plan. Weir had readers before he had a publisher. Scott had a name before she had a Substack. Whoever sold that debut novel had a pitch the rest of us never see. In every case, distribution came first, and the craft came along for the ride.

Luck is real. But luck needs somewhere to land. I’m building mine one comment trigger at a time, and this post is part of it.

Read it before anyone else does.
