Build Guardrails, Not Gates

Matt Goode18 June 20263 min read Get new pieces by email

# 6C# DELEGATE

“I just want them to use their judgment,” she told me – a founder who’d spent the last quarter approving expense reports, reviewing every client email, and signing off on calls three levels below her. She built the company on heroics, doing whatever it took, and when that stopped scaling, she did the natural thing: she added control. Approvals. Sign-offs. Run-it-by-me-first. And somehow every decision still landed on her desk, now with more steps in front of it.

She’d traded one trap for another. Heroics had made her the engine. Control made her the bottleneck. Both kept the whole operation running through her.

The two traps look like opposites

Most leaders cycle between them. Heroics feels like leadership – you’re in the work, saving the day – right up until you’re exhausted and nothing moves without you. So you swing the other way, into control: gates, approvals, sign-offs. That feels responsible. But it produces the same result by a slower road. Every move waits for you, and the team quietly learns to stop deciding.

The fix isn’t somewhere in the middle, looser or tighter. It’s a different axis entirely.

Gates or guardrails

A gate blocks. Nothing passes until you open it. Every decision becomes a request routed up to you, and your team idles at the barrier, waiting for a yes.

A guardrail does the opposite. It defines the edge of the road – here’s the budget, here’s the line you don’t cross, here’s what “good” looks like – and then it clears out of the way. Inside the lane, people move at full speed without checking over their shoulder, because they know exactly where the limits are and trust that you won’t second-guess a call that stayed in bounds.

Caption: Gates vs guardrails – both create order; only one creates speed.

Why guardrails are faster

Think about how a highway actually works. The reason you can drive seventy isn’t the absence of limits – it’s the presence of good ones. Lanes, shoulders, barriers. The structure is what makes the speed both safe and possible. Strip the guardrails out, and nobody goes faster; they slow to a crawl, because now every inch is a judgment call with no margin for error. Guardrails don’t restrict speed. They make it possible.

Teams run the same way. Gates feel safer, but they tax every decision with a wait. Guardrails feel riskier, but they’re the thing that lets a capable team move without you in the room. The leader who builds gates stays at the checkpoint forever. The one who builds guardrails becomes something better – the person who set the lane lines once, so everyone else could drive.

And here’s the quiet relief in it: guardrails are less work than gates, not more. A gate demands your attention every single time someone reaches it. A guardrail you build once, and it holds. You’re not approving, and you’re not absent. You set the edges, you back the calls made inside them, and you get your week back.

Worth sitting with this week:

  • Where have you installed a gate – an approval, a sign-off – that’s really just routing decisions back to you?

  • What’s one decision your team could own completely if they knew the guardrails?

  • Are you spending your week opening gates, or could you set the lane lines once and let them drive?
