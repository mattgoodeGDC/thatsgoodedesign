Psychological Safety × Accountability

Essay 4 of 5

Matt Goode24 November 202512 min read Get new pieces by email

# Psychological safety

Why the best leaders don’t balance trust and standards – they integrate them.

There’s a question leaders whisper far more often than they’ll admit aloud:

“I want people to feel safe… but I’m afraid we’ll lose our edge.”

I hear this everywhere – in federal agencies navigating political volatility, in founder circles wrestling with scale, in boardrooms where the stakes are high and time is short. Most recently, I heard it from a VP in a fast-growth tech company who looked worn down by what he believed was an impossible tradeoff: protect people or push them; build trust or demand excellence; create comfort or cultivate discipline.

That tension isn’t a failure of leadership – it’s a misunderstanding of how people learn and grow.

And this is where my entry into the field diverges from most voices writing about leadership. Before I ever coached executives or redesigned systems, I spent decades as a teacher. And anyone who has taught – truly taught – knows something most leaders never learn:

People grow at the intersection of safety and challenge.
Not safety alone. Not challenge alone.
The stretch lives in the middle.

In education, we call this the Zone of Proximal Development – the space in which a learner can do more with guidance and support than they can do on their own. Too much comfort, and learners stall. Too much pressure, and they shut down. But in the right conditions – the right blend of trust, clarity, and rigor – something remarkable happens: they surprise themselves.

That same developmental principle applies to adults. I call the organizational version the “Stretch Zone.”

It is the adult expression of the ZPD: a high-trust, high-clarity environment where expectations are unmistakable, where speaking up is not a gamble but a norm, and where learning happens publicly rather than in private silos. It’s the climate in which people take genuine ownership, not because they fear consequences, but because they’re invested in the mission and confident in the system around them.

And here is the central insight:

Psychological safety and accountability are not opposites.
They are co-requirements of adult learning and high performance.

Edmondson’s research at Harvard, Google’s Project Aristotle, and work from MIT Sloan, Stanford, and the Academy of Management Journal all point toward the same conclusion: trust doesn’t dilute standards – it enables them. Safety is what turns accountability from surveillance into stewardship.

Where one of these elements is missing, organizations slip into two predictable patterns:

  • Comfort without momentum – high safety, low clarity. People feel good, but nothing moves.

  • Compliance without engagement – high standards, low safety. Work gets done, but thinking shuts down.

But when both are high?

That’s the Stretch Zone – the environment where people rise into challenge rather than shrink away from it.

It’s where pressure becomes productive instead of corrosive.
Where candor becomes contribution instead of critique.
Where mistakes become information instead of indictment.

And although I now see this daily in organizations, the truth of it was etched into me long before I ever coached a senior leader.

What I learned about human psychology teaching middle schoolers

When a middle-schooler doesn’t feel safe, they don’t risk being wrong.
They don’t admit confusion.
They don’t raise their hand even when the answer sits fully formed in their mind.
They fold inward. They self-protect. They retreat into quiet because quiet feels safer than exposure.

And when a student feels pressure without support, they don’t accelerate – they disconnect. Their minds leave the room long before their bodies do. They stare past the page. They shrink themselves because smallness feels survivable. Anyone who has stood at the front of a classroom knows this look: the look of a learner who is present in name only, navigating the material with caution instead of curiosity.

But alter the conditions – establish clear expectations, create room to try, normalize mistakes that aren’t tied to shame – and something far more powerful emerges. Students begin to lean toward difficulty rather than away from it. They ask the second, harder question. They try approaches they’ve never attempted. They recover faster after missteps because the environment teaches them that a misstep is not a verdict, but an invitation. They start taking intellectual risks that would feel impossible in a climate of threat.

What many leaders overlook is that this dynamic does not disappear with age.
It simply acquires a professional vocabulary.

Adults aren’t different creatures. They’re children with more experience, more language, and more elaborate defenses. The psychology hasn’t changed – only the stakes have.

A student who won’t risk being wrong becomes an employee who won’t name the risk. A child who hides confusion becomes a manager who hides emerging problems until they harden. A teenager who braces for criticism behaves exactly like a director who edits themselves into strategic silence because candor feels dangerous.

The instincts are identical – self-protection over exposure.

And when leaders treat psychological safety and accountability as interchangeable levers, they inadvertently activate these same instincts. They generate friction where they intend to generate growth.

Because you cannot demand excellence from an environment that punishes vulnerability. And you cannot cultivate excellence in an environment that avoids standards and structure.

The Stretch Zone is not a managerial preference; it is a developmental necessity. It is the only space where human beings – regardless of age, title, or talent – reliably move from potential to actual performance.

Safety as the engine of true accountability

Modern leadership still clings to a dangerous myth: that accountability is what happens when pressure rises.

It isn’t.

Pressure alone produces anxiety, distortion, and political behavior – not ownership.

When people feel threatened, they don’t step forward into responsibility; they drift sideways into self-protection. Instead of offering their full perspective, they begin to calculate what is safest to say, trimming nuance and editing themselves before anyone else has the chance. Their updates become technically accurate but strategically hollow, scrubbed clean of the early intuitions and subtle warning signs that leaders most need to hear. They participate, but only in the safer, narrower ways that shield them from scrutiny. And from the outside, this can look deceptively like discipline. Leaders often misread the polished reports, the procedural compliance, and the quiet agreement around the table as signs of alignment and professionalism, when in reality the organization is tightening in all the ways that make learning impossible. People are no longer solving problems; they’re reading the room.

Threat doesn’t produce accountability. It produces distance – emotional, cognitive, and relational.

Real accountability emerges from a fundamentally different place: a belief that the work truly matters and that the environment is sturdy enough to hold the truth. When people trust both the standard and the system upholding it, they stop performing safety and begin practicing stewardship. They don’t hide inconvenient information or soften their assessments; they offer their best thinking because they know it will be met with curiosity rather than punishment. In these teams, difficult conversations are not deferred to hallway whispers or late-night messages. They happen in the room, at the precise moment when candor can still shape the direction of the work.

In environments like this, dissent does not require permission. It is simply part of how the team makes sense of complexity together. And mistakes are not treated as moments of exposure but as data – signals the system needs to adapt, improve, and stay honest.

As this climate establishes itself, candor ceases to be an act of bravery and becomes an act of alignment. People don’t share because they’ve mustered courage; they share because the culture has normalized truth as a form of respect.

Once psychological safety is consistent and expectations are unambiguous, people move differently. They stretch toward difficult problems instead of tiptoeing around them. They take bigger creative risks because curiosity outweighs self-preservation. They ask more incisive questions because the goal is clarity rather than survival. They surface risk earlier, when it is still small enough to be changed, instead of waiting until it has hardened into crisis. And importantly, they learn publicly – not because they enjoy vulnerability, but because they understand that transparency accelerates the work.

None of this is softness. It is the most sustainable form of rigor: rigor built on trust rather than fear, rigor that strengthens rather than strains, rigor that treats people as the system’s intelligence instead of its liability. When safety and standards rise together, accountability shifts from compliance – something enforced – to commitment, something shared by people who know the work matters and trust the environment enough to contribute fully.

Leadership is pedagogy in practice

The longer I work with organizations, the clearer it becomes: Leadership is applied pedagogy, and organizational design is applied learning theory.

Everything leaders do – consciously or not – shapes the learning environment their people inhabit. The smallest interactions, the tone of a meeting, the way ambiguity is handled, even the pauses before a leader responds – all of it signals something about what is safe, what is valued, and what is allowed. And the patterns I watched play out in classrooms for years show up with astonishing fidelity in adult systems. The contexts change. The stakes rise. The vocabulary gets more sophisticated. But the underlying psychology – how humans respond to safety, clarity, challenge, and relational trust – remains remarkably consistent.

This is why psychological safety can never be reduced to an HR initiative or a cultural “nice-to-have.” It is not a mood or a perk or an interpersonal preference. It is the foundational condition that allows people to think boldly, name issues early, and test ideas without worrying that visibility will cost them standing, credibility, or belonging. Without that condition, even the best strategies stall because people spend more cognitive energy on self-protection than on problem-solving.

No strategy – however elegant – compensates for an environment where people are bracing. No headcount plan substitutes for a culture where people are filtering. No OKR framework rescues a team that is performing certainty instead of practicing truth.

What leaders are truly doing, whether they articulate it or not, is architecting the learning environment their team will have to think inside of. They are determining whether learning accelerates or collapses. Whether information flows or bottlenecks. Whether dissent strengthens the work or disappears into private channels. Every system tells the truth about how it was designed.

In organizational life, the conditions that create a healthy adult learning environment are remarkably consistent, and they mirror what strong educators have always known:

  • Trust that steadies people enough to try – the kind of relational grounding that reassures people that visibility is not a liability. When trust is present, people stop managing impressions and start contributing insight.

  • Clarity that removes guesswork and creates direction. Ambiguity forces people into protective behaviors; clarity liberates their cognitive bandwidth for actual work. It transforms scattered effort into focused effort.

  • Standards that challenge without shaming – expectations that inspire stretch rather than fear. High standards without humiliation generate the kind of productive tension that fuels growth and excellence.

  • Voice that ensures truth travels early instead of late. When voice is structurally supported rather than merely permitted, teams surface risks while they are still reversible rather than after they have calcified.

  • Support that transforms mistakes into shared insight instead of private burden. Robust support structures signal that missteps are not career-defining events but part of an adaptive learning process.

Individually, each of these conditions matters. But together – when they reinforce one another – they create an environment where stretch becomes natural instead of rare. People begin to lean into difficulty rather than avoid it. They ask harder questions, notice earlier inflection points, and collaborate with a level of sincerity that feels radically different from the posturing and politeness that dominate more brittle cultures.

And when these conditions take hold, performance changes in ways leaders often don’t anticipate. The team becomes less guarded and more generative. Meetings shift from choreography to contribution. Energy that once went toward managing fear is suddenly available for solving actual problems. Insight compounds because people are no longer navigating interpersonal danger; they’re navigating the work.

Strategy can set direction.
Resources can expand capacity.
But only conditions unlock capability.

Organizations don’t rise to the level of their ambition; they rise to the level of the environment leaders create for them.

The real leadership question

At this point in the series, we’ve traced the early signals of silence, explored why safety accelerates speed, and examined the invisible tax fear imposes on systems. With this essay, we add the fourth truth: rigor and trust must rise together or they collapse together.

The question at the heart of this, the one that sits beneath every essay so far, is this:

Where have you leaned too far into comfort or too far into control –
and what might become possible if you stopped choosing between them?

Most cultures don’t need reinvention. They need a subtle but intentional redesign of the conditions that govern how people speak, challenge, question, and learn.

What you are ultimately building is the organizational equivalent of every great classroom:

  • clear enough that people know what excellence looks like;

  • courageous enough that they can name the truth;

  • structured enough that participation is required;

  • human enough that the stretch feels supported rather than scrutinized.

The Stretch Zone is exactly that: not a place where people are pushed harder, but a place where they are supported better. Where standards aren’t softened but scaffolding is strengthened. Where accountability shifts from surveillance to shared stewardship.

And in a landscape where organizations are often either brittle from pressure or sluggish from over-accommodation, the ability to cultivate this environment may be the sharpest competitive advantage a modern leader can create.

When safety and rigor rise together, performance becomes sustainable, learning becomes continuous, and momentum becomes collective.

Five Questions to Gauge the Stretch Zone

(Adapted from Edmondson’s work and practical systems coaching)

Use a 1–5 scale:

  1. Team members hold each other accountable respectfully.

  2. It’s clear what is expected of me in my role.

  3. Feedback is welcomed and acted upon – across all levels.

  4. Leaders model learning from mistakes and encourage reflection.

  5. People are encouraged to take ownership – not just tasks, but outcomes.

I’ve also included a simple Google Form – built on Edmondson’s five questions – that you can copy and use to track this dimension of psychological safety with your team. Click here for a copy of that form.

These aren’t abstract cultural ideals – they are behavioral signatures of high-learning, high-rigor systems. Research from Harvard, MIT Sloan, and HBR consistently shows that teams scoring high on these items adapt faster, innovate more consistently, and navigate complexity with far less friction.

They also reveal whether accountability flows only downward or truly circulates throughout the system – an essential marker of upward psychological safety, the single strongest predictor of whether teams challenge assumptions early.

What emerges from this reflection is not just cultural data – it’s a performance map:

Where energy flows.
Where ownership thrives.
Where fear or ambiguity slows everything down. –

The practical question is simple:

Which of these five items, if improved even slightly, would create the biggest shift in how your team works, learns, and leads?

What happens when we separate safety from standards

Across sectors – tech, education, humanitarian response – I see two patterns whenever organizations separate psychological safety from accountability:

Culture as Cushion
Leaders overcorrect for safety by avoiding directness. Expectations blur; performance drifts; frustration grows. Harvard Business Review calls this pseudo-harmony – the appearance of cohesion covering the absence of candor.

Accountability as Weapon
Standards are high, but dissent is punished. People comply without engaging. Problems surface late. Leaders get filtered data. Risk-taking evaporates. Accuracy becomes more dangerous than silence.

The Stretch Zone is the alternative – disciplined, human, sustainable. A space where feedback signals respect rather than risk.

And here is what leaders often forget:

AI will never build this for you.

AI can measure output, summarize feedback, and detect patterns in language.
But it cannot feel the temperature of a room.
It cannot read the emotional voltage beneath silence.
It cannot sense the hesitation before someone speaks the hard truth.
It cannot replace the human work of building trust.

That work begins in presence.
It grows through practice.
And it scales through design.
