Paul Revere Was the Failure

Matt Goode21 April 202610 min read Get new pieces by email

# DELEGATE# 6C

Paul Revere was not supposed to matter.

Hi, welcome from Boston. Those are fighting words. I wasn’t planning on writing this week; I’m up here to visit my parents. We missed each other at Easter, so I decided to come visit them this week. While I was here tho… an idea popped into my head. Yesterday was Patriots’ Day – the running of the Boston Marathon - but also the anniversary of the shots heard round the world - the Battle of Lexington and Concord.

And of course, why did the colonists “win” the day – because of course, listen my children and you will hear of the midnight ride of Paul Revere.

But hold on a sec… hear me out.

The Sons of Liberty spent years engineering a system that was never supposed to depend on him. They built Committees of Correspondence in every town. They drilled militia captains weekly. They pre-positioned powder and pre-arranged rally points. They designed signal protocols so redundant that if the primary messenger went down, the message would still reach its destination. They sent three riders that night, not one. Dawes took the land route. Prescott joined in Lexington. The lanterns in the Old North Church were a backup in case no rider got through at all.

The whole architecture was designed to refuse a hero.

And yet.

What do we remember? Longfellow, 1860. Hoof beats. Lanterns. One man warns a sleeping country sounds great, right? Eighty-five years after the ride, a poet wrote the architecture out of the story and wrote a single rider in. Total myth. But a lot sexier than “systems” or “procedures.” The receiving culture, the redundancy, the drilled captains, the parallel routes, the sexton in the steeple. All of it collapsed into one silhouette on a horse.

That is the failure I want to talk about. It is not a failure of Revere. He did his job. The failure is that the system the Sons of Liberty built was good enough to win a war and bad enough to get remembered as one man’s midnight ride.

This is the hero worker problem.

In my work, I call someone a “hero worker” when the organization has come to depend on them in a way the original design never intended. You know the one. The person whose out-of-office email makes three VPs panic. The name that shows up in every postmortem as the reason the crisis did not become a catastrophe. The employee whose resignation letter triggers an emergency leadership meeting before the coffee is cold. They are not villains. They are often the most committed person in the building. The problem is structural. The organization runs through them, and when they are tired, unreachable, or gone, the system does not realize what it has lost until it tries to operate without them.

Every founder I work with has a Paul Revere. Sometimes two. Sometimes the founder is the Paul Revere.

Here is the part of the Revere story most of us did not learn.

Paul Revere never made it to Concord.

He was captured by a British patrol outside Lexington. Stopped on the road, questioned, and held for hours. I did not know this for most of my life. A few years ago, my brother-in-law took me running on the route. He pointed out the spot where Revere was arrested. I stood there, breathing hard, and said what most people say when they find out. What do you mean, he was arrested? How did the message get out then?!

Seventh-grade history did not cover that part. Not for me, probably not for you unless you grew up in Boston. The poem does not cover it either. Longfellow does not want you to know his hero never made the last leg. Because the moment you know that, the whole story changes.

The message reached Concord because Samuel Prescott did. A local doctor who joined the ride in Lexington, who knew the roads, who kept going when the other two riders were stopped or thrown. Prescott finished what Revere started. Take that, Malcolm Gladwell - stay with me here... *

The militia companies mustered on time. The redundancy held.

The system worked because Revere failed.

Read that sentence again. The system worked because Revere failed. If the Sons of Liberty had bet the revolution on him, the British patrol that stopped him on the road to Concord ends the story. But they did not bet on him. They built something that could survive his capture. Prescott was not a backup plan after the fact. He was the design. The redundancy is what saved the revolution.

That is what a receiving culture is. An engineered condition. In the CREATE framework, it is the intersection of three of the six cylinders: Culture, Teaching, and Execution, running in sync long before the message arrives. Culture is what makes the captain willing to get out of bed. Teaching is what gave him the drill patterns he executes without thinking. Execution is the muscle memory that turns a warning into a mustered company in forty minutes. One cylinder is missing, and the ride is a ghost story.

The Sons of Liberty built all three. They built them patiently, without credit, in towns whose names nobody wrote a poem about. The receiving culture won the day at Concord. The ride got remembered.

The 6C Decision Compass maps the authority conversation the Committee of Safety had before the British marched. Six decision modes – Command, Consultative, Collaborative, Consensus, Core Expert, Choice – crossed with five delegation levels, one grid that tells you exactly where the line sits before you hand anything off.

The Massachusetts Provincial Congress did not improvise its authority structure on April 18th. Six months earlier, in October of 1774, it had voted Joseph Warren and the Committee of Safety formal power to alarm and muster the militia. It had directed every town to raise companies of minutemen. It had pre-positioned the stores at Concord and designated who held which call. By the time Warren sent Revere out the door on the night of April 18, the authority conversation had already happened. When Prescott picked up the warning in Lexington and carried it into Concord, he was not asking for permission from Boston. He was operating inside a decision architecture the Provincial Congress had built six months earlier, under which any competent militiaman in any town had pre-authorized authority to alarm his own neighbors. That is what the 6C does. It takes the conversation most organizations have in the middle of a crisis – who gets to decide this right now? – and moves it to a time when nobody is bleeding, and everyone is thinking clearly. Without that grid, every handoff becomes a negotiation in the dark.

DELEGATE is the eight-step handoff architecture for building the Prescott your organization needs, rather than promoting the person closest to ready and hoping the rest follows. Most leaders treat delegation as a single event. A title changes, an inbox gets forwarded, a Slack channel gets transferred, and the assumption is that ownership has moved with the paperwork. It has not. Ownership transfers through eight steps – including the Title versus License distinction most organizations collapse into one – and skipping any of them leaves the new owner holding the title without the license to act. Prescott did not finish Revere’s ride because he had been handed a business card. He finished it because the Provincial Congress had built the underlying competence, context, and authority into every minuteman company in Massachusetts, long before the night anyone needed it. That is the difference between a promotion and a handoff. A promotion is the announcement. A handoff is the architecture. DELEGATE is how you build the second, so you stop relying on the first.

The chairman of the Committee of Safety – Joseph Warren, the man who engineered the authority structure and dispatched Revere on the night of April 18 – died at Bunker Hill two months later. He did not live to see the system he built run at scale. It ran anyway. That is what a receiving culture is supposed to do.

Outlive the architect.

And we’re back…

Most of us in advisory work spend our days on the wrong end of this inversion. A client walks us through the save. The all-nighter. The call to the vendor. The founder who flew out at four in the morning to close the deal. We are supposed to be impressed. And the save was real. But the story the client is telling is a Longfellow story. One rider. One crisis. One legend. The receiving culture, if it exists, goes unnamed. If it does not exist, nobody notices because the ride is covered for its absence.

The question the client almost never asks is who was already awake.

Who had been organizing the downstream response before the crisis hit? Whose prior work made the midnight call possible? What receiving culture caught the message and ran with it while the founder got the credit for the ride? And the harder version. What happens the night the rider does not make it?

If your client cannot name the people who make the rider replaceable, you are working with an organization that has quietly organized itself around its Paul Revere. That is what needs to be named. Not the rider. The dependence.

* see: Malcolm Gladwell (a Canadian, lol) called Revere a “Connector” in The Tipping Point and used the ride to argue that certain people move ideas because of who they know. Gladwell’s Connector is the poem in a new suit. Total myth again. The real leadership question is not who the rider is or why his network is special. It is why the organization ever needed to depend on him in the first place, and whether the receiving culture would have held if the British Regulars had captured him earlier in the night.

Which, for the record, they did. And we won.

CLUTCH: How Leaders Build Systems That Run Without Them is a book about that question. CREATE is the engine that builds the receiving culture. The 6C Decision Compass is the grid that decides who holds which call before the crisis arrives. DELEGATE is the architecture that turns the Prescott in your organization into the person who finishes the ride when the named hero does not make it. The Sons of Liberty did all three. The poem erased all three. Most organizations repeat the erasure every quarter.

Here is the move for you this week.

The next time a client walks you through a late-night save, do not ask what they did. Do not ask how they handled it. Ask who was already awake when the message came in. Ask who had been building the room that could receive it. Ask what those people were doing six months earlier that nobody credited at the time. And then ask the question the client will not want to hear.

Who are you treating as a Paul Revere? And what happens on the night the rider does not make it?

The ride is a poem. The receiving culture is an organization. The failure is telling yourself you have built the second when what you have built is the first.

THREE QUESTIONS FOR ADVISORS AND COACHES

When a client walks you through their latest late-night save, sit with these:

  1. Who in the organization is being treated as a Paul Revere right now? What would it cost the company if that person did not make it to Concord?

  2. If the rider left tomorrow, would the receiving culture still be there? Or does it live in one person’s head and phone?

  3. What question could you ask your client this week that would surface the infrastructure they have been taking credit for, and the dependence they have been mistaking for strength?

If those questions hit a nerve, you’re not alone. Most of the organizations I work with – founders, leadership teams, schools, nonprofits, government agencies – arrive at the same place: the rider gets remembered, the receiving culture goes invisible, and the next crisis exposes the gap.
