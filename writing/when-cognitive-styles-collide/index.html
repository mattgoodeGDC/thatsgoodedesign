When Cognitive Styles Collide

Essay 5: Neurodiversity Series

Matt Goode13 January 202615 min read Get new pieces by email

# Neuroinclusion

I was in the room when it happened.

A product team, mid-sprint planning. Two brilliant people – both essential to the work – locked in what looked like a personality conflict but was actually something deeper.

The product manager was pacing. “We need to ship this feature now. The market window is closing. We can iterate later.”

The lead engineer hadn’t moved. “We can’t ship it now. There are three edge cases we haven’t tested. If we push this live and it breaks, we’ll spend the next month in crisis mode instead of building the next thing.”

The PM: “You’re overthinking it. We don’t need perfection, we need momentum.”

The engineer: “I’m not overthinking. I’m thinking. There’s a difference.”

The room went quiet.

The manager – well-meaning, conflict-averse – tried to smooth it over. “Let’s all just be flexible here. Meet in the middle.”

But here’s what no one said out loud:

Flexibility isn’t neutral.

For the PM, “being flexible” meant slowing down – which for someone whose brain works through momentum often means losing the thread that makes focus possible in the first place.

For the engineer, “being flexible” meant compromising on quality thresholds – which for someone whose brain notices structural patterns feels like being asked to ignore flaws they can literally see in the system.

Neither was wrong.

They weren’t even in conflict about the goal. They both wanted the product to succeed.

They were in conflict about how different brains process risk, urgency, and quality – and no one had given them a language to navigate it.

That’s when I introduced the 6C Decision Compass.

Not as a conflict resolution tool.

As a friction navigation protocol.

Because what looked like a personality clash was actually decision friction in disguise.

The 6C Decision Compass: A Brief Primer

Before we map the collision patterns, let me explain what the 6C Decision Compass is and why it matters for neuroinclusive teams.

The 6C Decision Compass is a decision-making framework that maps six modes of authority and input:

  • Command – I decide

  • Consultative – I decide, but gather input

  • Collaborative – We decide together

  • Consensus – We agree broadly

  • Core Expertise – The expert decides

  • Choice – I set guardrails; you decide

Most leaders use one or two modes by default and call it “leadership style.” They’re Command leaders or Collaborative leaders, and they apply that mode regardless of context.

But neuroinclusive leadership requires toggling – matching decision modes to cognitive profiles, contexts, and the type of decision being made. Because the friction you’re seeing isn’t about personality. It’s about mismatched decision modes colliding with incompatible brain types.

If CREATE is the engine with six cylinders that need to fire in sequence, and VOICES is the manual gearbox that lets you shift between leadership modes, then 6C is the navigation system that tells you which gear to use for which terrain. It makes decision authority explicit so people aren’t constantly decoding invisible power structures.

When you can say “This is a Core Expertise call, you own it” or “This is Consultative, I’m deciding but I need your input first,” you eliminate the cognitive tax of figuring out who has permission to decide what. People can focus on the decision itself, not on navigating social uncertainty.

And for neurodivergent professionals especially, that clarity is the difference between contributing expertise and spending energy managing ambiguity.

Now let’s look at where friction happens most – and how the Compass helps you navigate it.

The Friction No One Wants to Name

Here’s the conversation most inclusion training won’t have:

Neurodivergent strengths don’t just complement neurotypical approaches.

Sometimes they collide.

And most leaders, when they see this friction, default to one of two responses:

“Everyone just be flexible.”

Or: “Let’s agree to disagree and move forward.”

Both sound reasonable. Both ignore the neurology.

Because flexibility is neurologically expensive for some brains and nearly effortless for others. Asking someone to “just be flexible” with structural ambiguity when their brain specializes in pattern recognition is like asking someone to run a marathon while doing mental math. Possible? Maybe. Efficient? Not even close.

Asking someone to “slow down and think it through” when momentum is the only thing keeping focus intact is like asking someone to paddle upstream while the current is pulling them forward. You can do it – but you’re fighting your own cognitive wiring to make it happen.

This isn’t about personality. It’s about how different brains process uncertainty, time pressure, and structural integrity.

And if you want neuroinclusive teams to actually work – not just coexist politely – you need systems that expect friction and build navigation protocols.

That’s where the 6C Decision Compass stops being a decision-making tool and starts being a cognitive translation system.

The Three Cognitive Collisions That Stall Teams

Let’s name the patterns. Because once you see them, you’ll recognize them everywhere.

Collision 1: Speed vs. Precision

One brain says: “We need to move NOW while the momentum is here.”

Another brain says: “We need to check these three variables first or we’ll have to redo it.”

This isn’t about one person being impulsive and the other being cautious. It’s about different cognitive relationships with time and risk.

For some brains, momentum is the resource. Interest creates focus. Delay kills both. The urgency isn’t manufactured – it’s the cognitive condition under which hyperfocus becomes possible. “Move now” isn’t recklessness. It’s the brain saying, “I can see the whole solution right now, and if we wait, I’ll lose the thread.”

For other brains, structural integrity is the priority. Precision isn’t perfectionism – it’s pattern recognition applied to risk. “Check these variables first” isn’t stalling. It’s the brain saying, “I can see three points of failure everyone else is missing, and if we ignore them, we’ll pay for it later.”

Or as that engineer put it in the meeting: “I’m not overthinking. I’m thinking. There’s a difference.”

Both are right. Both are seeing something real.

And if your decision-making system doesn’t have a protocol for navigating this, you get one of two outcomes:

Either the fast brain bulldozes the precise brain (and you ship something broken),

Or the precise brain blocks the fast brain (and you miss the window entirely).

6C Solution:

Mode: Consultative

Structure: The momentum-driven brain decides timing (they own momentum as a resource), but must gather the precision-oriented brain’s input on quality gates before executing.

Alternative: Core Expertise – whoever has domain authority on this specific decision owns it. If it’s a “move fast or lose the market” call, that’s strategy expertise. If it’s a “will this break the system?” call, that’s technical expertise.

The key is making decision authority explicit – so friction doesn’t become personal.

Collision 2: Verbal Processing vs. Written Processing

One brain says: “Let’s talk this through in real-time.”

Another brain says: “I need to think and write before I speak.”

This one shows up in every meeting, every brainstorm, every “quick sync.”

For some brains, thinking is talking. They process out loud. Ideas form through conversation. The back-and-forth isn’t just collaboration – it’s how their brain works. Silence feels like stalling.

For other brains, talking interrupts thinking. They need internal processing time before external articulation. Speaking in real-time doesn’t unlock their best ideas – it forces them to choose between appearing engaged and actually thinking.

And when your meeting culture defaults to “let’s riff on this,” you’ve just designed a system that captures fast-talker ideas and filters out deep-thinker ideas.

Not because the deep thinkers don’t have ideas. Because the format doesn’t give those ideas time to form.

6C Solution:

Mode: Choice

Structure: Set the outcome (we need everyone’s input on this decision), but let people choose their processing path. Some people contribute live. Some people contribute in writing afterward. Both count.

Alternative: Collaborative with asynchronous input – post a written brief 24 hours before the meeting. Let people process and contribute in a shared doc. Then use the live meeting to synthesize, debate, and decide – not to generate ideas from scratch.

The shift is subtle but critical: you’re not asking people to “be flexible about how they communicate.” You’re designing multiple pathways to the same outcome.

Collision 3: Structured Process vs. Emergent Discovery

One brain says: “We need a checklist and timeline.”

Another brain says: “I don’t know what I’m doing until I start doing it.”

This is the planner vs. explorer tension – and it plays out in every project kickoff, every execution plan, every “how are we going to do this?” conversation.

For structured brains, process creates safety. A checklist isn’t micromanagement – it’s cognitive scaffolding. It reduces decision fatigue. It makes the invisible visible. When the steps are clear, the brain can focus on execution, not navigation.

For emergent brains, rigid process feels like cognitive constraint. They don’t know the best path until they start moving. The process reveals itself through iteration. Forcing them to map everything up front doesn’t create clarity – it creates paralysis.

Both approaches are valid. Both solve real problems. And both need to coexist if you want teams that can both execute reliably and innovate adaptively.

6C Solution:

Mode: Consultative on milestones + Choice on daily execution

Structure: The structured brain gets predictability (”we need X by Y date”), the explorer brain gets autonomy (”you decide how you get there”).

Alternative: Separate what from how – the structured brain defines the deliverable and the quality standard, the emergent brain chooses the workflow.

This isn’t compromise. It’s complementarity by design.

You’re not asking people to meet in the middle. You’re building systems where different cognitive styles each get what they need to perform.

Mapping Decision Modes to Cognitive Styles

Here’s where the 6C Compass becomes strategically powerful – different cognitive styles thrive in different decision modes.

Autistic professionals often thrive in Core Expertise and Command environments – where the rules are clear, the logic is transparent, and the decision path is structurally sound. Ambiguity doesn’t feel like freedom; it feels like cognitive noise.

ADHD brains often excel when given Choice – autonomy over how, when, and in what order they tackle work. The structure isn’t the timeline; it’s the outcome. That autonomy activates hyperfocus.

Dyslexic thinkers often need Consultative or Collaborative modes – the chance to talk through strategy, synthesize inputs, and build the idea verbally before committing to a path. Their brilliance shows up in real-time synthesis, not pre-formed written proposals.

Anxiety-driven cognition performs best in Consultative mode – where their threat modeling and scenario planning are explicitly gathered as input rather than treated as pessimism. They need to know their risk assessment will inform the decision.

Most leaders default to 1-2 modes for everything. Neuroinclusive leaders use all six – and know which cognitive profiles need which modes to perform their best.

What Breaks When You Don’t Use 6C

Let’s name the anti-patterns – because seeing what breaks helps you understand what the Compass actually fixes.

Here’s what happens without explicit decision modes:

  • Meetings run 2x longer because no one knows who’s deciding

  • Neurodivergent professionals assume they need permission for everything (unclear when Core Expertise applies)

  • Decisions get re-litigated because authority was never clear (was it Command or Collaborative?)

  • Innovation slows because people won’t move without explicit authorization

  • The fastest talker wins, not the best thinker

  • Pattern-detectors stop speaking up because their input arrives “too late”

  • Structure-oriented brains get labeled “inflexible” when they’re actually preventing disasters

This isn’t a neurodiversity problem. It’s a leadership clarity problem that neurodivergent professionals expose.

Quick audit: In your last five decisions:

  • Could your team name which mode you were using?

  • Did you explicitly say “This is Command, I’m deciding” or “This is Collaborative, we’re deciding together”?

  • Were people clear on when their input was shaping the decision vs. informing it?

If you answered no to these, you’re creating decision anxiety – and your neurodivergent team members are bearing the highest cost.

Why the 6C Compass Unlocks Neuroinclusive Leadership

Here’s what makes the 6C Decision Compass essential for mixed-brain teams:

It does three things that most decision-making systems don’t.

1. It Eliminates the Cognitive Decoding Tax

For many neurodivergent professionals, ambiguity around “Am I allowed to decide this?” creates constant low-grade anxiety. Every decision becomes a social decoding exercise: Do I have permission? Will I be seen as overstepping? Should I wait for approval?

When you name the decision mode – “This is Core Expertise, you own it” or “This is Consultative, I’m deciding but I need your input first” – you eliminate that cognitive tax.

People can focus on the decision itself, not on navigating invisible authority structures.

2. It Designs for Cognitive Complementarity

Different brains thrive in different modes.

Pattern-recognition brains often excel in Core Expertise and Command – where the logic is clear, the rules are explicit, and the decision path is structurally sound.

Momentum-driven brains often thrive in Choice – where autonomy activates hyperfocus and movement creates clarity.

Big-picture thinking brains often need Consultative – where they can talk through strategy, synthesize inputs, and build the idea verbally before committing to a path.

When you treat “good decision-making” as one mode applied uniformly, you’re designing for cognitive conformity.

When you use the 6C Compass, you’re designing for cognitive complementarity.

3. It Scales Neuroinclusive Systems

When anyone on the team can name the decision mode, you’re no longer the bottleneck.

“This feels like a Collaborative decision – we all need to weigh in.”

“Actually, I think this is Core Expertise – whoever knows the client best should call it.”

“Can we make this a Choice decision? Set the outcome, let people choose the path?”

When the system holds the decision logic, not just the leader, you’ve built infrastructure that scales.

And scaling neuroinclusive leadership means moving from “the leader navigates every friction point” to “the team has a protocol for navigating friction without you.”

6C + VOICES: Matching Decision Modes to Leadership Gears

Here’s where the integration gets powerful.

Different VOICES gears naturally pair with different 6C decision modes.

When you’re in Coach mode, you’re usually using Consultative or Collaborative – gathering input, co-creating solutions, building ownership through inclusion.

When you’re in Sculptor mode, you’re often setting Choice with clear guardrails – defining the outcome and constraints, then letting people find their own path within that structure.

When you’re in Operator mode, you might need Command for execution speed – clear directive, tight timeline, minimal deliberation.

When you’re in Encourager mode, Consensus might be appropriate – building broad agreement and collective momentum.

The mistake most leaders make is staying in one gear and forcing one decision mode regardless of context.

But neuroinclusive leadership requires toggling – shifting between gears and decision modes based on what the situation and the cognitive profiles in the room actually need.

That’s not indecisiveness. That’s adaptive precision.

6C + CREATE Relationships: When “Who Decides” Is Never Ambiguous

Let’s connect this back to CREATE – because the Relationships cylinder fires cleanly when decision authority is structurally explicit.

When “who decides” is ambiguous, relationships become navigation tools instead of collaboration tools. People spend energy figuring out informal power dynamics instead of contributing their expertise.

But when you use the 6C Compass to make decision modes explicit, the Relationships cylinder can do what it’s designed to do: build trust, clarity, and collaborative capacity.

“Is this my call?” is no longer a question people have to reverse-engineer through social cues.

It’s answered by the system.

And that clarity – that cognitive relief – is what lets neurodivergent professionals focus on their actual work instead of decoding invisible protocols.

The 6C Translation Guide for Neurodivergent Teams

Here’s how to name decision modes in ways that reduce anxiety and activate clarity:

Command: “I’m deciding this, here’s why, here’s the timeline.” → Reduces ambiguity. Provides structure. Works well when urgency is high and deliberation time is low.

Consultative: “I’m deciding, but I need your input first. Here’s the question I’m working through.” → Honors expertise without creating decision paralysis. Good for brains that think out loud.

Collaborative: “We’re deciding together. Everyone’s input shapes the outcome.” → Use with async options (written pre-work, post-meeting input) to include introverted and slow-processing brains.

Consensus: “We need broad agreement before moving forward. Let’s work toward alignment everyone can support.” → Useful when buy-in matters as much as the decision itself. Requires extra processing time for neurodivergent team members.

Core Expertise: “You own this decision because you have the domain knowledge. I trust your judgment.” → Reduces anxiety about overstepping. Activates competence-based confidence.

Choice: “Here’s the outcome and the constraints. You decide how to get there.” → Activates autonomy-driven focus. Reduces micromanagement friction. Honors different pathways to results.

There’s no such thing as frictionless collaboration. Just teams that have protocols for navigating friction – and teams that don’t.

The question isn’t whether cognitive styles will collide. It’s whether you’ve built systems that turn collision into complementarity.

Because when you do, you don’t get compromise.

You get the precision and the momentum.

The structure and the emergence.

The deep thinking and the fast execution.

You get teams that can hold multiple cognitive truths at once – and make better decisions because of it.

That’s not compromise. That’s the competitive advantage everyone’s looking for but most organizations are systematically filtering out.

Decision Friction Self-Assessment

Rate your agreement with each statement on a scale of 1-5: (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree)

1. When I make decisions, I explicitly name which mode I’m using (Core Expertise, Command, Consultative, etc.) so the team knows how authority is structured.

  • 1 = Never / 2 = Rarely / 3 = Sometimes / 4 = Usually / 5 = Always

2. My team has clear protocols for when to use real-time discussion vs. asynchronous input for decision-making.

  • 1 = No protocols / 2 = Vague protocols / 3 = Some protocols / 4 = Clear protocols / 5 = Well-established protocols

3. When cognitive friction emerges (speed vs. precision, verbal vs. written, structure vs. emergence), we have systems to navigate it rather than just asking people to “be flexible.”

  • 1 = No systems / 2 = Minimal systems / 3 = Some systems / 4 = Good systems / 5 = Robust systems

4. I can identify which decision modes different team members thrive in based on their cognitive profiles.

  • 1 = Cannot identify / 2 = Vague sense / 3 = Some clarity / 4 = Clear identification / 5 = Systematically mapped

5. People on my team can focus on contributing expertise rather than spending energy decoding who has permission to decide what.

  • 1 = High decoding tax / 2 = Significant tax / 3 = Moderate tax / 4 = Low tax / 5 = Minimal tax

Scoring Guide:

20-25 points: Your decision-making likely supports cognitive diversity well. Consider: Can you document your 6C decision protocols? Which specific practices prevent decision friction?

15-19 points: You’re creating some clarity but decision friction likely still emerges. Consider: Which collision pattern (speed vs. precision, verbal vs. written, structure vs. emergence) shows up most often in your team?

10-14 points: Decision ambiguity may be creating cognitive overhead. Consider: Try naming the decision mode explicitly in your next three decisions. Notice what changes in clarity and contribution quality.

5-9 points: Decision friction is likely a major source of cognitive tax. Consider: Your team is probably spending significant energy on “Who gets to decide?” rather than “What’s the best decision?”

Next Steps Based on Your Score:

If you scored 15+:

  • Document your decision-making protocols

  • Train your team to name decision modes themselves

  • Identify which modes you underutilize (most leaders overuse 1-2 modes)

If you scored 10-14:

  • Pick your next important decision: Name the mode before the discussion starts

  • Map your last 3 cognitive collisions to the patterns above – which one repeats?

  • Design one 6C protocol for your most common friction pattern

If you scored below 10:

  • Start simple: In your next team meeting, say “This is Consultative – I’m deciding but I need your input”

  • Notice: Does naming the mode reduce the “who’s deciding?” anxiety?

  • Identify one chronic decision friction point and map it to a 6C solution above
