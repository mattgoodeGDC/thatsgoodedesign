What Attila the Hun Knew That Your CEO Doesn’t

I found the answer on my dad’s bookshelf.

Matt Goode19 February 20267 min read Get new pieces by email

# CREATE

There’s a worn little hardback that lived on my father’s bookshelf for as long as I can remember. It wasn’t much to look at: eighty-six pages, a dramatic cover, a title that sounded like it belonged in a history class rather than a management seminar. Leadership Secrets of Attila the Hun by Wess Roberts, a former Army major turned insurance executive, whose slim volume would go on to become a New York Times bestseller translated into twenty-four languages.

I’m fairly certain it came home from the War College. My dad was career military, retired as an O-6 from the USAF, and this book had that unmistakable feel of something that got passed around among officers like contraband wisdom. Too fun to be doctrine. Too useful to ignore. I’ve since heard it referenced in military circles more times than I can count. Colonels and Command Sergeants Major will mention it with a knowing grin, the way people talk about a shared secret. It appeared in the Naval War College Review. Special operations veterans have called it a career-long companion. For a slim little book, it punched well above its weight class.

I picked it up as a teenager, probably out of curiosity more than ambition. I didn’t know yet that I’d spend the next few decades obsessing over how leaders are built, broken, and rebuilt. I just thought the idea of learning leadership from a fifth-century warlord was kind of wild.

It was. I remember the first time I read it distinctly. And it changed the trajectory of how I think about what leaders actually do.

What Attila Understood That Most Modern Leaders Don’t

Roberts did something clever. He took Attila, a man history remembers as “the Scourge of God,” and reframed him not as a barbarian but as a strategist. A unifier. A man who took a loose confederation of nomadic, multilingual, multiethnic tribes and forged them into the most dominant force the ancient world had seen.

The book is structured around what Roberts called “Attilaisms,” pithy, imagined speeches from Attila to his chieftains on topics like loyalty, decision-making, morale, discipline, negotiation, and the graceful handling of defeat. They read like the kind of wisdom you’d hear from a mentor who has no patience for pretense and no interest in impressing you. Just in making you better.

Here’s what stayed with me.

Pick Your Battles. No, Actually Pick Them.

One of the first lessons the book drills home is that great leaders are ruthlessly selective about where they spend their energy. Attila didn’t attack everything that moved. He was calculating, patient, and strategic about which conflicts to engage and which to let pass.

I think about this constantly in my coaching work. The leaders I see burning out fastest aren’t the ones facing the biggest challenges. They’re the ones who refuse to triage. Every email is urgent. Every disagreement is a hill to die on. Every initiative gets equal weight. Attila would have ridden right past half of it. Not because he was lazy, but because he understood that saying yes to everything is the same as saying yes to nothing.

Your Team Isn’t Watching Your PowerPoints

Roberts makes the point that Attila held himself to the same standards he expected of his warriors, and often to higher ones. His discipline wasn’t performative. It was the foundation of his credibility.

This is the principle I’ve come to call “modeling” in my own work, and it sits right at the heart of what makes or breaks a leader’s culture. You don’t get to talk about accountability while dodging your own feedback. You don’t get to preach vulnerability while armoring up in every meeting. Your people aren’t watching your PowerPoints. They’re watching your behavior. Attila knew that fifteen hundred years ago. We’re still learning it.

He Turned Captivity Into a Classroom

What fascinated me most, rereading Leadership Secrets of Attila the Hun as an adult, is that Roberts wasn’t describing one kind of leader. He was describing a leader who could move fluidly between roles. I think this was the seed for my VOICES framework – the idea that effective leaders don’t have one “gear.” They shift. Attila was a visionary who unified the tribes around a shared future, an operator who managed the logistics of campaigns spanning continents, a coach who developed chieftains and gave them real authority, and a sculptor who reshaped tribal structures to meet evolving threats. He spent part of his youth as a political hostage in a Roman court, and rather than wasting that time in resentment, he absorbed everything he could about how his adversaries thought, organized, and fought. He turned captivity into a classroom.

This is the quality I’ve come to think of as a leadership gearbox: the ability to shift between modes of presence without losing yourself in the process. The best leaders I’ve worked with, in schools, in government, in startups, share this quality. They’re insatiably curious. They treat every environment, even hostile ones, as a source of intelligence. And they know instinctively when the moment calls for vision, when it calls for precision, and when it calls for simply getting out of the way.

Attila understood tribal dynamics in ways that would make any modern org designer envious. He didn’t hoard talent at the center. He empowered his chieftains, gave them real authority, and held them accountable for results, not for process. He knew that a leader who needs to be the smartest person in every room has already lost. This one hit differently when I started coaching founders. So many early-stage leaders struggle with the transition from “I do everything” to “I build the team that does everything.” Attila made that transition with an entire civilization. The principle scales.

Morale Is Not a Perk. It’s Infrastructure.

Roberts dedicates serious attention to morale and discipline, not as soft skills, but as the structural underpinning of any effective organization. Attila treated the spirit of his warriors the way an engineer treats load-bearing walls. You don’t get to neglect it and then wonder why the building collapses.

I’ve seen this play out in every organization I’ve worked in. The schools where morale was treated as someone else’s problem were the schools losing their best teachers. The agencies where “resilience” was code for “just deal with it” were hemorrhaging institutional knowledge. Morale isn’t about pizza parties. It’s about whether people believe that their leader sees them, values them, and will fight for them.

Attila’s warriors followed him into impossible campaigns against the Roman Empire, not because they were afraid of him, but because they believed in what he was building. That’s morale as infrastructure. That’s the real secret.

The Chapter Nobody Writes

The chapter that surprised me most was the one on leaving well. Roberts writes about the importance of how a leader exits: stepping down, transitioning power, and handling defeat with dignity. It’s the leadership topic almost nobody talks about, and it might be the most important one.

I think about this chapter more than any other these days. When I left McDonogh School after seventeen years of teaching, I found myself reaching for Tennyson’s Ulysses to say what I couldn’t say in my own words – that the people I’d worked with were “souls that have toil’d, and wrought, and thought with me,” and that leaving them was one of the hardest things I’d done. But Tennyson also reminded me that “all experience is an arch wherethro’ gleams that untravelled world,” and that staying safe in a role you’ve outgrown is its own kind of failure.

The way you leave something tells the world everything about how you led while you were there. Do you burn bridges or build them on your way out? Do you hoard knowledge or transfer it? Do you make the transition about your ego or about the mission?

Attila, for all his ferocity, understood that a leader’s legacy isn’t written in the battles they win. It’s written in what survives after they’re gone. The act of releasing, of transitioning smoothly, might be the most underrated skill in all of leadership.

Eighty-Six Pages and Fifteen Hundred Years

The principles haven’t changed in fifteen hundred years. Be disciplined. Be curious. Be selective. Model the behavior you expect. Invest in your people. Handle defeat with grace. And when it’s time to go, go well.

My dad probably didn’t know that the little book on his shelf would become the first link in a chain that led me to a career built on helping people lead better. But that’s how these things work, isn’t it? The most important lessons don’t announce themselves. They just sit there on a bookshelf, waiting for someone to pick them up.

Thanks, Dad. And thanks, Attila.

Three Questions Attila Would Ask Your Organization

Before your next leadership meeting, sit with these:

  1. Where are you spending energy on battles that don’t matter, and what would it cost you to ride past them?

  2. If your team only watched your behavior and ignored every word you said, what would they learn about your actual values?

  3. If you left tomorrow, what would survive without you, and what would collapse?

If those questions hit a nerve, you’re not alone. Most of the organizations I work with – founders, leadership teams, schools, nonprofits, government agencies – arrive at the same place: the culture feels supportive, but performance has quietly stagnated. The work involves rebuilding the connection between safety and standards so your team gets both the trust and the challenge they actually need to grow.
