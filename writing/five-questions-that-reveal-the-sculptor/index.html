Are You Redesigning – Or Just Coping Faster?

Part 3 of 3: The Sculptor Series

Matt Goode6 February 202610 min read Get new pieces by email

# Winning feels like a near miss

Here’s the trap: Sculptor thinking feels like Operator thinking if you’re not paying attention.

You can spend an hour “working on the business” and actually just be working in it. You can talk about systems while still managing around them. You can attend the strategy offsite, nod along to the consultant’s framework, agree that you need to “think more architecturally” – and then go right back to solving the same problems you were solving before.

The gap between Sculptor and Operator isn’t intellectual. Most leaders understand the difference. They’ve read the books. They know “working on the system” matters. They can explain it clearly.

The gap is behavioral. It’s the distance between knowing and doing. Between understanding that you should redesign the system and actually redesigning it. Between agreeing that you need to stop being the bottleneck and actually building the architecture that removes you from the center.

So how do you know which side of the gap you’re on?

Over the past two days, I’ve made the case for Sculptor – the leadership gear that removes rather than adds, that redesigns systems rather than optimizing within them. Today, I want to offer something practical: five questions that reveal where you actually are. Not where you think you are. Not where you’d like to be. Where you actually are.

These questions are uncomfortable. That’s the point.

Question One: What Problems From Six Months Ago Are You Still Solving Today?

Not variations. Not evolutions. The same fundamental problem.

Pull up your calendar from six months ago. Think about what consumed your attention. The conflicts. The bottlenecks. The quality issues. The coordination failures.

Now look at today. How many of those problems are still here? Not adjacent problems. Not new versions of old problems. The same underlying dysfunction is still generating symptoms that land on your desk.

The conflict between product and engineering that you mediate every other week – were you mediating it six months ago? The decisions that escalate to you because no one else is sure they have authority – were they escalating six months ago? The quality gaps that require your intervention – were they requiring your intervention six months ago?

If you’re honest, the answer is probably yes. For most leaders, it’s yes to more than they’d like to admit.

Operators solve problems. They get good at it. Efficient. Skilled. They can handle recurring issues faster than before, with less energy, and with better outcomes. That’s real progress, as far as it goes.

Sculptors ask a different question: Why does this problem keep recurring?

If you’re solving the same problems you were solving six months ago, you’re not sculpting. You’re coping. You’ve become efficient at managing dysfunction rather than eliminating it.

I don’t say this to criticize. Coping is a survival skill. When you’re overwhelmed – and most leaders are – managing recurring problems is often the only option that feels available. But coping has a ceiling. You can get faster at it, but you can’t escape it. The problem remains. And you remain attached to it.

What’s one recurring problem you could stop solving by redesigning the system that produces it?

Question Two: What Would Break If You Disappeared for Two Weeks?

Not what would be delayed. Not what would pile up. What would actually break?

The client relationship that only you can manage. The decision that only you can make. The conflict that only you can mediate. The context that lives in your head and nowhere else.

If you disappeared tomorrow – vacation, illness, sabbatical, whatever – what would genuinely fall apart?

This question isn’t about vacation planning. It’s a diagnostic for whether you’ve built a system or whether you are the system.

Amy Edmondson’s research on psychological safety illuminates why this matters. She found that teams perform best when people feel safe making decisions, surfacing problems, and taking initiative without waiting for permission. But that safety doesn’t happen by accident. It’s architectural. It comes from explicit clarity about who owns what, what decisions can be made locally, and what guardrails are in place to catch mistakes.

Most organizations have the opposite. Authority is implicit. Decision rights are unclear. People learn – through experience and by observing what happens when others act without permission – that the safe path is to escalate. To wait. To check with the leader before moving.

That’s not timidity. That’s rationality. The system trained them.

If you can’t disappear for two weeks without things breaking, you haven’t sculpted a system. You’ve become the load-bearing wall. And load-bearing walls can’t move.

What would need to be true for your team to function fully without you for two weeks?

Question Three: When Did You Last Kill Something?

Not deprioritized. Not paused. Not “put on the back burner until Q3.” Killed. Ended. Removed completely.

A meeting that no longer serves its purpose. A report no one reads, but everyone produces. A process that exists because it’s always existed, not because it’s still valuable. An initiative that sounded promising but never gained traction. A commitment you made that isn’t delivering what you thought it would.

When did you last end something like that? Actually end it, not just let it fade?

Sculptors carve. That means removal is a regular practice – not a crisis response, not an annual cleanup, but an ongoing discipline. They look at what exists and ask: Is this still earning its place?

Most leaders are far better at adding than removing. The incentives point that way. You can announce a new initiative. You can celebrate a new hire. You can point to a new process as evidence of progress. Removal is invisible. Nobody sends a company-wide email celebrating the meeting you eliminated or the project you shut down.

But addition without removal is accumulation. And accumulation creates drag.

The organization slows down not because the work got harder, but because there’s more friction. More meetings. More approvals. More coordination overhead. More legacy processes exist because no one ever stopped them, not because they’re still valuable.

If you haven’t killed something in the past month – really killed it, not just deprioritized it – you’re not sculpting. You’re accumulating. And accumulation is the enemy of transformation.

What’s one thing you’re tolerating that you could eliminate entirely?

Question Four: Can Your Team Describe How Decisions Get Made – Or Do They Just Know to Ask You?

This question reveals whether your organization has decision architecture or just decision defaults.

In many organizations, decision-making is implicit. There’s no map. There’s no clarity about who owns what, where input is required versus optional, what escalation looks like versus what local resolution looks like. Decisions happen – but they happen through habit, hierarchy, and guesswork.

When decision-making is implicit, it defaults upward. Always.

This is true regardless of what you’ve said. You can tell people to “take ownership” all day long. You can express frustration that decisions keep landing on your desk. You can genuinely want people to act with more autonomy.

But if decision rights aren’t explicit – if people don’t know, concretely, what they can decide without asking – they’ll escalate. Not because they’re incapable. Because escalation is safer than guessing wrong. The system has implicitly taught them that.

The test isn’t what you’ve told people. The test is whether they can articulate it.

Ask three people on your team: How do decisions get made around here? Not in theory. In practice. For a specific type of decision – a resource allocation, a client issue, a product trade-off – who decides? Where does input come from? What happens when there’s disagreement?

If they give you a clear, consistent answer – “I own X, you own Y, we collaborate on Z, here’s what escalation looks like” – you’ve done the Sculptor work. The architecture exists.

If they hesitate. If they give vague answers. If three people give three different answers. If they ultimately shrug and say, “I guess I ask you,” – the architecture doesn’t exist. You don’t have a decision system. You have a decision default. And the default is you.

What decisions that currently route to you could you explicitly delegate – with clear guardrails – this week?

Question Five: What Are You Tolerating That You’ve Stopped Seeing?

This is the hardest question because it asks you to notice what you’ve normalized.

Every organization has invisible tolerances. The friction everyone works around. The workaround became standard practice. The dysfunction frustrated new hires for three months until they got used to it. The thing that would shock an outsider but barely registers for you anymore because you’ve been swimming in it so long.

These tolerances accumulate. They become the water. And because they’re familiar, they stop feeling like choices. They feel like facts. That’s just how it is here.

But they’re not facts. They’re design choices – either ones you made explicitly, or ones that emerged by default and were never revisited. And as long as they stay invisible, they can’t be changed.

The Sculptor discipline is to keep seeing what others have stopped seeing. To maintain the beginner’s mind – the capacity to notice friction that everyone else has normalized.

There’s a simple way to access this: ask someone who hasn’t normalized it yet.

Ask someone new to your team: What seems harder here than it should be? Ask a trusted peer from outside: What stands out when you look at how we work? Ask yourself – honestly – What would I never design this way if I were starting from scratch?

The answers to those questions are where the angels are hiding. The organization that could exist – more adaptive, more capable, more ready for transformation – if you could just see what you’ve stopped seeing.

What friction have you normalized that a newcomer would find unacceptable?

The Compound Choice

Every week, you make a choice. Usually not consciously.

You can spend your time solving problems – or removing the systems that create them.

You can push harder on execution – or redesign what’s blocking it.

You can add more to your plate – or set something free.

Operators always choose the first path. It feels productive. It looks like leadership. The feedback loop is immediate: problem, solution, satisfaction.

But there’s a cost. The problems keep regenerating. The calendar stays packed. The exhaustion accumulates. And five years later, you’re still solving the same problems you were solving five years ago – just faster.

Sculptors choose the second path often enough that it compounds.

The fires stop starting. The decisions flow to the right level. The capacity appears. Excellence becomes structurally possible instead of heroically demanded. The organization learns to run without your constant intervention – not because you’ve abandoned it, but because you’ve built something that doesn’t need you at the center of everything.

That’s not working less. The best Sculptors I know work with great intensity. But they work on different things. Less time solving, more time redesigning. Less time managing symptoms, more time addressing root causes. Less time in the system, more time on the system.

That shift creates space. And space is what transformation requires.

Back to the Wave

This series started with a question: If 56% of companies are failing at AI transformation despite massive investment, and the 12% succeeding aren’t bigger or better-funded – just better-built – how do you actually build better?

The answer isn’t a framework, though frameworks help. The answer is a shift in orientation.

From adding to removing.

From solving to eliminating.

From Operator to Sculptor.

The organizations riding the wave aren’t the ones pushing hardest. They’re the ones that cleared the path. They saw what was blocking transformation – the implicit decision rights that created bottlenecks, the cultural signals that made experimentation unsafe, the accumulated processes that nobody had questioned in years – and they carved it away.

The angel was already in the marble. They just had to see it.

Where to Go From Here

The frameworks in this series – VOICES, CREATE, 6C – form the core of my forthcoming book, CLUTCH: How Leaders Build Systems That Run Without Them.

But the book won’t give you transformation. Only you can do that. The book gives you tools – lenses to see what you’ve stopped seeing, questions that reveal architectural gaps, frameworks to design systems that run without heroics.

The real work is yours.

Pick one recurring problem and ask what system produces it. Map decision rights and push authority to where context lives. Kill something that no longer earns its place. Build the capacity to disappear for two weeks without everything breaking.

One Sculptor move. Then another. Then another.

The wave won’t wait. But now you know what to carve.

  • When did you last kill something?
