Skill Is the Wrong Word

Matt Goode26 June 202611 min read Get new pieces by email

# Coaching

The best public defense of the humanities I’ve read gets one word wrong. That word decides everything that follows.

To read the piece that started my mind whirling on this post, click here.

For a week this spring, I argued that the capacities good leadership runs on aren’t skills. They are a formation: built into how a person sees, not installed by a workshop or certified by a competency model. I made that case through Plato, Sophocles, and Homer, basically the argument that the humanities are more important than ever.

Then two living philosophers picked up the same fight in public, in the Chronicle of Higher Education and the New York Times, and I want to carry it somewhere neither of them has ventured: Into the room where you run an organization.

Start in a studio at the New York Times this spring, where the columnist Ross Douthat sat across from Jennifer Frey, a philosopher at the University of Tulsa who built its honors college and then watched the university’s new administration cut its funding by more than ninety percent. Douthat played the skeptic, the parent who wants his kids employable and can’t see why they’d spend tuition on Plato. Is Shakespeare better than John Grisham? he asked. Frey said yes, and defended it well: Shakespeare’s language, the way great art calls you toward truth and beauty, the hierarchy of goods you can’t pretend away the moment you say “higher education.” Aristotle, she reminded him, thought the purpose of education was leisure, schole, the root of the word “school,” the space set aside to cultivate the highest parts of a person.

To read that piece, click here.

It’s a serious case made by a serious person. And it has one fatal property. It only works on people who already believe it.

That isn’t my objection. It’s the one N. Ángel Pinillos made a few weeks later, in the Chronicle of Higher Education, in the best public defense of the humanities I’ve read. A piece that opens by throwing Frey’s entire argument overboard. Pinillos chairs the philosophy department at Arizona State University, one of the largest in the country, and he has watched the enrollment charts go gray for twenty years. So he did the thing almost no defender of the humanities is willing to do. He gave up the argument his own side keeps reaching for. The intrinsic-value case, that Shakespeare is higher, that great art calls you to the transcendentals, that self-cultivation is its own reward – he calls impotent. Not wrong. Impotent. If you already recognize the value, the argument is unnecessary. If you don’t, you’re being told that your failure to see it is itself the proof of your deficiency. There’s no third path. The argument can’t reach across the divide it was built to cross.

Pinillos and I agree on nearly everything. Well, almost. The intrinsic case is a dead end. The humanities build something real and identifiable. AI makes that capacity more valuable, not less. Where we split is on a single word, on what to call the “thing” the humanities... build.

He calls it a skill.

I completely disagree. In fact, that week of essays made exactly that point.

“Skill” is the whole disagreement, and it isn’t semantics. It’s the difference between two completely different theories. If you’ve ever sat through a leadership offsite and wondered, six months later, why nothing changed, the answer lives in this one word.

Here’s why he reaches for it. Skill is legible. A skeptical parent understands a skill. A legislator can fund a skill. A donor can be shown the return on a skill. Pinillos picks the word on purpose because he’s trying to win an argument in daylight with people who don’t share his assumptions, and “skill” is the currency those people accept. I understand the move. It’s a good-faith translation of something precious into terms the market will price.

But the translation is the trap.

The moment you call what the humanities teach ... skills, you’ve told every organization on earth exactly how to mishandle them. A skill is something you acquire. It comes from a workshop, a certification, a two-day offsite, and a feedback form that asks whether the session met your expectations. Skills are installed. They have a vendor and a completion date. The capacities I ascribe to what the humanities teach: reading what isn’t said, holding a decision open when the information is incomplete, authoring coherence before it exists, can’t be installed in anyone. These get built over years, or they get done poorly by people who took a weekend course and posted the certification on LinkedIn.

You don’t inspect a skill. You demonstrate a skill. A skill lives inside a person and shows up on command – in the recital, the exam, the thirty-minute interview, where someone performs competence for an evaluator who already holds the answer key.

Infrastructure is different. By infrastructure, I mean what carries the weight when no one is consciously carrying it. You can’t see whether it was built well by watching it. You find out under load – when the situation has no precedent, the weight comes down, and the thing either holds or it doesn’t. There is no answer key.

The inspector in the basement isn’t checking a skill. He’s checking a structure – whether it was built right, whether it holds.

Listen to the inspector, and you’ll notice the words do double work. Built. Holds. They’re the same words we reach for when we describe a person who came through something hard and stayed intact. Two built things, described in the same language. But they’re built in different places, and only one of them stays.

What gets built into a person is formation – the humanities’ work. Judgment, character, what a classroom lays down inside someone over the years. It’s real, and it’s load-bearing, but it isn’t transferable. It walks out the door when the person does.

What gets built around people, by the organization, is infrastructure. Roles, decision rights, and the handoffs that hold whether or not any one person is having a strong day. It’s impersonal by design – that’s the point of it. It survives the person.

But survival isn’t the whole of it. The same infrastructure that outlasts a person is also the last thing left that can still form one – the organization doing the schools’ old job, under load this time. It survives the person, and it forms the person. Both. Hold that; it’s where these three essays end up.

Formation gets mislabeled as skill because both live inside the person. So when something fails, everyone looks inside the person – first for the missing skill, then the missing character. They almost never look at what was never built around the person.

Neither one is a skill. A skill shows on command; these only show under load. And the line between them isn’t just inside versus around – it’s what leaves when the person leaves.

This isn’t a semantic quarrel. It’s the reason your leadership development budget is shaped the way it is.

The word you use for a capacity decides which line it goes on. Skills get a training budget – a course, a vendor, a sign-in sheet, a number for how many people attended. Infrastructure gets a capital plan – designed, built, maintained, inspected, never quite finished. Those aren’t the same line, not the same money, and they don’t produce the same thing. Call narrative intelligence a skill, and you send three managers to a storytelling workshop and measure whether they enjoyed it.

Call it infrastructure, and you ask the two questions a workshop was never built to answer. First: what in our system makes coherent meaning possible, or impossible? Second: when that capacity degrades – and it always degrades – whose job is it to notice and rebuild?

The first is a design question. The second is a maintenance question, and it’s the one no one is assigned: which is why Sophocles can still show you, exactly, how it fails. In Oedipus, the prophet Tiresias brings the king the truth, is accused of conspiracy for it, and is sent away. After that one public punishment, the rest of the court runs the same quiet calculation and goes silent. The information existed. The structure couldn’t carry it. That’s a culture problem with a mechanical cause, and the fix is structural: a receiving culture, where the person who names the problem early isn’t the person who pays for it. Not a morale program. In CLUTCH, that’s Culture, Relationships, and Ethics doing load-bearing work – and it’s the first thing to go when you treat formation as a weekend-course.

One word produces an event. The other produces a structure. That’s the whole difference, and it’s the thing Pinillos got wrong while getting everything else right.

I want to say the next part carefully, because I admire the argument and the man making it.

Pinillos and I are writing for different rooms, and you can tell where his is by where the piece ran. The Chronicle of Higher Education is the trade paper of the academy. His case is built exactly right for that room: a real, identifiable competence, an invaluable skill with material consequences, proven by grades and test scores, and where philosophy majors land on the LSAT. To the parents and legislators and donors who decide whether the humanities survive the next budget, that’s the argument that wins, and I’m glad he’s making it.

Here’s the gentle irony. His critique of Frey was that her argument only persuades the already-persuaded. He’s right. But his own argument has the same shape, one room over. Outside higher ed, in the room you and I work in, full of founders and operators and the people who run things, “it raises your GRE verbal” is not the case that lands. You don’t need your next VP to test well. You need to know what that person does when the test is gone, when the information is incomplete, the stakes are real, and there’s no answer key. Under load. Pinillos diagnosed the boundary of Frey’s argument perfectly. He just couldn’t see the boundary of his own.

So I’ll take his argument and finish it, because it deserves finishing. He’s right that the humanities build something verifiable and material. He’s right that the intrinsic-value case can’t carry the weight, and right that AI made the whole question urgent in a way it wasn’t a decade ago. He’s one word short – and it’s the wrong word, in a way that costs you.

What the humanities build into a person is formation, and formation can’t be acquired in an afternoon, performed on command, or bought by the seat. That’s exactly why his own evidence kept betraying the word he chose. But formation isn’t where this ends. Most of the people in your organization never got twenty years of difficult texts, and you can’t send them back to school for it. What you can build is the infrastructure around them – a leadership operating system that makes coherence, dissent, and judgment structurally possible, and that, given enough time and real stakes, forms some of the judgment the schools never did. That part is still yours to design. The next two essays are about how.

If you missed that week, here’s the shape of it.

I opened with “The Humanities Were Never Soft”: Rolling Stone had named five of these capacities and called them skills too, narrative intelligence, moral imagination, tolerance for ambiguity, deep listening, systems thinking, and I argued each one was a formation instead.

“The Philosopher-King Problem,” on Plato’s Republic and the reason organizations keep promoting the people least fit to lead.

“What Greek Tragedy Knew,” on Sophocles and the three ways a good leader fails inside a system that can’t develop him, deliver truth to him, or let him receive it.

“The Oldest Management Failure on Record,” on Homer’s Iliad and the day a commander’s authority and a fighter’s judgment stopped being the same thing.

“What Are You Practicing?” on Marcus Aurelius’s Meditations and the one architecture no organization can build for you, the interior kind.

“They Educated It Out,” on the engine underneath all five, creativity, the capacity to generate the frame before you optimize inside it.

Three thousand years of material, all of it describing a Tuesday in your company. I’ve hyperlinked them for your perusal later if you’d like.

His case rested on grades – the most direct evidence imaginable, he said, generated millions of times a year. But a grade certifies. It doesn’t transfer, and it proves nothing about a situation no one saw coming. By that standard, his argument had only ever been graded: an A in the one room it was written for. So I transferred it – out of the Chronicle and into yours, where the answer key is gone, and the weight is real. It held. But only once I swapped the word it was resting on. That is the test a grade can’t run, and his argument passed it. The demonstration was never the test; the load was – the moment the playbook runs out and the thing either holds, or it doesn’t. A claim that works only in the room it was written for is a skill. A claim that holds when you carry it into a room it was never built for is infrastructure.

There’s a piece of this Pinillos gave away too easily, the part Frey was right about, and it’s where this goes next.

Three questions to sit with this week:

  • What capacity does your team keep calling a skill, and funding like one?

  • Where are you running a workshop to install something that only gets built over years?

  • What becomes possible if you put that capacity on a capital plan instead of a training line?

CLUTCH is the operating system I built for this, the infrastructure that holds judgment when the situation has no precedent. Plato spent the Republic asking what it takes for something to function well. I’ve reread it for thirty years now, more times than is strictly healthy, and the one thing all that formation bought me is this: the ability to take a 2,400-year-old question and aim it at something Plato never imagined, your org chart. Which is rather the point. You can’t weekend-course your way to that. This is the buildable version of his question.
