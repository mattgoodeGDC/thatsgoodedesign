The Infographic of Broken Dreams: A Brief Rant on Leadership Slop

Matt Goode8 January 20267 min read Get new pieces by email

# Off the clock

They say a picture is worth a thousand words – but not if it already has a thousand words on it.

Scrolling through LinkedIn and Instagram has become an exercise in watching people try to cram an entire MBA curriculum into a single image. The algorithm demands sacrifice, and brother, are we sacrificing – mostly our eyesight and our will to live.

You know the ones. “47 LEADERSHIP HACKS THAT WILL TRANSFORM YOUR TEAM 🚀💡🔥” crammed into a single graphic with a gradient background that screams “I just discovered Canva.” Seven different fonts fighting for dominance. Bullet points nested inside bullet points. A sidebar. Three callout boxes. An icon library that looks like emoji vomit. And somehow, impossibly, a QR code.

All on one image.

It’s the all-you-can-eat buffet approach to wisdom: quantity over quality, volume over value, and if we can fit enough words onto a single JPEG maybe nobody will notice we’re just repackaging “be nice and work hard.”

A Brief History of Crimes Against Clarity

Remember the Afghan War PowerPoint slide? That glorious spaghetti diagram that looked like what happens when you ask AI to visualize a migraine? General Stanley McChrystal took one look at it and said, “When we understand that slide, we’ll have won the war.”

At least that monstrosity was meant to be part of a presentation – context provided separately by an actual human explaining things. These Instagram infographics? They’re trying to be the entire war strategy, the briefing, the appendices, and the executive summary all in one 1080x1080 pixel square.

It’s like they looked at that Afghan slide and thought, “You know what this needs? More information and less space.”

Edward Tufte is Weeping Somewhere

Edward Tufte literally wrote the book on visual communication. Multiple books, actually. The Visual Display of Quantitative Information is sitting on design shelves gathering dust while LinkedIn gurus create what Tufte would call “chartjunk” – but somehow worse, because at least chartjunk was usually trying to display actual data.

These infographics aren’t chartjunk. They’re everything-junk.

Tufte’s principles are devastatingly simple:

  • Maximize data-ink ratio: Every element should convey information

  • Avoid chartjunk: Decoration that interferes with understanding

  • Show the data: Let the information speak for itself

  • Encourage comparison: Make relationships visible

The leadership infographic industrial complex looked at these principles and said, “What if we did the exact opposite of all of that?”

Maximum decoration. Minimum actual information. Data buried under seven layers of gradients and drop shadows. And the only comparison you can make is “this one has slightly fewer emoji than the last one.”

Tufte would call it “contempt for the audience.” I call it a hostage situation, with your eyeballs as the hostages.

Richard Mayer Would Also Like a Word

And then there’s Richard Mayer, who spent his career studying how people actually learn from multimedia. He came up with 12 principles. These one-page monstrosities violate all of them simultaneously:

  • Coherence Principle: Cut the extraneous crap (violated by: cramming in 63 numbered tips)

  • Signaling Principle: Highlight what actually matters (violated by: making everything the same size in different colors)

  • Redundancy Principle: Don’t overload people with the same information in multiple formats (violated by: icons + text + more text + decorative text)

  • Spatial/Temporal Contiguity: Put related information together (violated by: putting everything everywhere because there’s nowhere else to put it)

But do the “50 Ways to Be a Better Boss” infographic makers care? They do not. They’re out here treating white space like it’s a moral failing, speedrunning cognitive overload like it’s an Olympic sport.

Between Tufte and Mayer, we have decades of research on visual communication. And yet here we are, in the hellscape of “23 TRAITS OF EXCEPTIONAL LEADERS” where every principle is honored in the breach.

What Years of Teaching Taught Me

I used to teach kids how to create presentations. Not because I had mad graphic design skills – I don’t – but because years in a classroom taught me some basic rules about communication.

The fundamental truth: If you’re trying to teach something, clarity beats comprehensiveness every single time.

A good visual should have:

  • One main idea

  • Enough breathing room that your eye knows where to land

  • A reason to exist beyond “the algorithm likes images”

If your infographic needs footnotes, you’ve already lost.

If someone needs to zoom in to read it, you’ve already lost.

If it takes longer to parse your graphic than it would to just read a paragraph, what are we even doing here?

The Context Problem (Or: Why Workshop Handouts ≠ Instagram Posts)

Here’s where this gets tricky. I’ve facilitated plenty of workshops where participants asked for a one-pager or some sort of documentation as a take-away. This is a good request. People want to remember what they learned. They want something to reference later. They want to share it with their teams.

In that context – with you standing there explaining things, the workshop’s shared experience still warm, and the ability to ask questions – a dense one-pager actually works. It’s a memory aid, not a teaching tool. It’s anchored to the conversation you just had.

But then someone takes that workshop handout, screenshots it, strips away all the context, and posts it to LinkedIn with “POWERFUL INSIGHTS 💡” and suddenly it’s doing a job it was never designed to do.

The difference:

  • Workshop handout: “Remember when we discussed X? Here’s the framework we built together.”

  • Instagram infographic: “Here is X with no setup, no examples, no nuance, and you have 2.5 seconds to absorb it while scrolling past a dog video.”

One is a souvenir. The other is trying to be the entire vacation.

And look, I get the impulse. You spent three hours facilitating a great session. You want to share that value with people who couldn’t attend. Noble goal! But the solution isn’t to cram the entire workshop onto a single image and call it “accessible.”

Some actual best practices for sharing frameworks digitally:

  1. Pick one piece: Share the most compelling insight, not the entire model. One wedge of the pie, not the whole bakery.

  2. Lead with context: A short paragraph explaining why this matters before showing what it is. Revolutionary, I know.

  3. Assume zero prior knowledge: Your workshop participants had three hours with you. Instagram has 2.5 seconds. Act accordingly.

  4. Make peace with multiple posts: If your framework has six parts, that’s six pieces of content. Not six sections crammed onto one graphic.

  5. Link to the real thing: Use the post to drive people to a PDF, a blog post, a video – somewhere they can actually learn instead of just squinting.

  6. Test the phone view: If you can’t read it clearly on your phone without zooming, no one else can either. That’s not engagement, that’s an eye exam.

The hard truth? Most frameworks worth teaching can’t be taught in a single image. And trying to force it just creates visual noise that helps no one.

The Greatest Irony

These “thought leaders” posting “40 ESSENTIAL COMMUNICATION SKILLS 📊✨” on infographics so dense they require a magnifying glass and a prayer are the same people charging $5,000 to teach you executive presence.

It’s like getting fitness advice from someone who gets winded opening a jar of pickles.

Or minimalism tips from a hoarder.

Or – and hear me out – leadership clarity from someone whose visual communication strategy is “JUST PUT ALL OF IT IN THERE.”

If your leadership framework requires 73 numbered points on a single image, you don’t have wisdom. You have a hoarding problem with bullet points and a fundamental misunderstanding of what an infographic is supposed to do.

A Modest Proposal

Here’s a wild idea: maybe hire an actual graphic designer?

I know, I know. Revolutionary. But there are literally thousands of talented designers on Fiverr, Upwork, and 99designs who would love to take your 47-point manifesto and turn it into something that doesn’t make people’s eyes bleed. For like, $50-$200.

That’s it. That’s the cost of not assaulting your audience’s visual cortex.

Less than your monthly LinkedIn Premium subscription. Less than that leadership book you bought and didn’t read. Less than one fancy coffee per day for a week.

But no. Instead, we get template #47 from Canva with every text box filled in because we paid for the whole canvas, we’re gonna use the whole canvas.

What This Could Be Instead

Here’s the thing: infographics can actually be great. A single compelling stat. A simple comparison. A visual metaphor that makes a concept click. Think Ben Franklin’s Join or Die. Or Charles Joseph Minard’s famous flow map of Napoleon’s Russia campaign.

But that requires choosing. Editing. Having the confidence to say, “This one thing matters most.” Minard’s point: This is what happens when you invade Russia in the winter.

Instead, we get everything bagels masquerading as insight – all the toppings, none of the bread, and you’re supposed to eat it off a screen while scrolling past forty other everything bagels that look exactly the same.

So what would a good one look like?

[Imagine generous white space here]
[One compelling image or data point]

Be clear.
Be kind.
Get out of the way.

Three lines. Not 73.

Epilogue: Look, I get it. The algorithm rewards this stuff. People are trying to build audiences and demonstrate expertise and fit into the 1080x1080 or 4:5 attention economy.

But maybe – maybe – we could collectively agree that if your “quick visual guide” requires people to screenshot and zoom in on their phone to read your 8-point font, it’s not actually a visual guide. It’s a PDF that got lost and ended up on Instagram.

Just a thought. From someone with no graphic design skills whatsoever, but enough years in a classroom to know the difference between teaching and cramming.

And someone who’s been asked for plenty of one-pagers after workshops – which is great! Just know that what works in a room with context doesn’t necessarily work floating naked on the internet.

Choose wisely.
