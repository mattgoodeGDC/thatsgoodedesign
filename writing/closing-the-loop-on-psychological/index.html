# Psychological safety

Closing the Loop on Psychological Safety: What the Research Makes Clear

Over the past week, I published a series of essays on psychological safety – a topic I didn’t come to academically, but experientially. Like many people, I learned the hard way during the pandemic. Not because I had psychological safety, but because I didn’t. COVID coincided with dramatic leadership turnover where I worked, and the combination of crisis plus toxic leadership created a kind of pressure cooker. It pushed me out of an organization I cared about, and it shoved me straight into the work of understanding why some teams bend under strain while others quietly break.

That’s when I first dove into the research – Amy Edmondson’s decades of scholarship on learning teams, speaking-up cultures, and high-stakes coordination. I devoured The Fearless Organization and found myself mapping her concepts onto everything I was seeing on the ground. I’d already been familiar with Google’s Project Aristotle before COVID – I used it in building teams and even in a novel advisory project I called “Squads” – but the pandemic cast that work in a new light. Suddenly, this wasn’t theory; it was survival.

So when Edmondson, along with Michaela Kerrissey and Hassina Bahadurzada, published a major new study in May 2024, I read it immediately. And last week, after writing a whole series reflecting on psychological safety in practice, I decided to return to the source material – to the data itself – and distill what this study actually shows about how psychological safety behaves under real-world stress.

What the study reveals isn’t just supportive of psychological safety. It’s defining. It reframes performance, burnout, resilience, and the true operational cost of fear.

1. Psychological Safety Doesn’t Reduce Burnout by Making Work Easier – It Reduces Burnout by Making Work Shared

Burnout is too often misdiagnosed as a simple equation of volume: too much work, not enough hours. But the data reframes it entirely. Burnout isn’t primarily about workload – it’s about containment. People burn out when they are forced to hold everything themselves: the questions they can’t ask, the mistakes they can’t admit, the concerns they can’t raise. It’s the secrecy, not the strain, that exhausts them.

In teams with higher psychological safety, that pressure doesn’t stay locked inside one person’s mind or one person’s shift. It’s shared. It’s spoken aloud. It becomes a collective challenge instead of a private crisis. The burden moves from “mine alone” to “ours to solve.”

And the shift isn’t symbolic – it’s measurable. A single standard-deviation increase in psychological safety led to a 0.72-point decrease in burnout. In this kind of research, that’s enormous. Most leadership or culture interventions nudge burnout by 0.10 to 0.20 points. A change of 0.72 is four to seven times larger – the kind of movement you normally only see when something fundamental in the system changes. And it happened during a crisis, when conditions were deteriorating, not improving.

Burnout didn’t decline because the workload lightened. It declined because the silence did.

2. Psychological Safety Doesn’t Just Help People Stay Engaged – It Changes Whether They Want to Stay at All

What keeps people in an organization is rarely a ping-pong table or a wellness seminar. People stay when they trust that telling the truth won’t cost them – when they can raise a concern without being punished, ask for help without looking weak, or flag a risk without being branded difficult. Psychological safety strengthens that trust more than any perk or program ever could.

And again, the effect isn’t subtle. The researchers found that higher psychological safety produced a 0.63-point increase in willingness to stay – a shift that, in survey research, is enormous. Most retention drivers move the needle by a tenth of a point, maybe two. A jump of 0.63 is the kind of movement you’d normally expect from a major policy change or a significant compensation overhaul. But here, it emerged from one thing: people feeling safe enough to speak up.

And remember: this wasn’t measured in calm conditions. People were exhausted, understaffed, and carrying the emotional weight of a global crisis. Yet psychological safety still pulled retention upward.

Psychological safety isn’t a morale booster.
It’s a retention mechanism.
Fear makes people leave.
Safety makes them stay.

3. Psychological Safety Doesn’t Just Work in the Moment – It Accumulates Like Institutional Memory

This may be the most important finding in the entire study – and the one most leaders overlook. Psychological safety measured in 2019 predicted lower burnout and higher retention in 2021. In other words, the teams that weathered the crisis best were the ones that had already built the habit of speaking up before the crisis ever began.

Psychological safety isn’t a switch you flip when things get hard. It’s a foundation you lay when things are still manageable.

Teams that had practiced open communication – the everyday moments of asking for help, pointing out problems early, challenging assumptions respectfully – carried something into the crisis that others did not: an emotional reserve. They had evidence from their own past that telling the truth was safe. They remembered that the last time they raised a concern, the sky didn’t fall. They had muscle memory for collaboration.

So when uncertainty spiked and conditions deteriorated, their instinct wasn’t silence or self-protection. It was coordination. It was information-sharing. It was, “Let’s talk about what we’re seeing.”

That’s the compounding nature of psychological safety.
It isn’t built in the moment you need it.
It’s built in all the moments before.

Psychological safety is a long game – and the returns accumulate.

4. Psychological Safety Protects the People Who Face the Highest Interpersonal Risk

One of the study’s most humane findings is how unevenly the benefits of psychological safety are distributed – and how powerfully it protects the people who have historically had the least protection. The buffering effect was strongest among physicians, women, and people of color – groups who often operate with narrower margins for error, greater scrutiny, and fewer truly safe avenues to express uncertainty.

When psychological safety is low, these groups bear the highest interpersonal cost. The risks are greater, the penalties sharper, the silence heavier. But when psychological safety is high, they experience the greatest lift – more freedom to ask questions, more room to admit doubt, more access to help without fear of judgment.

Psychological safety levels the playing field. It makes the work equally navigable for everyone, not just those with positional or cultural advantage.

In that sense, psychological safety isn’t just a performance mechanism. It functions as a justice mechanism – redistributing safety, voice, and protection in ways that teams rarely achieve on their own.

5. Psychological Safety Can Partially Offset Material Shortages – But Not When Deprivation Is Extreme

The researchers describe psychological safety as a “social resource” – something that grows in importance precisely when material resources decline. This draws from the conservation-of-resources model, which suggests that when staffing is thin, equipment is unreliable, or time is scarce, the human system relies more heavily on whatever resources remain. And one of the most powerful is how freely people can speak.

Psychological safety doesn’t make shortages disappear. It can’t conjure new staff or fix broken equipment. But it does make the system more adaptive. When people feel safe to tell the truth, they coordinate faster, raise issues earlier, catch errors before they cascade, and recalibrate expectations together. It turns scarcity into a shared problem rather than a hidden crisis.

The study is clear that psychological safety isn’t a miracle cure. In conditions that are truly catastrophic – completely bottomed out – even the strongest social resources can’t fully counteract the damage. But in the vast majority of stressful, constrained environments, psychological safety meaningfully moderates the harm. It keeps teams functional when conditions are fraying.

Which is why reducing psychological safety efforts during difficult times is such a costly mistake. It’s like cutting your emergency brakes because gas is expensive.

6. Psychological Safety Increases Adaptive Capacity, Not Comfort

A comforting team is not the same as a capable one. One of the quiet misconceptions about psychological safety is that it makes work softer or easier. The study makes the opposite clear: psychological safety isn’t about comfort – it’s about honesty. In high-uncertainty environments, decisions are made with incomplete information, ambiguous signals, and shifting conditions. The only way to navigate that complexity is if people feel safe enough to voice doubts early, before small issues become expensive ones.

Teams with high psychological safety weren’t “happier teams” in the superficial sense. They were sharper teams. They noticed anomalies sooner. They said the uncomfortable thing before it metastasized. They had the courage to stop the train when everyone else felt pressured to keep it moving. Their advantage wasn’t mood – it was information flow.

And information flow is what drives adaptability.
Psychological safety didn’t cushion the work.
It clarified it.

It created the conditions for teams to see clearly and respond quickly – the core ingredients of true capability.

7. Psychological Safety Reduces Errors by Changing How Quickly Truth Arrives

Most teams don’t fail because someone made a mistake. They fail because someone hid one. The study reaffirms one of Edmondson’s core insights: in high-psychological-safety environments, error rates don’t increase – error reporting does. And that reporting is what allows teams to learn, adjust, and prevent small slips from turning into catastrophic failures.

Low psychological safety doesn’t get rid of problems. It gets rid of visibility.

And invisible problems are the most expensive kind. They compound quietly. They spread unchecked. They show up late, when the cost of correction is highest and the options are fewest.

Psychological safety doesn’t protect teams from mistakes. It protects them from escalation through silence.

8. Psychological Safety Enables Leaders to Lead Honestly, Not Perfectly

One of the most powerful lines in the study is also one of the simplest: leaders must be “open about not knowing.” That isn’t weakness. It’s the foundation of real-time problem-solving. When leaders perform certainty, everyone else performs silence. People hide their questions, suppress their concerns, and mask the very information leaders most need to hear.

But when leaders model humility – when they say, “I’m not sure,” or “Let’s figure this out together” – the entire system relaxes into truth-telling. Information surfaces earlier. Issues are named while they are still small. Doubt becomes data instead of a private burden.

This shift creates more than psychological safety. It creates what I often call coordination safety – the ability for a team to distribute cognitive load without shame or fear. It allows people to think together, name reality quickly, and solve problems collectively instead of alone.

Humility isn’t soft. It’s catalytic.

9. Psychological Safety Reduces Organizational Costs in Every Direction

When you pull the study’s findings together, the economic picture comes sharply into focus. Psychological safety doesn’t just shape how people feel – it reshapes how the entire system performs.

When burnout goes down, errors and absenteeism drop with it.
When turnover decreases, hiring and training costs fall.
When information flows freely, rework declines and agility rises.
When trust increases, issues escalate sooner and decisions get made faster.

These aren’t soft benefits. They’re operational gains.

Which is why the real message of this research is so important: psychological safety is not empathy work – it’s efficiency work. It improves the economics of how teams function. It’s the opposite of indulgent. It’s pragmatic, measurable, and financially significant.

Fear is expensive.
Silence is expensive.
Burnout is expensive.
Replacing talent is very expensive.

Psychological safety is one of the rare interventions that reduces all of those costs at the same time – not by adding perks or programs, but by removing the friction of fear and restoring the speed of truth.
