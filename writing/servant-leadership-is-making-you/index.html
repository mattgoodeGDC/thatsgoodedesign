Servant Leadership Is Making You Weak

Matt Goode14 February 20265 min read Get new pieces by email

# VOICES

Servant leadership is the most popular idea in modern leadership, and no one is allowed to criticize it. It has the moral high ground. It sounds selfless. It flatters the leader’s self-image. And in many organizations, it’s producing leaders who can’t make hard calls – and calling that virtue.

Here’s the problem: somewhere between Robert Greenleaf’s original insight and today’s LinkedIn consensus, “servant leadership” stopped meaning “build conditions for others to succeed” and started meaning “put everyone else’s needs ahead of your own, always, no matter what.”

That second version isn’t leadership. It’s conflict avoidance with a halo.

I spent twenty years in classrooms before starting my consulting practice. And classrooms taught me something about service that most leadership books get wrong.

The best teachers I’ve ever worked with are deeply devoted to their students. They care enormously. They sacrifice time, energy, and comfort. But they are not servants to their students. Not even close.

A great teacher will look a talented student in the eye and say: “This isn’t your best work. I know what you’re capable of, and this isn’t it. Try again.” A great teacher will set a boundary that makes a student furious – because that boundary creates the conditions for growth. A great teacher will make an unpopular decision for the room, absorb the frustration, and not flinch – because the job isn’t to be liked. The job is to build something in people that they can’t yet build in themselves.

That’s not service in the way servant leadership gets practiced in most organizations. That’s architecture. It’s designing the conditions for growth, which sometimes means being the person who creates discomfort rather than removes it.

Here’s what I see when servant leadership goes wrong – and it does so almost every time.

The leader who won’t give hard feedback because “my job is to support my team.” So the underperformer never hears the truth. They continue to produce mediocre work, wondering why they’re not advancing, while the leader congratulates themselves on being empathetic. Meanwhile, the high performers on the team – the ones watching the low bar go unaddressed – quietly start updating their résumés.

The leader who won’t make a unilateral call because “I serve the team, I don’t dictate to them.” So every decision becomes a consensus exercise. Speed dies. The team spends three meetings debating an issue that requires direction and a deadline. The leader thinks they’re being inclusive. The team thinks nobody’s driving.

The leader who absorbs every problem that flows uphill because “that’s what servant leaders do.” They become the organizational shock absorber – protecting the team from discomfort, shielding them from hard realities, and taking on work that should be distributed. They burn out. And when they finally crash, the team they “served” has no idea how to function without them – because they were never asked to.

Three leaders. Three sincere commitments to serving their teams. Three teams that are disadvantaged by it.

The original insight behind servant leadership was genuinely powerful. Greenleaf’s question – “Do those served grow as persons? Do they, while being served, become healthier, wiser, freer, more autonomous?” – is one of the best diagnostic questions in all of leadership theory.

But notice what it actually asks. It doesn’t ask whether the leader is nice. It doesn’t ask whether the leader avoids friction. It asks whether the people being led are growing. Becoming more autonomous. Getting stronger.

By Greenleaf’s own standard, a leader who avoids hard conversations isn’t serving anyone. They’re protecting themselves from discomfort and calling it service.

Growth requires friction. Autonomy requires being challenged. Becoming stronger requires encountering resistance – not being shielded from it.

The best servant leaders in Greenleaf’s sense aren’t the gentlest people in the room. They’re the ones willing to be temporarily unpopular in the service of someone else’s long-term development. That’s a much harder kind of service than “How can I make your day easier?”

There’s a phrase I use with leaders I coach: serve the growth, not the comfort.

It changes every conversation it enters.

  • “Should I step in and fix this for them?” Serve the growth, not the comfort. Let them struggle. Be available, not rescuing.

  • “Should I soften this feedback?” Serve the growth, not the comfort. Say the true thing. Say it with warmth. But say it.

  • “Should I take this problem off their plate?” Serve the growth, not the comfort. The problem is the development opportunity. Taking it away isn’t service – it’s theft.

  • “Should I let the team decide this?” Maybe. But if you’re letting them decide because you’re afraid to lead, that’s not empowerment. That’s abdication dressed up as generosity.

The litmus test isn’t “Am I being helpful?” It’s “Are the people I lead becoming more capable, more autonomous, and more willing to do hard things – because of how I show up?”

If yes, you’re serving them.

If you’re just making things easier, smoother, and more pleasant, you might be serving yourself.

None of this means be harsh. None of this means that you should ignore your team’s needs. None of this means command-and-control is the answer.

It means the highest form of leadership service is building people who don’t need you. And building people who don’t need you requires being willing to do things that don’t feel like service in the moment: holding them accountable, creating productive friction, making unpopular calls, and caring enough about someone’s growth to tell them what no one else will.

That’s not the opposite of servant leadership. That’s what servant leadership was supposed to be before we sanded off every edge and turned it into a permission slip for niceness.
