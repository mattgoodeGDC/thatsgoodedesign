The Night Nobody Waited for Orders

Matt Goode22 April 20265 min read Get new pieces by email

# 6C# DELEGATE

Isaac Davis led his Acton company at the front of the advance on North Bridge. He was killed in the first volley.

His company kept advancing.

That is what distributed readiness looks like.

By the time the British Regulars reached the bridge on the morning of April 19, 1775, the Minutemen from Concord, Lincoln, Acton, and five other towns were already in position. Thirty-two companies. Roughly 1,380 men. They had mustered inside the window, the name implies – a minute’s notice, or close enough that it matters. Nobody had told them to converge on North Bridge. They had pre-decided where to go. They had drilled the rally points through the winter. When the alarm came, the question was not what do we do. It was how fast we could get there.

Now contrast that with how your organization moves when the bell rings.

A Slack message pings. It goes through a manager, who routes it to a VP, who routes it to the founder. Three days later, a decision lands. By then, the window has closed, the client has moved on, and the person closest to the problem has learned exactly what you did not want her to learn.

Most organizations I work with have built the opposite of distributed readiness. Central authority. Untrained edges. A culture that quietly punishes anyone who acts without permission, and then complains that nobody takes initiative. The founder wants his team to move fast. He has also, without realizing it, made sure that moving fast requires three approvals, a decision memo, and a meeting.

You know the textures.

The VP who tells the new hire to use her judgment on Monday, then overrules her in front of her peers on Wednesday.

The all-hands speech about ownership, delivered by the same founder who personally signed a vendor invoice at nine p.m. last Tuesday because the team was “still getting up to speed.”

The capable hire on day ninety who has stopped asking for permission and started waiting to be told, because she has learned which one gets her through the quarter without a fight.

By midmorning at Concord, Lieutenant Colonel Francis Smith was still sending officers back to Boston to ask for reinforcements he would not see for hours. His column was being fired on from stone walls by men who had never been introduced to each other and did not need to be. Smith brought the best-trained professional infantry in the world. He lost because he did not build the Minutemen. He brought Regulars.

If you are the Head of People or Operations reading this, you are probably the Minuteman of your own organization and have been for a while. You have drilled your team on the patterns they need. You have written the playbooks nobody reads until the night they break. You have built the receiving culture that the founder takes credit for when the save happens.

Here is the harder question.

The readiness your organization thinks it has – is it actually yours? Carried in your head, in your inbox, in the Slack threads you are on that nobody else reads? If you left tomorrow, would the muster pattern leave with you?

What you are watching is not a people problem. It is an engine running on three missing cylinders.

Teaching is what closes the competence gap – not the skills gap, the authority gap, the knowing-what-you-are-allowed-to-decide gap. Agility is what gives the edges permission to respond to conditions the center cannot see. Execution is the muscle memory that turns a situation into a response without waiting for instructions. Skip one, and you get an organization that looks busy and moves slowly.

The Minutemen had all three. The Provincial Congress had spent six months building Teaching into every town. Agility was pre-authorized by the Committee of Safety in October of 1774 – every competent militiaman had pre-cleared authority to alarm his own neighbors without asking Boston for permission. Execution lived in the weekly drills. When the bell rang, the question of who decides had already been answered. The company commanders did not need a Slack thread.

The capable person on day thirty who needs permission to act is not a training issue. She is a map of where your authority structure has not caught up with your growth.

Every time she escalates a decision she was hired to make, the organization pays a distributed readiness tax.

Nobody measures it. Everybody feels it. And the person who feels it most is usually the person who wrote the onboarding and has been quietly absorbing the gap ever since.

This week's move for you is not to build the whole system. You cannot. You do not own the authority lines that are the real problem.

The move is to pick one decision that currently routes through three people and name it to your leadership team as an example of what distributed readiness would cost. One call. Not a strategy memo. Not a culture initiative. One specific decision, one specific delay, one specific dollar figure. Make the tax visible.

Regulars wait for orders. Minutemen muster on the bell.

The question is which one you have been quietly building.
