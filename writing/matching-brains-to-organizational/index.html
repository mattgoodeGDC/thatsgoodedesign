Matching Brains to Organizational Problems

Essay 2: Neurodiversity Series

Matt Goode8 January 202614 min read Get new pieces by email

# Neuroinclusion

A few years ago, I was observing a leadership team struggle through a decision.

Smart people. Aligned on values. Genuinely committed to getting it right. But the conversation kept looping – same questions, same frameworks, same impasse.

Then I noticed something: everyone in the room processed information the same way.

Sequential. Verbal. Collaborative. They could all think on their feet, build on each other’s ideas in real time, and articulate their reasoning as they went.

It looked like high-functioning teamwork.

But they kept missing the same kind of problem – the ones that required zooming out, sitting with ambiguity, or asking a fundamentally different question than the ones the data was answering.

They weren’t lacking intelligence. They were lacking cognitive diversity.

They’d all been hired, promoted, and rewarded for thinking in a particular way. And now that sameness – the thing that made them feel like a cohesive team – was creating a strategic blindspot they couldn’t see from inside their own thinking patterns.

Quick diagnostic: Think about your last strategic planning session. Who noticed the patterns no one else saw? Who asked the “dumb question” that actually exposed the flaw in your logic? Who came back three days later with the synthesis everyone needed? Those are your neurodivergent thinkers – whether they’re diagnosed or not. And if you can’t name anyone who fits that description, that’s your signal.

Because the problem wasn’t that these leaders weren’t smart enough.

It’s that they were all smart in the same way.

The Question No One’s Asking

Most leaders treat neurodiversity as a compliance issue.

They ask: “How do we accommodate this person?”

Or, if they’re more progressive: “How do we help this person succeed despite their diagnosis?”

Both questions start from the same flawed premise – that neurodivergence is a deviation from an optimal cognitive norm, and the organization’s job is to smooth over the gaps so the person can still perform.

But here’s what that framing misses:

Different brains don’t just process differently. They see differently. And that difference isn’t a bug to fix – it’s a strategic capacity most organizations are leaving entirely untapped.

The question isn’t “How do we accommodate this person?”

It’s “Which organizational problems actually require this cognitive profile to solve effectively?”

Because once you stop thinking of neurodiversity as diagnosis categories to accommodate and start thinking of it as a cognitive toolkit, everything changes.

You stop designing for conformity.

You start designing for complementarity.

The Cognitive Profiles Your Strategy Is Missing

Let’s be specific.

When I work with organizations stuck on the same recurring problems – innovation stalls, quality failures, strategic blind spots, execution gaps – I don’t start by looking at competencies or credentials.

I start by looking at cognitive profiles.

Because certain kinds of problems don’t yield to certain kinds of thinking. And if your team is cognitively homogeneous, you’ll keep solving the same problems while the others compound quietly in the background.

Here’s what I mean – and what these cognitive styles actually unlock:

Autistic cognition – Pattern recognition at scale. Systems thinking. Logical consistency. Quality control that doesn’t fatigue under repetition. The ability to hold a mental model of an entire system and notice when one variable is out of alignment.

When an autistic analyst says “This doesn’t match the pattern,” they’re seeing variance that neurotypical brains average out into noise. When they say “This feels structurally wrong,” they’re detecting inconsistency at a resolution most teams can’t access. This isn’t nitpicking – it’s the brain that sees the structural flaw everyone else has been working around.

Dyslexic cognition – Big-picture strategy. Narrative thinking. The ability to synthesize across domains and see connections others miss. Crisis navigation that doesn’t freeze under ambiguity.

When a dyslexic strategist says “This feels off,” they’re detecting structural misalignment three moves down the road. While others optimize within known constraints, dyslexic thinkers are scanning for the paradigm shift, the reframe, the entirely different way of solving the problem. In evolutionary terms, this is “explorer” cognition – the brain that thrives when the terrain is unfamiliar and the old maps don’t work.

ADHD cognition – Novelty detection. Rapid pattern-switching. The ability to hyperfocus when interest aligns with task. Kinetic ideation – thinking that happens through movement, through trying, through iterating in real time rather than planning in advance.

When an ADHD innovator says “What if we tried this completely different approach?” they’re offering a solution that wasn’t constrained by how things have always been done. This is the brain that catches the early signal everyone else filters out as noise. The one that gets bored with stable systems and starts experimenting before the data says it’s time.

Anxiety-driven cognition – Threat modeling. Scenario planning. Risk mitigation that doesn’t require a crisis to activate. The brain that’s already asking “what if this fails?” while everyone else is celebrating the launch. This isn’t pessimism – it’s predictive vigilance. And in high-stakes environments, it’s the cognitive profile that prevents catastrophic blindspots.

Now here’s the part most leaders miss:

None of these profiles are better than the others.

They’re complementary.

And when you treat them as such – when you stop trying to force every brain into the same workflow and start matching cognitive profiles to organizational problems – you don’t just get better accommodations.

You get better solutions.

The Business Case You’re Not Seeing

This isn’t academic theory. The ROI is measurable.

JPMorgan Chase’s Autism at Work program reports a 50% increase in productivity among teams with autistic professionals in technology roles. Microsoft’s neurodiversity hiring initiative shows 30% improvement in quality assurance outcomes. SAP reports that teams with neurodivergent members are 30% more productive than those without.

The gains aren’t coming from “accommodation costs” being offset. They’re coming from accessing pattern recognition, systems thinking, and quality control that neurotypical teams simply can’t match.

The question isn’t whether you can afford to hire neurodivergent professionals.

It’s whether you can afford to keep solving problems with only one cognitive toolkit.

From Accommodation to Architecture

Let’s map this to CREATE – because this isn’t just about hiring. It’s about system design.

When I trace innovation failures or quality breakdowns back through the framework, the issue almost never lives in individual performance. It lives in Execution – specifically, in the assumption that execution systems should be designed for linear, sequential processing.

Most organizations build their workflows like assembly lines: clear steps, predictable handoffs, uniform expectations for how work gets done. And for certain kinds of problems – the ones where the path is known and the variables are controlled – that works beautifully.

But innovation bottlenecks, strategic blind spots, and quality failures don’t respond to linear thinking.

They require non-linear cognition.

They need the brain that sees the whole system at once.

The brain that notices the pattern everyone else missed.

The brain that asks “but what if we’re solving the wrong problem?”

And if your execution systems are designed exclusively for neurotypical processing, you’re not just making life harder for neurodivergent employees.

You’re systematically excluding the cognitive tools you need to solve your hardest problems.

This is where cognitive complementarity becomes a design principle, not a diversity initiative.

You don’t redesign execution to be “nicer.”

You redesign it because certain organizational problems literally cannot be solved by cognitively homogeneous teams.

Decision-Making Through a Neurodivergent Lens

Let’s get even more specific – because this plays out in how decisions get made.

The 6C Decision Compass maps six decision-making modes: Command, Consultative, Collaborative, Consensus, Core Expertise, Choice. Most leaders assume these modes are context-dependent – you choose the right one based on urgency, complexity, or stakeholder buy-in.

But here’s what I’ve observed working with neurodivergent professionals:

Cognitive profile doesn’t just influence how someone works. It influences which decision-making mode they need to perform at their best.

Autistic professionals often thrive in Core Expertise and Command environments – where the rules are clear, the logic is transparent, and the decision path is structurally sound. Ambiguity doesn’t feel like freedom; it feels like cognitive noise. Give them clarity, and they’ll execute with a precision most teams can’t match. But force them into a Collaborative decision process where the social dynamics are as important as the logic? You’ve just introduced a cognitive tax that has nothing to do with their ability to solve the problem.

ADHD brains often excel when given Choice – autonomy over how, when, and in what order they tackle work. The structure isn’t the timeline; it’s the outcome. Tell them what needs to happen and why it matters, then let them find their own path. That autonomy activates hyperfocus. But lock them into rigid, sequential workflows with no room for improvisation? You’ve turned their greatest strength – adaptive, kinetic problem-solving – into a liability.

Dyslexic thinkers often need Consultative mode – the chance to talk through their thinking out loud, to build the idea in real time through conversation rather than pre-formed written briefs. Their brilliance doesn’t show up in the first draft of the memo. It shows up in the way they synthesize across inputs, connect disparate ideas, and reframe the entire question mid-discussion. But if your culture requires “come to the meeting prepared with a written proposal,” you’ve just filtered out the cognitive profile most likely to see the strategic opportunity everyone else missed.

Anxiety-driven cognition performs best when decisions include explicit risk assessment – when scenario planning and threat modeling aren’t treated as pessimism but as essential inputs. These are the people who will save you from overconfidence bias. But only if you create decision structures where “what could go wrong?” is a valued question, not a mood-killer.

This isn’t about accommodating preferences.

It’s about designing decision structures that unlock cognitive capacity instead of suppressing it.

The Organizational Bottleneck Matrix

Here’s where this gets practical.

Stop thinking about neurodiversity as a talent pipeline issue. Start thinking about it as a problem-solving architecture issue.

Different organizational bottlenecks require different cognitive profiles. And when you map them explicitly, hiring becomes strategic – not because you’re “checking a diversity box,” but because you’re matching brains to problems.

Innovation stalls → You need explorer brains. Dyslexic and ADHD cognition excel here. These are the thinkers who get bored with optimization and start experimenting before the data proves it’s safe. They don’t wait for permission to see differently – they literally can’t help it. If your product roadmap feels stale, if your strategy feels incremental, if everyone’s solving the same problem slightly better instead of asking if it’s the right problem – you don’t have an innovation problem. You have a cognitive homogeneity problem.

Quality failures → You need exploiter brains. Autistic cognition, detail-oriented processing, the kind of thinking that doesn’t fatigue under repetition and doesn’t miss the edge case everyone else assumes won’t matter. These are the people who see the system-level inconsistency that’s been hiding in plain sight. They’re not being pedantic – they’re modeling structural integrity in real time. If your quality control keeps missing the same category of error, it’s because you don’t have enough brains wired to notice inconsistency at that resolution.

Strategic blind spots → You need pattern detectors. Autistic and anxiety-driven cognition both excel here – one sees structural patterns, the other sees threat patterns. These are the people who will tell you what you don’t want to hear before it becomes what you can’t ignore. If your leadership team keeps getting surprised by risks “no one saw coming,” it’s not that no one saw them. It’s that the people who saw them either weren’t in the room or weren’t listened to when they spoke.

Execution gaps → You need systems thinkers + kinetic activators. Pair autistic cognition (who can hold the whole system in mind and see where dependencies break) with ADHD cognition (who can move fast, iterate in real time, and adapt when the plan hits reality). Separately, they might struggle – one needs structure, one needs autonomy. Together, they’re the execution engine most organizations are trying to build with homogeneous teams and wondering why it keeps stalling.

This isn’t theory.

This is the pattern I see again and again when teams finally start solving the problems that had been chronic for years.

They didn’t get smarter.

They got cognitively diverse.

The Turn: From Deficit to Design

Here’s the shift that has to happen – and it starts with language.

Stop saying “accommodation.”

Start saying “optimization.”

Because when you design a meeting structure that allows for written pre-reads and post-meeting async input, you’re not accommodating ADHD or autistic employees.

You’re optimizing for insight capture.

When you create decision protocols that separate thinking time from speaking time, you’re not accommodating introverts or slow processors.

You’re reducing groupthink and increasing decision quality.

When you build workflows that allow for hyperfocus blocks and non-linear task sequencing, you’re not accommodating neurodivergent work styles.

You’re unlocking the conditions under which certain kinds of problems actually get solved.

The cognitive profiles you’re calling “accommodations” are actually the missing tools in your problem-solving toolkit.

And the systems you’re designing to “help people succeed despite their differences” are systematically preventing those differences from doing what they do best: solving the problems neurotypical cognition keeps missing.

Three Diagnostic Questions

Before we close, three questions to surface where your organization might be leaving cognitive capacity on the table:

1. What problems keep recurring despite hiring “smart people”?

If you keep hitting the same walls – innovation stalls, quality failures, strategic blind spots – the issue isn’t intelligence. It’s cognitive homogeneity. You’re hiring people who think the same way, which means they’ll see the same solutions and miss the same problems.

2. Where are you designing workflows around one cognitive style and calling it “best practice”?

Linear, sequential, verbally-driven, real-time collaboration isn’t a neutral default. It’s a cognitive preference that’s been institutionalized. And every time you call it “best practice,” you’re filtering out the cognitive profiles that could solve the problems you can’t.

3. When was the last time someone saw a problem no one else noticed – and how did your system respond?

If the answer is “we told them it wasn’t a priority” or “we asked them to focus on their assigned work,” you’ve just trained pattern-detectors to stop detecting. And the next time they see something critical, they won’t say anything. Not because they stopped seeing it – but because your system taught them their way of seeing doesn’t count.

There’s no such thing as an average brain.

Just cognitive profiles your strategy hasn’t learned to leverage yet.

The question isn’t whether neurodivergent employees can succeed in your systems.

It’s whether your systems are designed to solve the problems only they can see.

Because the cost of cognitive conformity isn’t just human.

It’s strategic.

And the organizations that learn to match brains to problems won’t just be more inclusive.

They’ll be more resilient, more adaptive, and more capable of solving the challenges neurotypical cognition keeps missing.

Cognitive Diversity Self-Assessment

Rate your agreement with each statement on a scale of 1-5: (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree)

1. I can clearly identify which recurring organizational problems might require different cognitive styles to solve.

  • 1 = Cannot identify / 2 = Rarely identify / 3 = Sometimes identify / 4 = Usually identify / 5 = Clearly identify

2. Our team composition intentionally includes people who think and process information in fundamentally different ways.

  • 1 = Not at all / 2 = Slightly / 3 = Somewhat / 4 = Significantly / 5 = Deliberately and systematically

3. When someone identifies a problem others missed, our system treats their perspective as valuable insight rather than tangential concern.

  • 1 = Rarely valued / 2 = Sometimes valued / 3 = Usually valued / 4 = Consistently valued / 5 = Actively sought

4. Our workflows and decision-making processes work well for people who think non-linearly, need processing time, or work best asynchronously.

  • 1 = Don’t work at all / 2 = Work poorly / 3 = Work adequately / 4 = Work well / 5 = Designed for this

5. I understand which specific cognitive profiles (pattern detection, systems thinking, strategic synthesis, etc.) would help solve our current organizational bottlenecks.

  • 1 = No understanding / 2 = Slight understanding / 3 = Moderate understanding / 4 = Strong understanding / 5 = Clear and specific understanding

Scoring Guide:

20-25 points: You’re likely leveraging cognitive diversity as strategic architecture. Consider: How can you document and scale your approach? What specific outcomes can you attribute to cognitive complementarity?

15-19 points: You recognize the value of different thinking styles but may not be systematically designing for them. Consider: Which organizational problems keep recurring? What cognitive profile might solve them? How could you intentionally design for that profile?

10-14 points: Your systems may be designed for cognitive conformity even if your intentions are inclusive. Consider: When was the last time someone who thinks very differently from you solved a problem your team had been stuck on? If you can’t remember, that’s your signal.

5-9 points: You’re likely selecting for cognitive sameness and experiencing the strategic costs. Consider: Look at your recurring problems – innovation stalls, quality failures, strategic blind spots. These aren’t execution problems. They’re cognitive homogeneity problems.

Next Steps Based on Your Score:

If you scored 15+:

  • Map your current team’s cognitive profiles (even informally – who spots patterns? who sees big picture? who catches risks?)

  • Identify which organizational bottlenecks align with which cognitive strengths

  • Design one pilot project that deliberately pairs complementary cognitive styles

If you scored 10-14:

  • Audit your last three recurring problems: What cognitive profile might have caught them earlier?

  • Interview one person on your team who thinks very differently from you: “What do you see that others miss?”

  • Choose one workflow to redesign: Can it work for both linear and non-linear thinkers?

If you scored below 10:

  • List your three biggest organizational bottlenecks (innovation, quality, strategy, execution)

  • For each, ask: “What if this isn’t an execution problem but a cognitive toolkit problem?”

  • Read the Organizational Bottleneck Matrix section again – which cognitive profiles are you missing?

Regardless of score: Read Essay 3 to learn how the “masking tax” is bleeding your best performers – and how to recognize cognitive camouflage before it becomes burnout and attrition. Because before you can leverage cognitive diversity, you need to stop asking people to hide it.

The business case is clear.

Organizations that get neuroinclusive leadership right reduce attrition, catch blind spots earlier, and build systems that don’t depend on heroic leadership.
