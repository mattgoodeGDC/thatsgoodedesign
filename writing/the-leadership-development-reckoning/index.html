The Leadership Development Reckoning of 2026

Why L&D Needs a System, Not a Syllabus

Matt Goode5 January 20267 min read Get new pieces by email

# Coaching# Winning feels like a near miss

Inspired by the HBR article “Leaders, Bring Your Best Self into the New Year” (Carucci & Chamorro-Premuzic, January 2026)

I took a deliberate pause from writing at the end of last year. Not a burnout collapse or a dramatic retreat, but a conscious step back – the kind where you realize the best parts of your life have been waiting patiently for you to stop multitasking long enough to notice them. I spent more time with people I love, cooked slowly, lingered in conversations that didn’t orbit work, and created little pockets of stillness where I could hear myself think again.

And of course, because I can’t help it, I read. A lot.

One of the pieces that stayed with me was a deceptively gentle but quietly radical article in Harvard Business Review. If it’s behind a paywall, apologies – most places allow a handful of free reads. But the opening line alone is worth the click:

“No one operates at maximal performance all the time.”

A simple sentence, but an utterly profound truth – and in many ways, the starting point of a larger reckoning.

The authors describe a divide between our maximal self – the best version of who we are when everything aligns – and our typical self, the one shaped by pressure, politics, exhaustion, and the invisible scaffolding that holds our organizations together. They note that leaders who manage to narrow this gap “consistently deliver better results and build healthier teams.”

I believe this deeply. And yet, as I read, a parallel truth kept rising like a tide: leaders aren’t struggling because they don’t know who their best selves are. They’re struggling because the systems they inhabit rarely make space for that version to exist.

We tend to talk about leadership as if it is an act of personal virtue, a psychological tightening of the core. But identity is contextual. Courage is contextual. Even values are contextual. And organizations – with their cultural antibodies, competing incentives, and unspoken rules – shape leaders far more than leaders shape their organizations.

Carucci and Chamorro-Premuzic name this drift with painful accuracy, describing “a kind of existential burnout: achieving outcomes that please others but fail to nourish their own sense of purpose,” alongside the “habitual behaviors, default adaptations, and everyday patterns of interaction” that pull people away from their intentions.

Most leaders know this feeling intimately: the subtle erosion of authenticity in the name of survival.

But what struck me is that these “default adaptations” aren’t solely psychological. They’re architectural. When you zoom out, they are predictable responses to systems that reward conformity over courage, political navigation over principled dissent, performance over learning, and heroic individualism over shared clarity.

So, in the spirit of building on the HBR article rather than merely echoing it, here are the five shifts leadership development must make in 2026 – if we want leaders’ maximal selves to be accessible not only in rare, brilliant moments but in the rhythm of their daily work.

1. Strategy: Leadership Development Must Become the Interpreter of the Business

The article offers a line that is both elegant and provocative: “Leadership development is not a journey of self-discovery but of self-reinvention.”

Reinvention is essential – but reinvention cannot happen divorced from the work.

For years, leadership development has functioned almost like a parallel curriculum, orbiting just outside the gravitational pull of real business needs. Companies move toward AI transformation, operating model redesign, customer experience overhaul – and L&D rolls out workshops on “effective communication” and “strategic mindset.” Good topics, just not strategic ones.

Leaders do not need abstract concepts. They need discernment – the capacity to connect what they are learning to the actual business decisions, pressures, and tradeoffs they face. If AI is reshaping the workflow, leaders don’t need an AI 101 primer. They need to learn where human judgment still leads, where AI accelerates, and where AI should be prevented from interfering. They need the ability to slow down the rush toward efficiency long enough to ask: What is the actual problem we’re solving? And where does technology serve – versus distort – good leadership?

Leadership development should not be a syllabus. It should be the interpretive arm of the strategy.

2. Architecture: Culture Lives Between Meetings, Not Within Workshops

The authors write that leaders slip unconsciously into “pleasing, over-performing, and self-editing,” while “mistaking old survival strategies for their true identity.” They also name leaders’ fears – “being wrong,” “losing control,” “disappointing others” – as well as the behavioral fallout: “staying silent,” “avoiding conflict,” “over-editing themselves with senior stakeholders.”

These are not personality flaws. They are predictable outcomes of the systems we place leaders in. One of the sharpest lines in the article is this: “Political skill – the art of fitting in – is rewarded far more consistently than transformative courage.” And the follow-on warning: “Cultural antibodies attack deviation.”

Exactly.

Leaders do not underperform because they are fragile, resistant, or misaligned. They underperform because many organizations run on an operating system built for a different era – one that disproportionately rewards safety, sameness, and social calculation. If we want leaders to be courageous, we must redesign the conditions that currently punish courage. If we want leaders to create psychological safety, we must first extend it to them. If we want leaders to think systemically, we must stop burying them in tactical overload.

Leadership cannot thrive inside an architecture that contradicts it.

3. Design: Co-Creation Is the Only Way to Bridge Best Self and Daily Self

The article recommends a deceptively simple reflection practice: “Identify three moments when you felt most yourself and three when you felt off-track.”

It is elegant and helpful, but it surfaces a larger idea: leaders rarely feel “off-track” in isolation. They feel off-track because they are navigating incoherent systems. Most leaders are managing a complexity debt few development programs ever acknowledge: competing priorities, unclear decision rights, cross-team friction, emotional labor, strategic ambiguity, and a steady drip of not knowing what will matter most next week.

This is why co-creation in leadership development is not a nice-to-have – it’s essential infrastructure. We must stop designing leadership programs for leaders and start designing programs with them. Not after the fact. Not once a year in an advisory council. But in quarterly cycles of shared sensemaking, prototyping, feedback, and reinvention.

The article encourages leaders to “revisit your aspirational identity every 90 days.” Organizations should mirror that rhythm. Every quarter, leaders should be in conversation about the realities reshaping their work – and the supports required to meet them with integrity.

Co-creation is not a facilitation technique; it is cultural oxygen.

4. Delivery: Stop Delivering Content. Start Delivering Relevance.

The article makes an important distinction between learning mode and performance mode, noting that as leaders rise, “the risk of exposure increases,” making it harder to ask naïve questions or to learn publicly. So leaders retreat into performance mode – the safe, polished, self-edited version of themselves.

But here’s the more profound truth the article gestures toward: leaders stay in performance mode not only because of fear, but because of bandwidth. Most leaders today live in a near-constant state of cognitive triage: decision firefighting, context switching, emotional caregiving, strategic uncertainty, cross-functional conflict. Performance mode becomes the only way to stay upright.

Learning becomes sustainable only when it is directly connected to the tensions leaders are already carrying, embedded in real workflows instead of abstract programming, paced to reduce cognitive load rather than add to it, and modeled by senior leaders who are willing to practice what the article calls “publicly modeling vulnerability, such as admitting when they’ve changed their mind.”

When executives do this inside development environments, something shifts. The air gets lighter. Leaders breathe again. Learning becomes communal rather than performative.

5. Measurement: We Don’t Need Precision – We Need Insight

The article warns that “identity-hardening is one of the biggest barriers to change,” and it recommends that leaders engage in rituals of unlearning – naming habits that were once strengths but now create limitations.

Organizations need this same unlearning. We must release ourselves from the idea that measurement requires perfect precision or punishing dashboards, and replace it with measures that illuminate rather than surveil. Measures that help leaders see their impact, understand their patterns, and develop agency over their own growth.

The article closes with a line that I find both grounding and expansive: “Sustaining your best self is not a matter of willpower. It’s a discipline of awareness, intention, and continual reinvention.”

Leadership development should adopt this same ethos – a discipline, a rhythm, a commitment to noticing, adjusting, and evolving in partnership with leaders, not in parallel to them.

Where Leadership Development Must Go in 2026

Leaders don’t need more pressure to be their best selves. They need systems that prevent them from being drawn away from their best selves.

The real obstacles are not personal deficiencies. They are systemic ones: chronic overload, cultural antibodies, fuzzy expectations, political incentives, and misaligned architecture. If leadership development wants to matter in 2026, it must focus on psychological safety for leaders – not just their teams – rewarding intelligent risk-taking, reducing avoidable friction, institutionalizing co-creation, supporting identity shifts, and using measurement as insight, not judgment.

The HBR article is a call to inner alignment. This is a call to system alignment.

A leader’s best self is not a personal triumph. It is a structural possibility. When organizations build the right conditions, leaders’ best selves stop being aspirational moments and become the operating norm.
