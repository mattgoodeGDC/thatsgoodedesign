Upskilling as a Leadership Act

Matt Goode31 October 20259 min read Get new pieces by email

# Coaching

When the middle thins, everyone must learn to lead.

In my last essay, I wrote about the hollowing of the middle – the quiet collapse of the connective layer that once turned vision into action and culture into continuity. It was never just a story about structure. It was a story about what happens when we optimize for speed and inadvertently erode the spaces where meaning is made.

This essay is part two: the rebuild.

Because as organizations have flattened and technology has scaled, something deeply human has been lost in translation. AI can now synthesize vast amounts of information, forecast patterns, and even write the memos we used to labor over. But what it can’t do is teach. It can’t model self-awareness, build trust, or turn uncertainty into shared understanding.

What’s gone missing isn’t middle management as a layer of control – it’s middle management as a layer of learning. The relational infrastructure that once helped people grow, that turned mistakes into lessons and friction into alignment, has quietly disappeared.

The future doesn’t need more managers to enforce efficiency.

It needs more teachers – leaders who cultivate capability, not dependency. Leaders who can slow the conversation just long enough for others to think, make meaning, and reconnect the dots between purpose and performance.

Because leadership, at its best, isn’t about directing work.

It’s about developing wisdom – in people, in systems, and in the shared culture that outlasts any single role.

The New Mandate

According to the World Economic Forum’s 2025 Future of Jobs Report, 63% of employers cite skill gaps as the top barrier to transformation, and 85% plan to prioritize upskilling in the next five years. On its face, that sounds like a rational response to a rapidly changing world – invest in training, close the gap, move on.

But that statistic hides a deeper truth: learning is no longer an HR function; it’s a leadership function.

Upskilling isn’t just about transferring knowledge – it’s about transforming capability. It’s not a compliance exercise; it’s a cultural one. The organizations that will thrive in this next era aren’t the ones with the most courses or the most significant training budgets. They’re the ones where learning has become leadership’s daily practice.

We don’t need more programs.

We need more leaders who model curiosity, who create the psychological safety for others to ask hard questions, and who treat reflection not as a luxury, but as a discipline.

In these organizations, learning is baked into the culture. Coaching replaces control. Questions carry as much value as answers. Leadership and learning are no longer separate acts – they are the same motion, the same mindset, the same muscle.

The Managers Were the Mentors

When we flattened organizations, we didn’t just remove oversight – we removed the people who taught.

Middle managers were never simply conduits for information or compliance. They were the translators of culture – the ones who gave direction and emotional meaning. They carried the informal feedback loops, the hallway conversations, and the micro-coaching moments that made learning continuous and personal. Their leadership was lived, not announced – the quiet cadence of support, correction, and belief that helped others grow into their potential.

As Gloria St. Martin-Lowry writes in A Guide for New Middle Managers, these leaders “anchor motivation through clarity, feedback, and coaching.” They ensure that every employee understands not just what to do, but why it matters.

Now, in many organizations, that layer has disappeared – and with it, the scaffolding of human growth. People are being promoted faster but developed more slowly. Titles are advancing; wisdom is not.

Again, see my recent posts about EQ in the Age of AI. AI is crushing the domain of knowledge, but only humans can have wisdom. See my analogy about tomatos being a fruit…

It’s a quiet irony of the modern era: in flattening the org chart for efficiency, we created a new kind of bottleneck – a learning bottleneck. We gained speed, but lost the teachers who knew how to turn momentum into mastery.

Upskilling as Emotional Infrastructure

The Gallup Global Leadership Report (2024) reframes leadership not as a hierarchy of authority, but as the ability to meet four fundamental needs of followers: Trust, Compassion, Stability, and Hope. It’s a simple framework, but one with profound implications.

And it’s not theory. Gallup’s data show that employees who believe their manager cares about their development are nearly five times more likely to be engaged – a finding that reinforces what most of us already know intuitively: people don’t grow because they’re managed; they grow because they’re mentored.

When done well, upskilling becomes the modern mechanism for meeting these four needs:

  • Trust – When leaders share what they’re learning, they model integrity and transparency. Growth becomes a shared journey, not a silent expectation.

  • Compassion – When leaders coach instead of critique, they make growth feel safe. They replace judgment with curiosity.

  • Stability – When learning is embedded into the rhythm of work, it anchors teams amid change. Reflection becomes the pulse that steadies the system.

  • Hope – When organizations invest in development, they communicate belief in people’s potential – a subtle but powerful act of faith.

This is what technology can’t replicate. AI can process data, optimize tasks, and scale knowledge – but it can’t communicate belief. It can’t look someone in the eye and say, I see more in you than you see in yourself – and I’ll help you get there.

In that sense, upskilling isn’t just a strategy for capability. It’s a practice of care. It’s the modern vehicle for trust – and the bridge between efficiency and belonging.

From Training to Teaching

Most organizations have training programs.
Few have teaching cultures.

At first glance, the distinction seems semantic – a matter of labels. But dig deeper, and it reveals a structural blind spot.

Training is transactional: What do you need to know to perform?
Teaching is transformational: What do we need to understand to progress?

Training fills knowledge gaps. Teaching builds adaptive capacity.
Training reinforces the process. Teaching redefines possibility.
Training ensures compliance. Teaching ignites creativity.

That distinction marks the line between efficiency and evolution – between organizations that survive disruption and those that lead through it.

When viewed through the lens of organizational systems, training is a programmatic input; teaching is a cultural output. Training happens within HR. Teaching happens everywhere leaders show up with curiosity and humility. It’s not a course or a competency – it’s a way of operating.

In Goode Designs’ CREATE Leadership Framework, Teaching is the central gear that drives modern performance. It’s how the system converts clarity into capability, strategy into skill, and culture into continuity. Each of the six cylinders fires more efficiently when leaders teach through it:

  • Culture – Define what’s worth learning. Teaching keeps the “why” visible and connected to every decision.

  • Relationships – Make learning social and safe. People don’t learn from authority; they learn from trust.

  • Execution – Turn workflow into learnflow. Every deliverable becomes a feedback loop, every project a prototype.

  • Agility – Treat experimentation as data, not failure. Teaching reframes mistakes as insights.

  • Teaching – Multiply leadership by passing experience forward. This is how influence scales without hierarchy.
    Ethical Decision-Making – Lead with humility, not certainty. The best teachers model integrity by staying open to being taught.

The organizations that thrive in the next decade will not be the ones that simply train better; they’ll be the ones that teach by design. They’ll turn reflection into infrastructure – embedding curiosity, feedback, and shared sense-making into how work gets done.

Teaching, at this level, is not a soft skill. It’s a strategic capability. It is how leaders make culture visible and strategy sustainable. It’s how they turn human connection into a competitive advantage.

Because in an era when AI can replicate information but not insight, teaching remains the last mile of leadership – the human layer where clarity becomes commitment and learning becomes belonging.

Rebuilding the Middle as a Mesh

The future of leadership isn’t a pyramid – it’s a mesh.

We don’t need to rebuild the middle layer; we need to reimagine it as a living network of mentors, coaches, and learners who share what they know in real time. Hierarchies distribute authority. Networks distribute wisdom. In a complex, fast-moving environment, the value of leadership lies not in its position on the chart, but in its proximity to learning.

This new architecture of leadership doesn’t eliminate structure; it changes what structure is for. It shifts the question from Who reports to whom? to Who learns from whom? The organizations that will thrive aren’t those with the flattest lines, but the richest connections – where knowledge flows laterally, trust compounds, and insight travels faster than approval chains.

That might look like:

  • Rotational “learning stewards” who facilitate post-project reflections and capture lessons before momentum fades.

  • Cross-team mentorship circles that pair institutional memory with emerging energy.

  • AI-powered learning hubs that curate and surface wisdom from across the organization – ensuring expertise is shared before it’s lost.

  • Internal office hours where peers teach peers, because the best answers are often one desk – or one Slack thread – away.

In The Middle Manager as Innovator (MIT Sloan Management Review, 2023), Ingvild De Paoli, Silvia Profili, and Alessia Sammarra found that leaders who actively coach others are 2.4× more likely to drive both innovation and retention. Their finding reframes teaching not as a detour from productivity, but as its source.

Teaching doesn’t take time from work.
Teaching is the work.

It is the connective act that transforms experience into intelligence – the quiet, compounding habit that keeps organizations not just efficient, but alive.

Leadership as a Learning System

Flattened organizations often fall into two powerful illusions:

  • That communication equals connection.

  • That efficiency equals effectiveness.

Both are seductive – and both are false.

Because human systems don’t scale on communication alone, they scale on comprehension. Information is abundant. Meaning is scarce. And in every team, someone has to turn noise into narrative – to connect the dots between what’s happening and why it matters.

That someone used to be the middle manager – the quiet interpreter who turned directives into dialogue and helped teams translate pressure into progress. But as that layer has disappeared, the need hasn’t. It has simply shifted.

Now, that work belongs to everyone.

The next generation of organizations will not succeed by communicating faster, but by understanding deeper. They will scale through sensemaking – through leaders and teams who can pause long enough to align, reflect, and learn.

That’s where upskilling reclaims its truest purpose. It doesn’t just transfer skills; it restores culture as a living system of learning. It reintroduces the connective tissue between humans and purpose – the very muscle memory that keeps organizations from fragmenting under the weight of their own efficiency.

Upskilling, at its best, is not an HR initiative. It’s a cultural intervention – a way of rebuilding what flattening stripped away: meaning, mentorship, and momentum.

The Leadership Flywheel

When leadership becomes teaching, something profound happens.

Leaders model learning.
Teams mirror it.
Systems adapt to it.
Trust compounds.

That’s the flywheel of a learning culture – a self-reinforcing loop where growth no longer depends on mandate, but on momentum. It’s how organizations begin to grow faster than the rate of change, because they’ve shifted from reacting to the future to rehearsing for it.

Teaching transforms leadership from a position of authority into a practice of generosity – the daily act of turning experience into shared intelligence. When every leader becomes a teacher, every challenge becomes a classroom. Every mistake becomes curriculum. Every conversation becomes culture.

That’s the quiet revolution unfolding in the best organizations today:

Not a return to hierarchy, but the rise of humanity – where leading and learning have finally become the same act.

In the end, sustainable leadership isn’t about titles or tenure – it’s about transmission. The organizations that will endure aren’t those that communicate the most, but those that teach best – where knowledge flows freely, trust compounds naturally, and growth becomes a collective reflex.

Because when leadership becomes teaching, learning stops being an activity and becomes a way of life.

Reflection Prompt

“Who are the quiet teachers in your organization – and what might change if you recognized them as your most valuable leaders?”
