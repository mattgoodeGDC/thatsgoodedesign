The Wave Won’t Wait: Why Most Organizations Aren’t Ready for What’s Coming

Part 1 of 2

Matt Goode1 February 20266 min read Get new pieces by email

# AI

Picture this: A CEO just approved $2 million for an AI initiative. The pilot worked beautifully. Small team, clear use case, measurable results. Then came the rollout.

Six months later, adoption is at 12%. The tool exists. People aren’t using it. When she asks why, she gets shrugs, workarounds, and a lot of “we’re still figuring it out.”

The technology wasn’t the problem. The system it landed in was.

The Wave Is Here

AI isn’t a tool to adopt. It’s a wave – and it’s already moving.

PwC’s 29th Global CEO Survey captures 4,454 executives watching it build. When asked to name their single greatest concern, the answer was clear: Are we transforming fast enough to keep up with technology and AI?

The anxiety is justified. The response to it is not.

Thirty percent of CEOs report increased revenue from AI. Twenty-six percent have seen lower costs. These numbers sound promising until you do the math.

Fifty-six percent have seen neither.

The majority of global organizations – including many pouring significant resources into AI – have nothing to show for it. Not yet. Maybe not ever.

Because the wave doesn’t care about your quarterly targets. It doesn’t wait for your change management initiative. It doesn’t grade on effort.

The 12% Who Are Ready

PwC identifies an AI “vanguard” – the 12% of companies seeing both revenue gains and cost savings. What separates them from the 56% treading water?

It’s not better technology. It’s not more funding. It’s not smarter people.

It’s infrastructure.

The vanguard built foundations first:

  • Technology environments that enable AI integration

  • Clearly defined roadmaps for AI initiatives

  • Formalized Responsible AI and risk processes

  • Organizational cultures that enable adoption

Culture isn’t a soft add-on you address after the technology is deployed. It’s load-bearing architecture. Without it, the technology sits on a foundation of sand.

Meanwhile, the other 88%:

  • Only one in four CEOs says their company tolerates high-risk innovation projects

  • Only one in four has routine processes to stop underperforming R&D

  • Fewer than one in ten have implemented five or more proven innovation practices

Half of all CEOs say innovation is “central to their company’s business strategy.”

Fewer than one in ten have built the systems to support it.

This is the gap between aspiration and design. Between wanting transformation and deserving it.

The Workforce That Checked Out

Now layer in what Gallup has been documenting for years.

Only about one in five employees worldwide are engaged at work. The rest – disengaged or actively disengaged – show up, comply, do what’s asked, and nothing more.

The discretionary effort that fuels innovation? The willingness to experiment, to flag problems early, to try something new even if it might fail?

It’s sitting on the sideline.

CEOs want reinvention from a workforce that has mentally clocked out. And can you blame them? They’ve learned the lesson their organizations taught them: the safe play is the smart play. Don’t stick your neck out. Don’t fail visibly. Don’t be the one holding the bag when the pilot doesn’t scale.

That 80% didn’t become disengaged because they’re lazy. They became disengaged because the systems they work within punished the very behaviors transformation requires.

The Vicious Cycle

Here’s what no one wants to say out loud:

The pressure for transformation is causing the failure to transform.

Watch how it works:

  1. Executives demand rapid transformation. The wave is coming. Move faster.

  2. Organizations lack psychological safety. But experimentation means visible failure. And visible failure isn’t safe here.

  3. Innovation theater replaces actual innovation. Pilots launch. Decks get built. “AI initiatives” get funded. Activity substitutes for progress.

  4. Results disappoint. The 56% with nothing to show for it.

  5. More pressure is applied. Why aren’t we moving faster?

  6. Return to step one.

The wave builds. The organization treads water. And leaders wonder why throwing more urgency at the problem isn’t working.

The Tyranny of the Urgent

PwC found that CEOs spend 47% of their time on issues with time horizons of less than one year.

Only 16% goes to anything beyond five years.

They’re firefighting when they should be foundation-building. Reacting when they should be designing. And they know it – when asked what concerns them most, the fear of not transforming fast enough topped the list.

The irony: the CEOs most worried about long-term viability spend more time on short-term issues than their peers. The anxiety about the future is trapping them in the present.

That’s not a discipline problem. It’s an architecture problem. When every decision routes through you, when every fire needs your attention, when the system depends on heroic leadership to function, you become the bottleneck.

And when the wave hits, bottlenecks become casualties.

The Trust Gap

One more data point, and it’s the one that should keep executives up at night.

PwC measured total shareholder returns for companies with high versus low trust concerns.

The gap? Nine percentage points.

Trust isn’t a soft metric. It’s not about being liked. It’s operational, accountable, digital – and it shows up directly in value creation.

Two-thirds of CEOs report their companies faced trust concerns in the past year regarding AI safety, data privacy, transparency, and climate impact. The organizations bleeding shareholder value aren’t the ones with bad technology. They’re the ones where stakeholders – employees, customers, investors – have lost confidence in the system.

Gallup’s engagement crisis isn’t a motivation problem. It’s a trust problem. And trust, unlike enthusiasm, can’t be manufactured with a town hall and a slide deck.

Trust is architectural. You either build it into the system, or you don’t have it.

The Wrong Question

The question keeping CEOs up at night – are we transforming fast enough? – is the wrong question.

The organizations moving fastest aren’t pushing harder. They built systems that enabled people to move. The vanguard 12% didn’t outwork the 56%. They out-designed them.

So the real question is this:

  • What have you built that allows your people to move?

  • What’s the architecture of decision-making in your organization?

  • What happens when someone fails visibly while trying something new?

  • Where does discretionary effort go to die?

These aren’t motivation questions. They’re design questions. And they have design answers.
