The Angel in the Marble

Part 1 of 3: The Sculptor Series

Matt Goode4 February 202611 min read Get new pieces by email

# Winning feels like a near miss

Michelangelo reportedly said he saw the angel in the marble and carved until he set it free.

I’ve thought about that line for years – not as art history, but as a leadership philosophy. Because it captures something most leadership advice gets exactly backwards.

We’re trained to add. More communication. More check-ins. More initiatives. More dashboards. More off-sites. More urgency. The assumption underlying all of this is that transformation requires accumulation – that if we simply pile on enough effort, enough resources, enough pressure, something will finally shift.

But Michelangelo didn’t add the angel. He removed everything that wasn’t the angel. He didn’t build; he revealed. He looked at a block of stone and saw what was already possible, hidden beneath the excess. Then he carved.

That’s the leadership move almost no one teaches. And it’s the one that separates the organizations that transform from the ones that just thrash.

The Accumulation Trap

On Monday (2/2) and Tuesday (2/3) of this week, I wrote about the 56% of companies that have invested in AI and have nothing to show for it. The data came from PwC’s 29th Global CEO Survey – 4,454 executives watching the wave build, most of them drowning in it.

The vanguard 12% weren’t working harder. They had built systems that enabled transformation.

But here’s the question that the essay didn’t answer: How do you build better when you’re already maxed out?

I ask this because the leaders I work with aren’t lazy. They’re exhausted. Their calendars look like Tetris games played by someone losing badly. Their to-do lists reproduce overnight. Every meeting spawns two more. They’re operating at capacity – running flat out just to stay in place – and then someone tells them they also need to lead a transformation.

The instinct, when you hear that, is to find more capacity. Wake up earlier. Stay later. Protect Sunday afternoons for “strategic thinking.” Squeeze an offsite into the already-groaning Q2 schedule. Add a transformation initiative to the already crowded priority list.

That instinct is wrong. Not because effort doesn’t matter – it does. But because you can’t add your way out of a system problem. Most transformation failures are systemic problems.

W. Edwards Deming – the statistician who helped rebuild Japanese manufacturing after World War II – spent decades studying why organizations succeed and fail. His conclusion was stark: 94% of performance problems are system problems, not people problems. When something isn’t working, the issue is almost never effort or talent. It’s the invisible architecture that shapes how effort gets applied and how talent gets used.

Think about what that means. Ninety-four percent.

When adoption stalls, we blame the workforce. When quality slips, we blame the team. When transformation fails, we often attribute the failure to resistance to change. However, Deming’s research suggests that one should look elsewhere. Look at the system. Consider the incentives, information flows, decision rights, and feedback loops. Look at the architecture.

The leaders are still stuck in the 56% – they’re trying to add transformation to a system designed to resist it. They’re pushing harder on the accelerator while the parking brake is still engaged.

The 12% did something different. They released the brake first. They started removing.

The Gear Most Leaders Skip

In my work with founders and leadership teams, I use a framework called VOICES – six leadership stances for different terrain. If you read last week’s Wave series, you’ve seen this before. If not, here’s the short version.

Think of VOICES as gears in a vehicle – each one designed for different conditions:

  • Visionaries set direction and paint the horizon. Where are we going?

  • Operator drives execution with discipline. How do we get there?

  • Independent empowers ownership by stepping back. Who owns this?

  • Coach develops capability through feedback and reflection. How do I help you grow?

  • Encourager fuels belief and resilience through hard stretches. What do you need to keep going?

  • Sculptor reshapes the systems themselves. What’s blocking us – and can I remove it?

Most leaders default to two gears regardless of what the moment requires: Visionary and Operator.

It makes sense. These are the gears that got rewarded. Visionary is what “leadership” looks like in the popular imagination – the inspiring speech, the strategic clarity, the compelling vision of the future. Operator is what organizations measure – the execution, the results, the getting-it-done. Both are visible. Both are heroic. Both put the leader at the center of the story.

The other four gears require a harder material. Independent means stepping back when you want to step in. Coach means investing in someone’s development when it would be faster to just do it yourself. Encourager means tending to morale and belief when you’d rather focus on results. And Sculptor means admitting the system you built – or inherited and didn’t change – might be the problem.

So leaders skip them. Not consciously. They just reach for what’s familiar, what’s rewarded, what’s worked before. Visionary when they need to inspire. Operator when they need to deliver. Back and forth between two gears while four others rust.

For founders, especially technical founders, first-time founders, anyone who built something from scratch – Operator dominates. It’s the gear that got them here. They outworked competitors. They pushed through obstacles. They made things happen through sheer force of will and relentless execution. Operator isn’t just a gear for them; it’s an identity.

But Operator has a ceiling.

You can push harder within a system only until you hit the system’s limits. And when the system itself is the problem – when the architecture is what’s blocking transformation, not the effort – no amount of Operator energy will break through. You’ll just burn hotter while going nowhere.

That’s where Sculptor comes in.

Sculptor doesn’t optimize within the system. Sculptor redesigns the system. It asks a fundamentally different question: not How do I get this done? but What’s making this harder than it needs to be – and can I remove that instead?

This is the gear most leaders skip. Not because they don’t value it. Not because they haven’t heard that “working on the business” matters as much as “working in the business.” They skip it because no one taught them how to use it.

Leadership development programs focus on doing more: communicating more clearly, delegating more effectively, coaching more frequently, and inspiring more consistently. The assumption is always addition.

Nobody teaches you to look at a recurring problem and ask: What if this problem shouldn’t exist at all?

Nobody teaches you to remove.

The Accidental Diminisher

Liz Wiseman’s research on Multipliers offers a window into why Sculptor matters – and why skipping it costs more than most leaders realize.

Wiseman studied leaders across industries, trying to answer a simple question: Why do some leaders double the intelligence and capability of their teams while others – often without meaning to – cut it in half?

The answer wasn’t charisma. It wasn’t an experience. It wasn’t even raw intelligence. It was behavior.

Multipliers create space for others to think, take risks, and grow. They ask questions instead of providing answers. They challenge people to stretch beyond what they thought possible. They create an environment in which intelligence can emerge and capability can compound.

Diminishers – often the smartest, most capable, most well-intentioned people in the room – do the opposite. They rescue too quickly. They solve problems before others have a chance to struggle. They dominate the airtime. Their brilliance casts a shadow.

What struck me most in Wiseman’s work was her concept of the “accidental diminisher.” These aren’t bad leaders. They’re often exceptional performers who got promoted precisely because of their ability to deliver results. They work hard. They care deeply. They’re trying to help.

But their help is the problem.

When you rescue too quickly, people stop trying. When you solve problems before others can engage, you train the organization to wait for you. When your speed leaves no space for others to figure things out, you become the bottleneck – not because you’re controlling, but because the system has learned to depend on you.

The accidental diminisher is a leader stuck in Operator. They’re working hard, producing results, solving problems – and slowly training their organization to need them for everything. The system contracts around them. Decisions route upward. Initiative atrophies. When transformation requires distributed intelligence, risk-taking, and adaptation, the system can’t deliver it.

Sculptor breaks that pattern.

Not by working less. By working differently. By asking: What would need to change so this problem stops regenerating? What am I holding that someone else could own? What system am I compensating for that I could redesign instead?

Why Sculptor Is the Transformation Gear

Go back to that 56%.

They didn’t fail because they chose the wrong AI tools. Most of them chose fine tools – capable, well-designed, appropriate to their use cases. They failed because they installed new technology into old systems. Systems designed for a different era. Systems are optimized for different problems. Systems built on assumptions that no longer hold.

Then they wondered why adoption stalled.

Here’s what I’ve watched happen, over and over: A company invests in AI. The pilot works beautifully – small team, clear use case, motivated early adopters. Leadership celebrates. The rollout begins. And then... nothing. Adoption plateaus at 15%. The tool exists. People aren’t using it.

The Operator response is predictable: more training, more communication, more pressure. Town halls explaining the strategy. Dashboards tracking adoption metrics. Nudges, reminders, and “change management initiatives” are designed to encourage individuals to adopt the new behavior. Charisma sells the idea to the workforce.

None of it works. Because the technology was implemented in a system that made using it more difficult than not using it. The workflows don’t accommodate it. The incentives don’t reward it. The cultural signals say the safe play is the smart play, and using the new tool visibly – experimenting with it, potentially failing with it – doesn’t feel safe.

Until that changes, nothing changes.

The Sculptor question is not “How do we get people to adopt this?”

It’s What’s making adoption hard – and can we remove that?

That’s a fundamentally different orientation. Operator adds pressure. Sculptor removes friction. Operator demands different behavior within the same system. Sculptor redesigns the system until the desired behavior becomes the path of least resistance.

The vanguard didn’t give better speeches about transformation. They redesigned structures, incentives, and processes until adoption became easier than resistance. They carved away what was blocking the change they wanted to see.

The Removal Discipline

Here’s what makes Sculptor uncomfortable: it often appears to involve doing less.

Kill the meeting that no longer serves its purpose. Eliminate the approval gate that adds delay without adding value. Stop requiring the report no one reads. End the initiative that looked good in the planning deck but never gained traction. Decline the project that would be desirable but isn’t essential.

This is deeply uncomfortable for leaders who’ve been rewarded their entire careers for addition. Launching things. Announcing things. Building things. The leadership narrative is one of accumulation – more revenue, more headcount, more products, more initiatives. Nobody hands out awards for the meeting you eliminated or the project you killed.

But removal creates capacity. And capacity is what transformation requires.

Think about your own calendar. How much of it is spent managing problems that shouldn’t exist? Mediating conflicts that stem from unclear decision rights. Chasing updates that a better system would surface automatically. When answering questions, people could answer them themselves if they had the appropriate context. Sitting in meetings that exist because they’ve always existed, not because they’re still valuable.

Every recurring problem you manage is a system design failure. Every decision that routes to you unnecessarily is an architectural gap. Every workaround your team has normalized is a signal that something needs to be redesigned, not optimized.

Amy Edmondson’s research on psychological safety illuminates why this matters. She found that the highest-performing teams aren’t the ones that make fewer mistakes – they’re the ones that surface mistakes earlier, learn from them faster, and adapt more quickly. But that only happens when the system makes it safe to speak up. When the architecture rewards candor instead of punishing it.

Most organizations have the opposite architecture. They’ve built systems that teach people to hedge, to hide problems, to wait for permission, to avoid visible failure. And then leaders wonder why transformation stalls.

The Sculptor sees those systems. Instead of working harder to overcome them, asks: What would need to change so that the desired behavior becomes the natural behavior?

The First Sculptor Question

Most leaders, when they encounter a problem, ask: How do I solve this?

Sculptors ask something different: Should this problem exist at all?

That’s not a productivity hack. It’s not a time-management technique. It’s a fundamental reorientation in how you see your role.

Because many of the problems leaders spend their days solving are artifacts of systems they inherited or created – systems that made sense once but don’t anymore, or never made sense but nobody questioned them.

The meeting that exists because it’s always existed. The approval process was designed for a risk profile that the company outgrew years ago. The reporting cadence aligned with the previous CEO’s communication preferences. The coordination overhead exists only because the two teams lack shared visibility into each other’s work.

These aren’t problems to solve. They’re problems to eliminate. And the only way to eliminate them is to see them clearly – not as fixed constraints, but as design choices. Choices that can be unmade.

What am I managing that shouldn’t require management?

That’s the first Sculptor question. Sit with it. If you’re honest, you’ll find more answers than you want. Problems you’ve normalized. Friction you’ve accepted. Workarounds you’ve stopped noticing because you’ve been doing them so long.

The angel is hidden in there. The organization that could exist – more adaptive, more capable, more ready for transformation – if you could just clear away what’s obscuring it.
