1. The Signal Before the Silence

Essay 1 of 5

Matt Goode17 November 20259 min read Get new pieces by email

# Psychological safety

I still remember a conversation I had with a team at USAID. They were working long hours under extraordinary pressure, and yet it wasn’t the logistics or the technical hurdles that made the work hard – it was something far more human. One team member pulled me aside and said quietly, almost apologetically, “I know we’re supposed to move fast, but I’m not sure it’s safe to say what I really think in that room.”

That moment has stayed with me. Because what he named wasn’t just discomfort – it was risk. And the cost of that risk wasn’t merely interpersonal. It was operational. Leaders often imagine silence as the absence of noise, but in complex environments, silence is data. It signals friction in the system.

Psychological safety isn’t a buzzword; it’s a performance variable. And yet it remains one of the most misunderstood dynamics in modern leadership. Too often, it’s dismissed as a “soft” value – something you tend to once the ‘real’ work is done. But the evidence tells a different story: psychological safety is what makes the real work possible.

Dr. Amy Edmondson’s decades of research at Harvard Business School – and Google’s landmark Project Aristotle study – converge on the same insight: the best-performing teams are not the ones with the highest raw talent, the longest tenure, or the strictest accountability structures. They are the teams where people feel safe to speak up, challenge assumptions, admit mistakes early, and surface concerns before they calcify. In Project Aristotle’s final report, psychological safety emerged as “by far the most important” of all the predictors of team success.

Silence, by contrast, is never neutral. It is not the absence of conflict; it is the presence, and if you’re a leader paying attention, that fear often shows up first as hesitation – the quick inhale before someone decides not to voice the thing they’re actually thinking. It’s an early-warning system, a whisper that something in your culture, your cadence, or your structure isn’t aligned with what your people need to contribute fully.

I learned this long before I worked with executive teams or crisis-response units. I learned it in a previous environment where trust had quietly eroded. It wasn’t dramatic at first. It showed up in small avoidances – people being careful, guarded, selective about what they said and when. It showed up in hallway honesty but meeting-room silence. It showed up in the subtle choreography of self-protection: the softened opinions, the unasked questions, the things people rehearsed privately but never said aloud.

What struck me most was that no one wanted it that way. Not the faculty, not the staff, not even leadership. But when systems send mixed signals – when transparency is promised but unpredictability is felt – people learn to inhale before they speak. They learn to calculate rather than contribute. That experience stayed with me. It’s one reason I care so deeply about this work now: because I’ve seen what happens when safety erodes. I know how costly that silence becomes – not just emotionally, but operationally.

And here’s the part leaders often underestimate: in every system, the whisper always comes before the shout.

If you’re willing to listen early – truly listen – you can design before the damage. If you ignore it, the system will escalate on your behalf.

So the real question becomes:

Are you paying attention to the silence?

What silence tells you (if you’re listening)

If you’re a leader, coach, or systems architect, pause long enough to ask yourself a few uncomfortable questions: Where is voice missing on my team? Which conversations feel thinner than they should? Where are questions going unasked, tensions going unspoken, or feedback being filtered through deference rather than clarity?

It’s dangerously easy to mistake compliance for alignment. Teams can look cooperative on the surface while quietly navigating around risks they don’t feel permitted to name. In low-safety environments, people don’t stop generating ideas or spotting problems – they stop sharing them. The insight doesn’t disappear; it internalizes. And once it goes underground, you lose access to the very information that allows your system to adapt.

This is why Amy Edmondson’s original research continues to be so influential. She defined psychological safety as “a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking,” a framing that has been validated repeatedly across industries. That “risk” might look like naming a flaw in the plan, surfacing a client concern early, questioning underlying assumptions, or offering dissent to someone higher on the org chart. In Edmondson’s early hospital studies – and later reinforced by research in aviation, manufacturing, and tech – the teams that performed best weren’t the ones with fewer errors. They were the ones who spoke openly about mistakes early.

When that interpersonal risk feels too high, silence inevitably fills the space. And that silence isn’t merely cultural; it’s structural. People hold back not because they lack courage, but because something in the system has taught them that withholding is safer than contributing. As Google’s Project Aristotle found, psychological safety isn’t a “nice to have” feature – it’s the single strongest predictor of whether a team learns quickly, solves problems creatively, and collaborates effectively.

This is why coaching confidence is never enough. You can coach people to be brave, but bravery cannot compensate for a system that punishes candor. You can teach communication models, but they won’t take root in a meeting rhythm that rewards speed over reflection. The real work is architectural: redesigning the structures, norms, and rituals that determine whether voice is welcomed or withheld.

So ask yourself – not as an evaluator, but as an investigator:

What in my system makes speaking up feel costly?

And what would need to shift so that contribution feels like the default, not the exception?

A simple reflection – grounded in the data

Let’s begin with five questions drawn from – or adapted from – Edmondson’s Psychological Safety Index. Treat them as a quiet diagnostic. You can run them as a self-check, use them in a team retrospective, or integrate them into your leadership cadence as a standing pulse.

Rate each on a scale from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree):

  1. Members of this team are able to bring up problems and tough issues.

  2. I feel comfortable speaking up about mistakes or concerns.

  3. When I share an idea, it is taken seriously.

  4. People on this team are willing to listen to different points of view.

  5. It is easy to raise a concern with my manager. (adapted)

I’ve also included a simple Google Form – built on Edmondson’s five questions – that you can copy and use to track this dimension of psychological safety with your team. Click here for a copy of that form.

These questions may look simple, but Edmondson’s longitudinal studies – as well as meta-analyses published in Harvard Business Review, MIT Sloan Management Review, and the Annual Review of Organizational Psychology – all highlight the same pattern: teams don’t fail from lack of skill. They fail from a lack of voice. The earliest signals of dysfunction show up not in the KPIs, but in the conversations that never happen.

So here’s the threshold that matters:

If your average score across these items is below a 4.0, you’re not looking at a personnel issue. You’re looking at a leadership design issue.

And design issues are fixable – but only if you’re willing to listen before the silence becomes costly. Leaders often hope that interpersonal trust will “grow” on its own, but safety is rarely the product of goodwill. It’s the product of architecture: meeting structures, power dynamics, decision norms, and the unwritten rules that govern how risk is handled in the room.

So ask yourself:

What would your team’s answers reveal about the conditions you’ve built – not the intentions you hold?

So what do we do about it?

Here’s where I want to offer both perspective and practice. Psychological safety isn’t something you announce – it’s something you design. Cultures don’t shift because a leader says, “This is a safe space.” They shift because the system proves it consistently in moments when speaking up carries real risk.

When I coach executives, founders, and systems leaders, I start with one clarifying question:

What are the unspoken rules of voice in your organization?

Every team has them.
Who speaks first?
Who speaks last?
Who interrupts?
Who apologizes before offering an idea?
Who offers dissent freely – and who never does?
And most telling of all: What is the cost of candor?

Edmondson’s research makes this plain: teams rarely silence themselves because of explicit rules. They silence themselves because of micro-norms – the tiny, repeated moments where people watch how leaders respond to uncertainty, challenge, or bad news. Those moments accumulate into what organizational theorists call the felt sense of safety. Not the stated culture – the lived one.

In my own experience, trust rarely evaporates all at once. It frays in the micro-moments: the hesitations, the carefulness, the subtle cues that honesty carries a cost. By the time someone finally asks for a candid 1:1, the truth has often already gone underground.

That’s why, after surfacing these unspoken rules, I shift leaders quickly to examining their systems. Because trust doesn’t live in values statements. Trust lives in rituals. Every one of these structures either expands capacity… or quietly constricts it.

If you want a starting point that is small, accessible, and meaningful, run a Voice Health Index each week. It’s the same five questions, delivered through an anonymous form that your team can complete in under a minute.

What matters isn’t just the average score – it’s the delta:

  • The gap between how safe you think the environment is

  • and how safe your team actually feels

That difference is where the real leadership work lives.

Leaders almost always overestimate safety – not from ego, but because people behave differently around authority. Anonymous data cuts through that distortion. It lets you see beyond the smiles, the nods, and the carefully managed updates into the lived reality of your team.

Once you collect the data, track it over time. McKinsey’s research shows that trend lines reveal adaptive capacity long before outputs decline. Rising scores tell you conditions are improving. Falling scores warn you that fear, confusion, or uncertainty may be creeping in.

The goal isn’t surveillance – it’s awareness.

And in a low-trust environment, even asking these questions can feel fraught – like touching a bruise everyone already knows is there. But repair, while slow and sometimes uncomfortable, is always less costly than pretending nothing is wrong. What matters isn’t perfection; it’s the signal that you are willing to see the reality of your team’s experience and respond to it. The moment people genuinely sense that – the system begins to shift.

And in leadership, that distinction changes everything.

  • Are scores dipping after a launch or tight deadline?

  • Do certain subteams experience different safety climates?

  • Does safety rise after clarity sessions – or fall after leadership transitions?

But here’s the question leaders most often skip:

How will you respond to what you learn?

Because psychological safety is less about measurement and more about repair – the willingness to acknowledge tension, adjust conditions, and prove that voice leads to action. Without that follow-through, even the best diagnostic becomes just another artifact of distrust.

Before you try to scale, check the signal

Psychological safety is a prerequisite for scale. You can’t move, learn, or recover fast without it. Every study on adaptive performance – from Google’s Project Aristotle to Edmondson’s research to McKinsey’s work on high-velocity organizations – reinforces the same truth: speed without safety creates fragility, not momentum.

But unlike burnout or turnover, psychological safety rarely announces itself. It doesn’t shout. It whispers. And only the leaders who are listening – truly listening – will hear the early signal before silence becomes a liability.

Ask yourself:

Where are you mistaking quiet for calm?
Quiet teams aren’t always aligned; sometimes they’re bracing. Sometimes they’re calculating risk. Sometimes they’re protecting the system from truths it isn’t yet designed to receive.

Where might your system be signaling a deeper need for safety – and are you prepared to respond with design, not just encouragement?
Because encouragement is personal.
Design is structural.
And sustainable leadership requires both.

The research is clear: teams don’t thrive because leaders are inspirational. They thrive because the conditions allow people to contribute fully, question openly, and recover quickly. Safety isn’t the work you do after the work. It is the infrastructure that makes the work possible – the scaffolding that holds complexity, velocity, and honesty together.

So let’s build from there.
Not with platitudes, but with architecture.
Not with promises, but with practice.
Not with hope alone, but with systems that make trust the default rather than the exception.

You can design that.
And your team is already telling you where to begin – in whispers, if you’re listening.
