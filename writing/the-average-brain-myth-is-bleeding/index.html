The Average Brain Myth Is Bleeding Your Best Talent

Essay 1: Neurodiversity Series

Matt Goode7 January 202614 min read Get new pieces by email

# Neuroinclusion

For years, I sat in leadership meetings – literally in the pews, as it were – and wouldn’t say a word for an hour. My colleagues would debate, riff, build on each other’s ideas in real time. I’d nod. I’d listen. I’d stay quiet.

And then I’d go for my afternoon run.

That’s when it would hit – the flood of ideas, the connections I’d been tracking, the feedback I should have offered, the questions that mattered most. My brain needed movement and solitude to do its work. I’ve come to understand about myself that I am a slow processor and a deep thinker. The insights don’t arrive on command. They arrive when the pressure lifts.

But here’s what leaders saw: a lack of engagement. A lack of interest. Maybe even a lack of leadership potential.

The structure and pace of the meetings didn’t allow for thinking time. Often held during lunch, there was relentless pressure to get stuff done – though ironically, decisions often didn’t get made either. We’d talk in circles, rush to conclusions, then revisit the same issues two weeks later because no one had actually thought about them.

I wasn’t disengaged. I was designed differently.

My silence wasn’t absence. It was discipline. I was doing the cognitive work others skipped – the slow, careful pattern recognition that happens when you resist the pressure to speak before you’re ready.

But the system had already decided what “engagement” looked like. And it looked nothing like me.

You probably have your own version of this story. Maybe you’ve watched someone go quiet in meetings and wondered if they cared. Maybe you’ve written them off as “not leadership material” because they don’t think out loud. Or maybe you are the person who goes quiet – and you’ve learned to read your own silence as a professional liability rather than a different way of processing complexity.

That’s when I realized: we’ve built entire organizations around a neurological fiction.

What Happened When I Redesigned the Room

When I started facilitating meetings at the organizational level, I brought this understanding with me.

I began building in pauses – actual thinking time before asking participants to respond. Not vague “let’s take a moment” pauses. Structured silence: “We’re going quiet for three minutes. Get out a notepad if you need to. Walk to the window if that helps. When we come back, we’ll hear from people who rarely speak first.”

Something remarkable happened.

The pauses didn’t just help the slow processors like me. They helped everyone. The “muggles” – the quick thinkers who normally dominated the airspace – became better listeners. Instead of their brains racing ahead to their next comment, they actually heard what others were saying.

The quality of the dialogue shifted. Decisions became sharper. Follow-through improved.

Neurodiversity-friendly design wasn’t just accommodation. It was better practice, period.

But it got me thinking: if a simple structural shift could unlock that much collective intelligence, what else were we missing? It wasn’t the people who needed fixing. It was the norms and structure of the meetings – the system itself – that was the issue.

We’ve designed leadership cultures that reward speed over depth, mistake verbal processing for critical thinking, and privilege extroverted cognition while wondering why our decisions lack nuance. We call it “collaboration” when it’s often just performance. We promote people who think out loud and overlook people who think deeply.

And then we’re surprised when our leadership pipelines are narrow, our decisions are shallow, and our best thinkers burn out trying to conform to a rhythm that was never built for them.

I wasn’t the problem. The system was.

The question isn’t “How do we get quieter people to speak up?” The question is: “How do we build systems that unlock the intelligence already in the room – including the intelligence that doesn’t perform on cue?”

Because if we keep building leadership cultures that reward only one way of thinking, we’re not developing leaders. We’re selecting for sameness and calling it strength.

The Myth We Keep Building For

The “average employee” is a myth – an imaginary person who thinks in predictable patterns, communicates on cue, and thrives in standardized environments. Someone who loves back-to-back meetings, makes crisp eye contact, answers quickly in real time, and never needs a minute to process before responding.

They don’t exist. Yet our systems are still designed for them.

Hiring rubrics. Feedback models. Performance metrics. Meeting structures. Leadership assessments. Almost all of them assume a uniform way of processing information, managing attention, and demonstrating competence. And when someone doesn’t conform – when they need thinking time before speaking, or write more clearly than they talk, or can’t track a conversation while maintaining eye contact – we don’t redesign the system.

We ask the human to adapt.

To perform. To mask.

This isn’t just a moral failure. It’s a strategic one.

Because neurodiversity – ADHD, autism, dyslexia, and the broader spectrum of cognitive variance – isn’t a deficit to accommodate. It’s a design advantage we’re systematically suppressing.

The Innovation We’re Leaving on the Table

McKinsey’s research shows that companies in the top quartile for ethnic and cultural diversity outperform their peers by 36% in profitability. That’s not a rounding error – for a $100M company, that’s $36M in performance difference. But here’s what most organizations miss: that advantage doesn’t come from demographic representation alone. It comes from cognitive friction – from different brains seeing different patterns in the same data.

Teams with neurodivergent members consistently outperform homogeneous ones in pattern recognition, systems thinking, and nonlinear problem-solving. Not because neurodivergence is a superpower. Because cognitive variance creates the conditions for breakthrough thinking.

But here’s the paradox we’re living in:

We say we want creativity – but we reward conformity.

We say we value difference – but we demand cognitive disguise.

We claim we’re building inclusive cultures – but we’re still measuring contribution through a neurotypical lens.

And the cost isn’t the energy required to “accommodate” neurodivergent professionals.

The cost is the chronic cognitive load required for them to pretend to think like everyone else.

It’s the double work of not only doing your job – but doing it as if your brain processes information the same way as the person who designed the job description. It’s the executive function tax of translating your natural workflow into someone else’s preferred communication style. It’s the attention residue left over after every meeting where you spent more energy managing how you’re perceived than actually contributing what you know.

If you’ve ever watched a brilliant mind burn out in your organization, this is likely why. The system demanded they spend more energy managing perception than contributing insight. Eventually, that math stops working.

Why Leaders Can’t See It: The Two-Gear Trap

When I coach leaders through this blindspot, I almost always find the same pattern.

They operate in only two gears – Visionary and Operator.

They cast vision. They drive outcomes. They move fast. They’re deeply committed to performance, to results, to momentum. And that commitment is genuine.

But neurodivergent professionals don’t just need speed and direction.

They need Coach and Inclusive gears – leaders who pause, ask, listen, and co-design. Who create space for processing. Who understand that silence isn’t always hesitation; sometimes it’s integration.

Here’s the part that makes this so difficult: these leaders think they’re being supportive. They genuinely care about their people. They’ve read the inclusion training materials. They’ve nodded through the bias workshops.

But they’ve never learned to listen beyond their own brain type.

So when someone goes quiet in a meeting, they read it as disengagement – not as evidence that the meeting design itself excludes a certain kind of thinking.

They don’t ask: “How do you process best?”

They ask: “Why aren’t you speaking up?”

The framing reveals everything.

Because what we’re witnessing isn’t a people problem. It’s a systems problem dressed up as a performance issue.

And that’s where the heroic leadership myth does its most insidious work. Because instead of adapting systems to fit different brains, we reward the leaders who can force-fit people into existing structures. We promote the manager who “handles difficult personalities” – never questioning whether the system itself is what made those personalities seem difficult in the first place.

That’s not inclusion.

That’s endurance theater.

And it’s bleeding organizations dry of the very cognitive diversity they claim to need.

Culture, But for Whom?

Let’s connect this to the CREATE framework – because when I trace burnout and attrition back through the system, it almost always starts in the same place: Culture.

Not the culture you describe in your values deck.

The culture you design through your norms.

Because culture, at its core, is just the accumulated set of behaviors your system rewards and punishes. And when culture is built for the “average brain,” neurodivergent employees experience it as a constant cognitive guessing game.

What looks like “good culture” to some – spontaneous collaboration, open-plan offices, real-time brainstorming, speak-up-to-show-engagement norms – can feel like cognitive landmines to others.

Norms like “respond quickly to signal alignment” or “make eye contact to show you’re listening” aren’t neutral. They’re calibrated for neurotypical processing. And they don’t just exclude thoughtful voices – they actively teach those voices to stay silent.

Because if the cost of contributing is performing a neurotype you don’t have, most people will eventually stop paying it.

They’ll stop speaking up in meetings.

They’ll stop offering the pattern they noticed that no one else saw.

They’ll stop pointing out the systemic flaw everyone’s been working around.

Not because they don’t care.

Because the system has taught them that their way of caring doesn’t count.

This is what I mean when I say the CREATE model starts with Culture. You can’t build trust (Relationships), execute effectively (Execution), or adapt intelligently (Agility) if your foundational norms are systematically filtering out cognitive difference.

And here’s the part that makes this so urgent:

Once that filtering becomes normalized, it becomes invisible.

You stop seeing it as exclusion. You see it as “culture fit.”

You stop recognizing it as a design flaw. You see it as a “communication issue.”

And the brilliance you’re losing? It doesn’t announce itself on the way out. It just quietly stops showing up.

What Neuroinclusive Design Actually Looks Like

So what does repair look like?

Let’s map it through CREATE – my framework for organizational systems design. CREATE stands for Culture, Relationships, Execution, Agility, Teaching, and Ethics – the six domains that determine whether your systems unlock or constrain human potential. For neurodivergent professionals, the repair work starts with Culture but touches every domain.

Here’s what that looks like in practice:

Culture – Stop designing for the average brain. Start by asking: when you hire for “culture fit,” are you actually screening for “brain type fit”? Because if your norms reward one way of processing information, you’re not building culture – you’re building conformity. Neuroinclusive culture doesn’t mean one more slide in the inclusion deck. It means interrogating what your systems are actually rewarding. It means recognizing that “speak up to show engagement” isn’t a neutral expectation – it’s a cognitive tax on anyone who thinks before they talk.

Relationships – Build trust through cognitive flexibility, not cognitive conformity. In 1:1s, ask “How do you process best?” – not “Why didn’t you contribute in the meeting?” Create pathways for contribution that don’t all require real-time verbal performance. Separate thinking time from speaking time. Let people contribute asynchronously. We don’t call adjustable desks an “accommodation” for tall people. We call them good design. The same principle applies here.

Execution – Redesign decision-making protocols. Not everyone thinks out loud. Not everyone needs a whiteboard session to see the system. Create multiple entry points for input: written briefs before meetings, post-meeting reflection windows, and decision documents that allow for async feedback. The goal isn’t to slow down – it’s to stop leaving insight on the table because you’re only listening in one cognitive register.

This isn’t about lowering standards.

It’s about raising the ceiling by removing artificial cognitive barriers.

Because here’s what most leaders miss: when you design for cognitive diversity, you don’t just help neurodivergent employees. You help everyone. Clarity helps everyone. Multiple modes of contribution help everyone. Separating thinking from performing helps everyone.

You’re not accommodating differences.

You’re designing better systems.

The Economic Case No One’s Making

Let’s be direct about the numbers – because this isn’t just a moral argument.

73% of neurodivergent professionals report masking at work daily. That’s not a soft stat. That’s a cognitive load tax that shows up in every performance metric you’re already tracking – if you know how to read them.

Neurodivergent employees leave jobs at twice the rate of their neurotypical peers. Not because they can’t do the work. Because the sustained effort of pretending burns them out faster than the work itself.

Innovation cycle times slow when teams lack cognitive diversity – because everyone’s seeing the same problems through the same lens, which means you’re solving for speed but missing the solutions that require a different way of seeing.

If you’ve watched someone brilliant flame out in your organization – someone whose departure genuinely surprised you because “they were doing so well” – this is probably why.

The performance metrics looked fine.

But the energetic math didn’t.

And by the time you saw the exhaustion, they’d already been running on fumes for months.

Three Diagnostic Questions

Before we close, three questions worth sitting with – not as rhetorical provocations, but as genuine leadership diagnostics:

1. When you hire for “culture fit,” are you actually hiring for “brain type fit”?

Look at your last five hires. What communication style do they share? What processing speed? What mode of presence? If they all think the same way, you haven’t built diversity – you’ve built an echo chamber with different résumés.

2. How much cognitive energy are your best people spending pretending to be someone else – and what could they create if they didn’t have to?

This is the invisible tax. The one that doesn’t show up in your productivity dashboards but shows up in your retention data, your innovation pipeline, and the ideas that never get voiced because the effort of translation felt too high.

3. If your systems only work for one kind of brain, how many kinds of problems can you actually solve?

Cognitive homogeneity creates strategic fragility. You can execute efficiently within known parameters – but you can’t see the disruption coming from outside your frame. That’s not a diversity problem. It’s a risk-management problem.

There’s no average brain.

Just average systems – and leaders brave enough to redesign them.

The question isn’t whether your best people can adapt.

It’s whether your systems are designed to deserve them.

Because the cost of cognitive conformity isn’t just human.

It’s strategic.

And the organizations that figure this out first won’t just be more inclusive.

They’ll be more resilient, more innovative, and more capable of solving the problems no one else can see yet.

Meeting Design Self-Assessment

Rate your agreement with each statement on a scale of 1-5: (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree)

1. Our meetings include structured thinking time before asking people to contribute.

  • 1 = Never / 2 = Rarely / 3 = Sometimes / 4 = Usually / 5 = Always

2. People on my team can contribute meaningfully without speaking in real-time during meetings.

  • 1 = Not possible / 2 = Rarely / 3 = Sometimes / 4 = Usually / 5 = Always

3. When someone is quiet in a meeting, I assume they’re processing thoughtfully rather than disengaged.

  • 1 = Never / 2 = Rarely / 3 = Sometimes / 4 = Usually / 5 = Always

4. Our meeting culture values depth of contribution over speed of response.

  • 1 = Speed strongly valued / 2 = Speed valued / 3 = Both equally / 4 = Depth valued / 5 = Depth strongly valued

5. I can clearly see how our current meeting design might exclude certain ways of thinking.

  • 1 = Not at all / 2 = Slightly / 3 = Somewhat / 4 = Clearly / 5 = Very clearly

Scoring Guide:

20-25 points: Your meeting design likely supports cognitive diversity well. Consider: What specific practices create this environment? How can you document and scale them?

15-19 points: You’re creating some space for different processing styles, but there’s room for more intentional design. Consider: Which meetings could benefit most from structured thinking pauses? Who on your team processes best in writing?

10-14 points: Your meetings may be inadvertently excluding thoughtful contributors. Consider: What would happen if you built 3-5 minutes of silence into your next strategic discussion? Who might contribute differently if given that space?

5-9 points: Your meeting culture is likely rewarding speed over depth and verbal processing over careful thinking. Consider: When was the last time your “quietest” team member offered an insight that changed your thinking? If you can’t remember, that’s your signal.

Next Steps Based on Your Score:

If you scored 15+:

  • Document what’s working in your meeting design

  • Identify which practices could be shared with other leaders

  • Consider: Are there other systems (hiring, feedback, decision-making) where similar principles could apply?

If you scored 10-14:

  • Choose one recurring meeting to redesign with structured thinking time

  • Ask three people on your team: “How do you process best?” and “What would help you contribute more effectively?”

  • Experiment with providing meeting materials 24 hours in advance

If you scored below 10:

  • Start with your next important meeting: Build in one 3-minute thinking pause before asking for input

  • After the meeting, notice: Who contributed that doesn’t usually speak first? What quality of thinking emerged?

  • Ask yourself: “Am I measuring engagement by participation performance, or by quality of contribution?”

Regardless of score: Read Essay 2 to learn how to identify the cognitive diversity that’s already in your team – and how to create the conditions for those different ways of thinking to strengthen your decisions, not slow them down.

The business case is clear.

Organizations that get neuroinclusive leadership right reduce attrition, catch blind spots earlier, and build systems that don’t depend on heroic leadership.
