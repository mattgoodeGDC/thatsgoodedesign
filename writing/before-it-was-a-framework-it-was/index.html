Before It Was a Framework, It Was a Fight

How post-it notes in a federal crisis room and a grading rubric for middle schoolers led to the same uncomfortable truth about leadership

Matt Goode13 February 202618 min read Get new pieces by email

# CREATE

I built the same tool twice. Well, maybe three times. Different lifetimes, different organizations, different stakes. I didn’t realize it until much later.

I built the same tool three times. Different lifetimes, different organizations, different stakes. I didn’t realize it until much later.

The first time, I was a teacher trying to solve a grading problem. The second time, I was a leadership coach trying to prevent a team from tearing itself apart. The third time, I was starting my own consulting practice and watching the exact same dysfunction play out in every client organization I walked into. All three times, the root cause was the same: people operating without a shared language for how decisions get made. And all three times, the fix was the same. Not more authority. Not better people. A common framework that made the invisible visible.

That framework eventually became the 6C Decision Compass, the navigation system at the heart of CLUTCH: How Leaders Build Systems That Run Without Them. But it didn’t start as a chapter in a book. It started with post-it notes and an argument about middle schoolers.

The 5C Rubric: Solving for Coherence

At McDonogh School, where I taught for 17 years, we launched the Middle School Academy – an immersive, experiential program that pulled students out of the regular curriculum for a concentrated period of exploration. Full credit to Jennifer Smith and Denise Wolf, two fifth-grade teachers who were the genesis of this idea over lunchtime conversations, who dared to imagine what if school were different. Think of Academy as a mid-semester term where middle schoolers could take a deep dive into courses spanning the humanities, science, mathematics, and the arts. Students from multiple grade levels worked side by side. Teachers designed courses from scratch. At the end, everyone presented their learning in a public showcase.

It was ambitious. It was also, predictably, controversial.

The objections were real and came from every direction. Eighth graders were working toward high school placements; any time in Academy was time away from “curriculum”. In a zero-sum curricular world, every hour given to an experimental program was an hour taken from something established. And here was the question that almost killed the whole thing: with 24 wildly different courses – from robotics to poetry to field ecology – how do you ensure a common level of rigor? What does excellence look like when one student is building a bridge, and another is writing a one-act play?

A small committee of teachers, myself included, was tasked with answering that question. The answer wasn’t standardized content. It couldn’t be. The whole point of Academy was diversity of experience. So instead, we built a universal rubric around five dimensions that transcended subject matter.

I called them the 5Cs: Collaboration, Critical Thinking, Communication, Commitment, and Character.

Each C had its own set of observable behaviors. Collaboration wasn’t just “works with others” – it meant compromising with peers, contributing meaningfully to the team, and listening with respect. Critical Thinking meant generating new ideas, making connections, and demonstrating effective problem-solving. Communication required active listening, relevant questioning, and clear articulation. Commitment was about initiative, self-direction, and time management. Character mapped to the school’s own Compass values: Respect, Kindness, Honesty, and Responsibility.

The measures were deliberately simple. Rarely. Sometimes. Often. Consistently. No percentages. No curves. Just an honest observation of whether a student was actually demonstrating the behavior. And importantly, no mastery. How do you master “communication”?

It worked. Not because the rubric was brilliant (if I may say so myself – they’re still using it even though I left years ago), but because it gave 24 different teachers, teaching 24 different things, a shared vocabulary for what mattered. A robotics teacher and a ceramics teacher could both look at a student and say, “She’s consistently strong in Critical Thinking but only sometimes demonstrates Commitment,” and mean the same thing. The rubric made evaluation transparent, and transparency made rigor possible across radical diversity.

I filed that lesson away. I didn’t know I’d need it again.

The Room Where It Happened

Years later, I was working at USAID’s Bureau for Humanitarian Assistance, embedded with TPQ – the Office of Technical Program Quality. If you’ve never heard of TPQ, that’s by design. These were the quiet specialists, the people the agency called when the question was too technical for generalists. Infant nutrition. Specialists in a particular kind of volcanic mudslide you only see in Indonesia. Water and sanitation in Afghan displacement camps. Every person in that office was a world authority in their field.

They were also collectively a decision-making disaster.

Not because they lacked intelligence. The opposite. When you fill a room with people who are each the foremost expert in their domain, you create a particular kind of dysfunction. Everyone has an opinion. Everyone’s opinion, within their lane, is probably correct. And everyone’s instinct – born from genuine expertise and genuine care – is to weigh in on everything, even decisions outside their specialization. The motivation is almost always good. These are people who entered public service because they believe in the work. A little ego, or realistically a lot, to be sure. But mostly it’s the compulsion of someone who knows what right looks like and can’t stand watching it go wrong.

The structural problem made everything worse. TPQ experts were frequently deployed to Disaster Assistance Response Teams (DARTs) and pulled from headquarters for weeks or months. Decisions were sometimes made without key people present. When those experts returned, the questions were immediate and sharp. How was that decision made? Who made it? Under what authority? By what criteria?

The teams had no framework for answering those questions. No shared language. No documented process.

The result was predictable. Trust eroded. Transparency became suspect. Psychological safety – the foundation of any high-performing team – began to crack. People didn’t feel informed. People didn’t feel included. People started questioning motives rather than processes, because when the process is invisible, the only thing left to question is intent.

And in this context, the stakes weren’t quarterly targets or product launches. Lives were involved. Real ones. The programming these teams advised on determined whether displaced populations received adequate nutrition, whether health responses reached the right communities, and whether emergency funds were kept away from terrorist organizations (Looking at you DOGE, with your claims of fraud, and waste - show me the indictments, please). Decision ambiguity wasn’t an organizational annoyance. It was a moral failure.

I was working with a particular team on TPQ where all of these tensions had converged. Leadership knew something was wrong. The team members knew something was wrong. Everyone wanted clarity, but nobody had a mechanism for creating it. So we built one.

Post-It Notes and Pattern Recognition

I designed what turned out to be a multi-day workshop. The first step was archaeological: go back through two years of decisions – every single one, big and small – and write each one on a post-it note. As simplified as possible. One decision per note. I wish I had a picture of that wall.

Then we stepped back.

This is the part that’s hard to teach in a book but easy to see in a room. When you put two years of decisions on a wall, patterns emerge. Not immediately. At first, it looks like chaos – a mosaic of yellow, green, and blue squares with no organizing principle. But if you’re patient, and you have someone in the room with a talent for pattern recognition and the 30,000-foot perspective to use it, the clusters start to form.

We moved post-it notes. We rearranged. We argued about where things belonged. And eventually, the decisions sorted themselves into four columns based on the nature of the decision: USG/Agency/BHA decisions (matters determined above the team’s authority), Operational Decisions (advising on existing or potential programming), Routine Execution (the daily work), and Crisis Response (emergencies requiring immediate action).

That first column was the most painful to populate. These were decisions where, regardless of your expertise, regardless of what you could see on the ground, the call had been made at a level so far above you that your input wasn’t just unwelcome – it was irrelevant. Gaza was a vivid example. The team, in fact, the whole agency, could see what was happening. They had people on the ground, in camps. They possessed the technical knowledge to respond effectively. But policy constraints, the Biden administration’s stance, and the guidance flowing down from leadership meant the full weight of their expertise could not be brought to bear. The Rohingya crisis carried the same anguish. The team knew what was right; yet people were dying. They knew what was feasible. But something was better than nothing, and nothing was what the decision architecture sometimes produced.

Naming those constraints didn’t make them less painful. But it made them legible. And legibility is the first step toward trust.

Seven Mechanisms

I went home after the first day and couldn’t stop thinking about the columns. Categories of decisions were useful, but categories alone don’t tell you how to decide. The team needed mechanisms, not just labels.

Not sure that I slept much that night, my mind full a-buzz, but I came back the next day with seven.

  • Consultation. The decision falls outside the team’s scope, but there may be latitude in the execution details. Apply other decision-making models. Document the results and rationale.

  • Command. The team lead decides, with or without input. Clear. Fast. Sometimes necessary.

  • Choice. Outcomes are determined by ranked-choice voting among present members with relevant subject-matter expertise. Results and rationale documented.

  • Cumulative. Outcomes are determined by majority vote.

  • Collaborative. Open discussion where multiple contributors share ideas and insights. The final decision emerges from collective input, though it may still be guided by a designated leader.

  • Consensus. A decision reaches consensus when at least 80% of the quorum supports it. The minority view is acknowledged and documented. The decision moves forward. This was crucial: consensus didn’t mean unanimity. It meant sufficient alignment to act without leaving the dissenting 20% feeling erased.

  • Core Expertise. Decisions are delegated to the team member whose narrow technical specialization makes them the most qualified person in the room – possibly the most qualified person in the world – to make that call.

Seven mechanisms. Each is suited to different conditions. The key insight wasn’t that any single mechanism was superior. It was that the team needed to name which one they were using before they used it.

The Grid

But mechanisms alone still weren’t enough. Seven ways to decide are useful. Knowing which way to use when is the actual problem.

The breakthrough came when we added a second axis. The Y axis captured the type of decision: national security and U.S. foreign policy, operational programming, routine execution, and crisis response. The X axis captured scope – who the decision affected: the whole agency, the team, a sub-team, or an empowered individual.

Then we mapped the seven mechanisms onto the intersections.

Some squares had only one option, and there was nothing to discuss. Where national security or agency-wide foreign policy met the broadest scope, the answer was Command. Full stop. In many of those cases, even the team leaders had been told what they were going to do. There was no room for interpretation, let alone debate.

But other squares opened up. Operational decisions affecting the whole team’s programming? Consensus was the only option. Which immediately surfaced a productive argument: What is consensus? Is it 80 percent agreement? 75 percent? Does the dissenting minority get their concerns documented? Does documentation actually matter if the decision moves forward anyway? Every team I worked with eventually developed its own definition, and that was fine. The point wasn’t uniformity across teams. The point was that within each team, the threshold was explicit, agreed upon in advance, and applied consistently.

The richest squares were the ones with multiple valid options. Routine operational matters affecting only a sub-team could be handled through Choice, Collaborative, or Consensus depending on time constraints, budget pressures, or other external factors. The grid didn’t prescribe a single answer. It prescribed a menu – a bounded set of legitimate mechanisms the team had already vetted and agreed to.

That was the whole point. Every decision, before it was made, got sorted into a square on the grid. And the options available in that square had been predetermined. The team didn’t have to relitigate the process every time a new decision showed up.

There is, I should note, a healthy irony in all of this. Establishing the criteria for each square required its own decision-making process – the very one we were building. How do you decide how to decide before you’ve decided how to decide? Every threshold we set, every mechanism we assigned to a square, was itself a decision that needed a mechanism. And the mechanism we were reaching for didn’t exist yet because we were still constructing it.

What made it work – and I say this without a touch of ego – is that the team trusted me and the process. As an external coach, I wasn’t a stakeholder in any of their technical debates. I didn’t have a lane to protect or a portfolio to defend. That meant I could serve as an independent arbiter when the conversation got stuck, when two equally valid perspectives created a logjam, when the consensus threshold for defining the consensus threshold threatened to become its own infinite loop. I could break ties without anyone feeling overruled, because I had no stake in the outcome beyond the system itself working.

That role – the trusted outsider who holds the process while the team holds the content – turned out to be essential. And it taught me something I later built into CLUTCH – sometimes the most important leadership move is making sure someone in the room has no agenda except clarity.

No need to convene the entire team for an emergency when subject-matter expertise was really the only voice needed. No need to agonize over whether the team lead had overstepped when the grid already specified that routine execution decisions at the sub-team level were hers to make.

Were there edge cases? Of course. Occasionally, a decision didn’t fit neatly into any square and required some wrangling. But the grid was loose enough to cover 99.9 percent of situations, and the rare exceptions actually reinforced the system: the team could point to the grid, acknowledge that this particular situation didn’t map cleanly, and have a brief, focused conversation about which square came closest. Even the exceptions used the shared language.

Buy-In Is the Whole Point

Here’s what made the grid work in practice, and what separates it from every decision-making framework that looks elegant on a slide deck and dies on contact with reality: buy-in.

Every person on the team had participated in building the grid. They had sorted the post-it notes. They had argued about which mechanism belonged in which square. They had debated the consensus threshold. They had agreed, before any specific decision was on the table, on the rules that would govern all future decisions.

That meant when a team member returned from a six-week DART deployment and asked, “How was that decision made while I was gone?” the answer wasn’t defensive. It was structural. A team leader could say: “It was routine execution at the sub-team level. I solicited input from the people in the room, but per the grid, this was ultimately my call.” And the returning expert could review the grid, confirm the process matched the agreed framework, and move on – not because they necessarily would have made the same decision, but because they trusted the process by which it was made.

That distinction matters enormously. Trust in outcomes is fragile; you only trust the decision if you agree with it. Trust in process is durable; you trust the decision because you trust the system that produced it, even when you might have chosen differently.

Anyone in the room knew in advance that while a team lead might ask for advice, certain decisions would ultimately be hers. Anyone returning from the field could read the meeting minutes, check the decision log, see which square the decision fell into, and verify that the documented mechanism matched the agreed-upon grid. The transparency wasn’t a retroactive justification. It was the architecture itself.

And eventually, it became shorthand. Once the whole team had bought into the system, nobody needed to narrate the logic or defend the process. You could just say: “Operational decision, affecting the whole team. We used Collaborative.” Nine words. Everyone knew what that meant: the boundaries, who had input, and how the call was made. No lengthy justification. No defensive posture. Just a coordinate on a map that everyone had helped draw.

As an artifact of that original work, I’ve included a copy of the framework above. I probably broke a security rule or two getting it out of the building; I emailed it to my personal account so I could think about it on the train during my commute, turn it over in my head at home, sketch refinements at the kitchen table. It wasn’t launch codes. It was a grid on a spreadsheet. But if anyone from the USAID security office is reading this, I’d like to think the statute of limitations has expired. And after what DOGE allegedly did with my personally identifiable information, I’d say we’re even.

And here’s the part that surprised me most: the grid didn’t reduce people’s sense of influence. It increased it. When decision rights are ambiguous, people either overreach – weighing in on everything, hoping something sticks – or withdraw, assuming their input doesn’t matter. The grid eliminated both failure modes. People knew exactly where their voice carried weight, which meant they invested that voice more fully in the squares where it mattered.

The Same Problem, Years Apart

I didn’t see the connection until much later. The 5C rubric and the 7C grid – and its eventual metamorphosis into the 6C Decision Compass – solved the same problem. A group of diverse specialists, each excellent in their own domain, needed a shared language to evaluate something that lacked an obvious common standard. For middle school teachers, the question was: What does rigor look like across 24 different courses? For TPQ experts: What does legitimate decision-making look like across dozens of technical domains? And for the business owners and team leaders I work with now, the question is quieter but no less urgent: How do I stop being the person every decision has to flow through before anything moves?

In all three cases, the answer was the same. You don’t standardize the content. You standardize the criteria. You give people a common vocabulary for the dimensions that matter, and then you trust them to apply it within their context.

The rubric didn’t tell the robotics teacher how to teach robotics. It told her what to look for when she watched her students work. The grid didn’t tell the nutrition expert how to advise on programming. It told the team how to make the decision about that programming – who should be involved and how much authority was being transferred.

Structure creates clarity. Clarity creates trust. Trust creates speed. That sequence held true in a middle school and in a federal agency responding to global humanitarian crises. The scale was different. The stakes were different. The sequence was the same.

From Government Tool to Leadership System

The grid worked. It worked so well that I started seeing the same dysfunction everywhere – not just in government but in startups, school leadership teams, nonprofit boards, and corporate departments. The symptoms were always the same: smart people, good intentions, eroding trust, and a leader slowly drowning under the weight of every decision routing back to them.

But the USAID grid was bespoke. One axis was calibrated for a federal agency neck-deep in technical expertise and geared for fast emergency response – two things that did not often go together well without friction. The decision types, the scope categories, and even the consensus thresholds were tuned for a very specific organizational context. You couldn’t hand that grid to a SaaS founder or a school principal and expect it to translate.

So the mechanisms stayed, though not all seven. “Cumulative” – majority vote – folded into Consensus; it was really just consensus with a lower threshold, not a distinct mode of thinking. “Consultation” became “Consultative,” a subtle but important shift from noun to adjective, emphasizing a mode of operating rather than a one-time event. And “Choice” evolved most dramatically: at USAID, it meant ranked-choice voting among present experts, a formal mechanism for a specific context. In the final framework, Choice became something broader and more powerful: full delegation, complete autonomy, the leader stepping back entirely. Same word. Different animal. Seven became six: Command, Consultative, Collaborative, Consensus, Core Expert, and Choice.

What needed replacing was the government’s unique axis. That answer came from Michael Hyatt’s work on delegation levels (shout out to Zachary Cochran), and it clicked immediately. Hyatt’s spectrum – from Level 1 (”Wait: do exactly as I say”) through Level 5 (”Own Completely: this is yours, start to finish”) – did something the USAID axis couldn’t: it described the degree of authority transfer in language that was universal. It didn’t matter whether you were running a humanitarian response team or a marketing department. The question “How much of this decision am I actually handing over?” applied everywhere.

When I overlaid the six decision modes with Hyatt’s five levels, the grid transformed. Instead of a government-specific diagnostic, it became a 6×5 matrix with 30 possible coordinates – each a precise description of how a decision will be made and how much ownership will be transferred. The principle was unchanged from that TPQ workshop: before any decision is made, name the coordinate. But now the coordinate system was portable.

“This is Consultative, Level 3. I want your recommendation, and I’ll make the final call.”

Twelve words that prevent twelve weeks of confusion. And twelve words that work whether you’re advising on infant nutrition programming in East Africa or launching a product feature in Austin.

The Lesson Underneath the Lesson

Frameworks don’t come from theory. They come from problems that won’t go away until someone builds a structure around them.

The 5Cs arose from a debate over whether experimental education could be rigorous. The 6Cs arose from a dispute over whether technical experts could trust each other’s judgment when some were 8,000 miles away during the decision. Both fights were really about the same thing: what happens when smart, caring people lack a shared way to evaluate what matters.

The answer, both times, was to make the criteria visible. Not to impose a single right answer, but to give people a common language for the dimensions that define quality – or in the case of decisions, legitimacy.

If you lead a team and you’ve ever felt the friction of people questioning decisions that seemed perfectly reasonable to you, the problem probably isn’t the decision. It’s the absence of a shared framework for understanding how that decision was made, by whom, and under what authority. Make that visible, and trust doesn’t just recover. It compounds.

That’s what the grid does. Not perfectly. Not in every situation. But consistently enough that a room full of the world’s foremost experts – people who had every reason to believe their individual judgment was sufficient – looked at a wall of post-it notes and agreed: we need this.

If they needed it, your team probably does too.
