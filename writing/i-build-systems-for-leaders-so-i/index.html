I build systems for leaders. So I drew one up for myself.

Two loops, thirty projects and one person turning the crank

Matt Goode28 September 20265 min read Get new pieces by email

# Everything runs through me

For about twenty years I’ve told leaders some version of the same thing. If the work only runs when you’re in the room, you haven’t built a system. You’ve built a dependency with your name on it. I wrote a whole book about it. CLUTCH argues that leaders should build systems that run without them.

Since I was RIF’ed from USAID and unexpectedly started a business, I have been building it from scratch as a solopreneur. This August, I took a hard look at my own back office.

Goode Design & Consulting, which helps technical founders scale their businesses. A schools practice, because schools are still my people. Leadership coaching. Several books under two names, each with its own marketing. Two websites, one for the author and one for the business. Several social media accounts. LinkedIn. This Substack. I ran it all on memory and a paper to-do list.

That is the exact setup I spend my working week talking clients out of.

So I drew one up for myself.

It has a name, because everything I build eventually does – it’s the teacher in me.

GOs, short for Goode OS, said “goes.” Two loops sit side by side. The gold one runs the work. The dark one, DALE, brings in the work. Under both run two rails: Todoist, which carries the tasks, and Drive, which carries the story. Where the loops touch, one small circle says Me. That circle is the most honest part of the drawing.

What follows is what I learned building it, and I think most of it travels to anyone running a practice of one or a few.

The parts list

GOs runs on tools most people in my line of work already pay for. Todoist for tasks, Google Drive for documents, and a set of AI workspaces, each holding one kind of work. I use AI to organize and automate the back office, never for my writing or anything creative. The essays, the books, and the coaching are mine.

One of those workspaces is the hub. It reads everything, writes me a morning brief, and runs a sweep every Monday. The rest are spokes: the books, the client practice, the schools work, DALE for revenue, and a few personal things that run the same way.

The spokes stay in their own lanes. That sounds like a flaw. It turned out to be the design.

Two loops

The gold loop is the operating loop. Each morning the hub reads Todoist and Drive and tells me what the day holds. I pick a task. The task says which lane it belongs to, so I work it there. When the job is done, it gets handed over: the task is closed, any follow-ups go into Todoist, and a short note goes into Drive saying what was done and decided. Tomorrow the hub reads the note. Nobody copies and pastes anything, least of all me.

The dark loop is the revenue loop. Someone reads something I wrote, takes a short leadership quiz, and lands in a CRM I built by hand: first in Sheets, with some JavaScript to automate the dull parts, and now in Airtable. DALE has two halves. The builder decides the structure: what I offer, who it’s for, how the CRM is laid out. The runner works inside that structure: reviewing the pipeline and scoring against a running document of my ideal client personas. Once a month, the runner writes field notes for the builder. What people pushed back on. Where the gaps were. Which scores matched what actually happened. The builder changes things. The runner doesn’t.

The same loop runs in a second, quieter mode for finding early readers for the fiction. No scoring there. Nobody reading a novel should be scored. Right now it’s building the advance reader list for Wrath.

Where the two loops touch, there’s me. I decide. That’s the whole job description now, and it’s a smaller job than the one I had.

Four rules that travel

You don’t need my tools for any of these. Most of them work on paper. I still love my Moleskine to-do list – something about filling in those boxes really satisfies me!

  • One owner per task. Every task in GOs carries a permanent code, like CLUTCH-3, and a label naming the lane it belongs to. If two things could own a task, nothing does. The code also lets me refer to any job by name, which matters more than I expected.

  • The tools carry state. People carry decisions. Todoist knows whether a thing is done. Drive knows the story of how it got done. I’m no longer the place where facts live. Anything that needs me comes back as a numbered list, and I answer by number. “Yes to 1, no to 2, 3 can wait” is a morning’s worth of management.

  • Separate whoever builds the system from whoever runs it. When the same hands build and run, the structure drifts every time a Tuesday gets hard. Split the roles, even if you play both on different days.

  • Learn monthly, against real results. The field notes are the only reason DALE gets better, not just busier. A score nobody checks against what happened is a guess with decimals.

What still creaks

It’s a few weeks old, and it isn’t finished.

Each part of the work runs on its own and learns about the others only from what gets written down. That keeps things clean, and it means the handover notes carry the whole load. When one is lazy, the next morning is too. Some of it still needs me at the keyboard, doing by hand what should run by itself. And I still turn the crank. The system doesn’t decide what matters this week. It clears away everything except that decision, which is the point, but the crank does not turn itself.

A question for you

Most coaches and consultants I know run their practice the way I ran mine: well, from memory, and slightly too late at night. The work is good. The back office is a person.

So I’m thinking about turning this into something other coaches can use. Not my exact setup, which is built around my odd mix of work. Yours, built on the same rules and set up around your practice, that you own and run. If it works for enough people, I’ll probably charge for it. But mostly I want other coaches to get their evenings back.

I don’t have anything to sell you yet. I have a drawing, a system that works for one person, and a question: would you want one?

If you run a solo or small practice and you’d want a system like this for your own work, raise your hand. Six questions, two minutes. Your answers will shape what I build, and whether I build it at all. I read every answer, and I’ll only follow up if you say I can.
