Seventy-Four Years Later

Matt Goode24 April 20265 min read Get new pieces by email

# Off the clock

Along the old Battle Road that runs between Lexington and Concord, there is a small stone marker almost hidden in the hostas. You can drive past it for years without noticing. I did. Someone had not.

The stone marks the graves of British Regulars who died on April 19, 1775.

The inscription, a stanza from a poem by James Russell Lowell, reads:

They came three thousand miles, and died, To keep the past upon its throne; Unheard, beyond the ocean tide, Their English mother made her moan.

Consider what the stanza refuses to do. It does not call these men invaders. It does not call them oppressors. It does not gloat. It names what they left behind: a mother, an ocean, a country that would never know where they fell.

Nobody composed these inscriptions in 1775. Lowell wrote the poem in 1849, after visiting one of these roadside graves with Nathaniel Hawthorne. Seventy-four years after the battle. The stones were placed over the decades that followed, some as late as 1910. British soldiers are buried at sites scattered along the road their army took that day, and nearly all of them have been marked, tended, and cared for by towns that had every reason to forget them.

A single generous moment is a mood. A century and a half of sustained attention is an architecture.

Here is the thing nobody in 1775 specified.

The Sons of Liberty designed the militia. They designed the muster. They designed the signals, the redundancy, the Committees of Correspondence, and the parallel routes. They designed every piece of the receiving culture that held when Revere was captured, and Prescott finished the ride. They designed all of it patiently, for years, in rooms whose minutes survive.

None of them designed a culture that would tend the graves of the men who came to kill them.

That was not in the architecture. It is what the architecture produced.

This week, somebody left two small wooden crosses with paper poppies and a Union Jack on a stone marking men whose names are not even carved on it. None of that was specified. All of it is what the culture the Sons of Liberty built made possible, without instruction, long after the architects were gone.

The deepest evidence of what your culture has actually built is what it produces that nobody specified. Not the handoffs you designed. Not the onboarding deck. Not the all-hands slides. What the culture does, at scale, in the long tail, for people who cannot repay the attention, when no one is watching and no one is keeping score.

Most companies that call themselves families have built the opposite of Battle Road.

They have built a culture whose downstream output is that the moment someone leaves – for a competitor, for a new sector, for a different life, for any reason at all – they become persona non grata. Slack access revoked Friday afternoon. The farewell lunch is the last time the name is spoken. A culture does not announce this. It just produces it, for every person who exits. Every time. The reason does not matter. The architecture handles the rest.

That is the downstream. That is what was actually built.

The word family was the marketing. The erasure is the architecture.

I left a company once that called itself a family. I still have occasion to be in the building. When management sees me coming, they put their heads down, change direction, find a reason to move away. My former teammates, seeing me in the same room, walk over for handshakes and slaps on the back.

Two responses to the same arrival. Only one of them was trained.

I kinda wanna send them a copy of CLUTCH when it drops, lol.

The stone is still there.

Notice what the receiving culture at Battle Road produced that nobody in 1775 asked for. Not the muster. Not the Minuteman companies. Not the victory. A small stone, tended for more than a century, for men whose names are not carved on it, by the same towns that mustered the minutemen who killed them.

The Sons of Liberty did not live to see this. The towns that placed the markers had no relationship with the men buried beneath them. The poet who wrote the stanza had been born forty-four years after the war. Every person in the chain was acting without supervision and without credit, in quiet repetition, for reasons that were never written down.

That is the test.

Not whether your founding documents are generous. Not whether your values doc reads well. The test is what your culture produces, unprompted and uncredited, for the people it no longer needs.

A Friday afternoon of revoked access.

Or a century and a half of quiet decisions.

Both are what the architecture built. Only one of them is what anyone would admit to designing.

The question is what your organization is producing, right now, that you did not specify.

Would you be proud of it if it were carved in stone seventy-four years from now?
