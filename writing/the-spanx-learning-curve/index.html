The Spanx Learning Curve

Matt Goode15 July 20263 min read Get new pieces by email

# My role is changing

You hand a stretch project to someone with real promise. They’re a level below the work, but they’re ready to grow into it – that’s the point. Two weeks in, they hit the first real stumble. A deliverable comes back rough. A call they made needs to be walked back.

Everything turns on how you read that stumble. Most leaders read it as evidence – proof the person wasn’t ready – and quietly pull the work back to do it themselves. They think they’re being responsible. They’re actually ending the development right where it was supposed to start.

The first stumble is data, not a verdict

When you hand someone work that stretches them, a stumble isn’t a failure. It’s the curve. Skill doesn’t arrive on day one; it’s built through exactly the rough drafts and wrong turns you’re tempted to rescue them from. The problem is almost never that the person can’t do it. It’s that you didn’t name the curve out loud – to them or to yourself – so the first wobble landed as failure instead of as the expected first rep.

This is one of the eight elements of DELEGATE, and the one leaders skip most under pressure: Expect the Process. Say it at the handoff: “This will be bumpy at first. The first version won’t be right, and that’s not a problem – it’s the plan. Here’s where I’ll be when you hit the wall.” Name the curve, and a stumble becomes a checkpoint. Skip it, and the same stumble becomes a verdict.

What pulling it back teaches

The cost of grabbing the work back isn’t just the hours you spend redoing it. It’s the lesson you teach. Pull a stretch project back at the first wobble and the person learns, fast, not to stretch – because stretching got the work taken away and their competence quietly questioned. So next time they play it safe, hand you the hard parts, and stop reaching. You’ve trained the exact passivity you were frustrated by, and the work boomerangs permanently back to your desk.

Caption: Name the learning curve, or the first stumble reads as failure.

Sara Blakely expected the curve

Sara Blakely built Spanx into a billion-dollar company with no business training and no background in fashion. She didn’t know how to write a patent, so she read a textbook and drafted one herself. She didn’t know how to manufacture hosiery, so she cold-called mills until one said yes. By any conventional measure, she was unqualified at the start of nearly everything she did – and she treated each stumble as tuition, not as a sign to stop.

That’s the posture to extend to the people you’re developing: not the expectation that they arrive expert, but the expectation that they’ll learn in public, wobble early, and get good through the reps. Your job isn’t to protect them from the curve. It’s to name it, stay close while they climb it, and resist the urge to rescue them off it. The stumble you let them work through this quarter is the reason you can hand them something bigger next quarter – and the reason it won’t come back to you.

Worth sitting with this week:

  • Where did you pull a stretch project back at the first stumble – and what did that teach the person?

  • On your next handoff, what would it sound like to name the learning curve out loud, up front?

  • Whose growth are you cutting short by mistaking the curve for a verdict?
