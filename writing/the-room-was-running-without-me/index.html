The Room Was Running Without Me

A lesson about lessons.

Matt Goode28 March 20264 min read Get new pieces by email

# Everything runs through me# DELEGATE

The room was running without me.

I was near the door, watching a student lead a full-class debrief on a collaborative persuasive essay three of his peers had written on whether Andrew Jackson’s removal of the Cherokee Nation was justified. Not reading from it. Interrogating it. He was asking follow-up questions, pushing back on weak claims, redirecting the conversation when someone conflated government policy with inevitable history. The other students were tracking, responding, and building on each other’s arguments. I had said maybe four words in the last twenty minutes. The accountability structure I had spent weeks and months (this was probably April) building was doing exactly the work I would have otherwise done myself.

Seventh grade. American history. I didn’t have a name for it yet. I knew I had designed something.

Here is what nearly 20 years in that classroom actually taught me: the word “leadership” never appeared in my job description, and I was doing almost nothing else. Not teaching content. Designing accountability structures. Distributing authority before it was needed. Installing culture before installing curriculum. Every Socratic seminar protocol I handed to students, every peer revision structure built around evidence standards, every decision about which student ran which part of a simulation was an act of organizational design. I was simply not compensated as though it was.

The business world eventually noticed the gap. They named it leadership development, built conferences around it, wrote the books, and hired the consultants. What they built, almost without exception, was training. Skills programs. Workshops. The assumption underneath all of it is that leadership is something you learn – not something you design into a system. Schools had been doing the latter for over a century, in every building, every September. The knowledge didn’t transfer because the status didn’t travel with it. And so, business kept reinventing a broken answer to a problem that had already been solved elsewhere.

That gap is structural, and it has consequences. When a consultant tells a founder to “empower their team,” they are pointing at something middle school teachers have been building since September, in rooms full of twelve-year-olds, with no budget and constant real-time feedback from the most honest audience imaginable. Seventh graders DO NOT perform engagement out of politeness. You know immediately when the system isn’t working.

The three things that crossed directly from my classroom into the leadership work I now do with founders and executives aren’t insights I developed after I left teaching. They’re things I built on repeat, for years, in a building where no one thought to call it leadership. Here’s what organizations keep failing to do that every decent classroom does by default.

The first is decision rights distributed before they’re needed. In a well-designed classroom, students know which decisions belong to them before any situation tests it – who runs the Socratic seminar, who tracks the evidence standard, who calls the question when discussion drifts. By September, before any crisis forced the question, the authority map already existed. Founders almost always reverse this sequence. They clarify who owns what only after something goes wrong – precisely when clarity is hardest to establish, and the stakes are highest. The map gets drawn in the fire, which is the worst possible time to draw it.

The second is culture installed before content. I never taught the first unit – on colonization, on the Constitution, on the Trail of Tears – before the class knew how we worked. How we disagreed. What we did when someone cited a bad source. What it means to revise a claim under pressure. Leaders rush toward strategy before they have built the operating conditions that make strategy executable. The sequence is not a preference. It’s a prerequisite.

The third is feedback loops that run without the leader as gatekeeper. By October of any given year, my seventh graders were giving each other more precise, more actionable feedback on argument structure and evidence quality than most management teams exchange in a full quarter. Not because they were exceptional. Because I had built the criteria, the structure, and the practice into the room itself. The loop ran when I stepped back, because I designed it to run that way.

If your organization doesn’t have all three, you don’t have a team problem. You have a design problem.

Three questions worth sitting with:

  1. If you stepped out for two weeks tomorrow, which decisions would stall – and is that because your people lack judgment, or because you never mapped who owns what?

  2. Can your team name the three or four principles that govern how you make decisions together – or did you skip straight to strategy?

  3. When was the last time your team gave each other hard, useful feedback without you in the room?

That is what CLUTCH argues. Not that leaders need to work harder. The system needs to be designed to work when they step back. I built that case over 20 years in a classroom. I’ve spent the years since figuring out why I had to leave to be heard.
