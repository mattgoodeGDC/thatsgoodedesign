It’s Not You. It’s the System.

Matt Goode12 June 20267 min read Get new pieces by email

# Everything runs through me

“Best quarter we’ve ever had,” he told me. Then, almost in the same breath: “and I’ve never been more buried.”

He’s a CTO – builds hard, knows the product cold, sits in on every customer call because that’s how the difficult ones close. The growth was real. So was the exhaustion, and here’s the part that didn’t add up to him: it was getting worse, not better, in direct proportion to the wins.

A great quarter should buy you room. His bought him more calls he couldn’t miss, more decisions that routed through him, more nights spent catching up on the work he’d set aside to do the closing. Success was pouring more volume through the one part of the system that can’t be in two places at once – him.

You can’t hustle your way out of a bottleneck

Watch what he kept doing, because almost every capable builder does the same thing. He’d hit the edge of his capacity, feel the strain, and answer it by working harder. Earlier mornings. Tighter calendar. One more personal system to keep the plates spinning. And every time, the relief held for about a week before the load caught back up – because effort doesn’t change the architecture. It just runs the same design at higher RPM.

A bottleneck isn’t a measure of how hard you’re working. It’s a property of how the work is routed. When every call that matters flows through one person, that person becomes a constraint no amount of personal horsepower can fix – because the problem was never the horsepower. It was the single lane that everything had to merge into.

It’s not a character flaw. It’s a design state.

This is the reframe that actually lets a leader breathe, so let me be precise about it. The reason you’re the bottleneck is not that you’re disorganized, or undisciplined, or secretly not good enough for the job you built. Noam Wasserman spent years at Harvard studying close to ten thousand founders and named the trap cleanly: the very instincts that build a company – ownership, speed, high standards, the reflex to just handle it – are the same instincts that cap it. You move fast by doing it yourself. The system grows around your judgment. One day, you look up, and you’re the merge point on your own highway, every car idling for you.

That’s not a verdict on your character. It’s a design state, and the distinction matters enormously – because you can’t change your character on a Tuesday, but you can change a design. You don’t rise to the level of your effort; you fall to the level of your systems. The leaders who get free didn’t find more willpower than you have. They changed what had to route through them.

Caption: Work harder vs. change the design – effort buries you, redesign compounds.

Why does the best month make it worse?

The mechanism is counterintuitive, and it’s the whole trap. Every time you pull the work back – take the call yourself, fix the deck yourself, sit in the meeting because briefing someone would take longer – you get a clean result and a small hit of relief. You also send a signal that costs you later: this still runs through me. The gap you were trying to cover gets one more day of permission to stay a gap. The way out isn’t less commitment. It’s pointing the same commitment at a different target – building the lanes instead of being the only one open. That’s slower for a few weeks. Then it’s the only thing that ever actually lightens the load.

  • What are you still pulling back to do yourself, that’s already drifting back to your desk?

  • If the bottleneck is a design and not a verdict, what’s the first piece of the design you’d change?
