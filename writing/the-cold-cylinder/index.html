The Cold Cylinder

Matt Goode22 July 20262 min read Get new pieces by email

# CREATE# Winning feels like a near miss

An engine never tells you a cylinder has gone cold. The car keeps moving. It just works harder, runs hotter, and burns more fuel to do less. By the time the warning light comes on, you’ve been losing power for months.

Leaders run the same way, and the cold cylinder is almost never the one you’d guess. It’s the one you stopped checking, because it used to run clean on its own.

Six cylinders, one honest question each

CREATE has six: Culture, Relationships, Execution, Agility, Teaching, Ethics. Most leaders keep the two or three they’re strong in roaring and let the rest idle, never feeling the drag because the car still moves.

So run the diagnostic, and watch your gut more than your answer.

  • Culture: if you stepped away for a week, would it hold its shape, or bend toward the loudest voice in the room?

  • Relationships: when did someone last tell you something you didn’t want to hear?

  • Execution: does work actually finish, or just move?

  • Agility: When the plan broke last month, how long did it take the team to adjust?

  • Teaching: who is measurably more capable because of how you led them this quarter?

  • Ethics: what did you decide recently that cost you something to get right?

  • You felt the cold one. It was the question you wanted to skip – the one where the honest answer is “I’m not sure,” or “it’s been a while.”

The leverage is where it feels worst to push

Here’s the counterintuitive part. You don’t fix the engine by adding power to the cylinders already firing. But that’s exactly where every instinct sends you, because pushing on your strength feels productive and safe. The operator runs the Execution cylinder harder. The visionary polishes Culture again. Meanwhile the cold cylinder, the one starving the whole engine, goes another quarter unexamined.

Caption: The leverage is the cold cylinder – pushing the ones already firing just adds heat.

The cost of that misplaced effort is real and compounding. A team can run a long time on four cylinders before anyone names it, and the whole time they’re working harder than they should to get less than they could, burning goodwill and energy to cover for a gap no one’s addressing. One honest quarter-turn on the cold cylinder buys back more than a year of pushing on the warm ones.

One practical note: don’t run all six questions and launch a six-front improvement plan. That’s how a diagnostic turns into another source of overwhelm. Pick the single cylinder that made you flinch and give it one concrete action this week. One hard conversation. One thing taught. One decision made cleanly. A cold cylinder doesn’t warm up on its own. It warms up from a rep.

Sit with these before you decide where to push next:

  • Which cylinder did you not want to read just now?

  • What have you been telling yourself to avoid looking at it?

  • If you warmed that one over the next month, who would feel the difference first?
