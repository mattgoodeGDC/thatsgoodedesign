No job is finished until it leaves a note

Systems don't break in the middle of the work. They break where it changes hands.

Matt Goode3 October 20265 min read Get new pieces by email

# DELEGATE# Everything runs through me

Most work doesn’t get lost while someone is doing it. It gets lost in the gap between one person putting it down and the next person picking it up.

Hospitals learned this the expensive way. A patient’s care changes hands at every shift change, and for a long time that handoff was an informal verbal rundown. When nine pediatric residency programs replaced it with a structured routine called I-PASS, medical errors fell by 23 percent and preventable adverse events by 30 percent. Same doctors. Same patients. The only thing that changed was the handover.

That study stuck with me, because most of the founders I coach have the same problem. Most of them would also tell you they don’t do handovers at all.

You hand off to yourself all day

If you run a practice of one or a few, handovers can sound like a big-company problem. You don’t have shifts. There’s no one to hand to.

But you hand work over constantly. Morning You hands a proposal to Afternoon You. Monday You leaves a half-finished follow-up for Thursday You. The You Who Just Got Off a Client Call hands the next steps to the You Who Will Remember Them, eventually, probably.

Every one of those is a handover. Most of them happen in your head, with no note. That’s the hospital’s informal rundown, minus the hospital.

In my house this is a standing joke. I load the dishwasher, get distracted – squirrel – and forget to start it. I can’t tell you how many mornings I’ve come downstairs wanting coffee and found the French press still sitting in the dishwasher, dirty.

My wife never seems to have this problem. Or maybe she does, and I’ve forgotten, because she can remind me of things from years ago.

That’s a dropped handover. Evening Me did most of the job and handed the last step to nobody. Morning Me paid for it before coffee.

Here’s what the other kind looks like.

Those are book award deadlines for my next novel, WRATH, most of them months away. Fiction isn’t my main business, which is exactly why I would forget every one of them. So each task carries what future me will need: the hard deadline, a start date a week or so earlier, a reminder, and the small decision already made. Books must arrive by 12 February, not merely be posted by it. One copy from the November print order, trade cover only. October Me made those calls, so March Me doesn’t have to dig for them or get them wrong.

That’s a handover across six months, from me to me, and nobody had to remember anything.

You’ll also notice every task has a number. That’s the first of the three choices from my last essay: every task gets a permanent name. The word before the dash says which part of my work it belongs to. The number never changes and never gets reused, even if I rename the task. So a note can say “Done: WRATH-42,” and I can still find it a year from now. One task can point to another by its number. And when a list of questions is waiting on me, I can answer “WRATH-42 is fine, push WRATH-39” instead of explaining which awards thing I mean.

The note at the end of every job

When I built my own system, the rule I’m proudest of is the boring one. No job is finished until it leaves a note.

The note has three parts:

  1. What was done and decided. Two or three sentences, written as a story, not a status. Decisions matter more than activity, because you forget decisions first.

  2. What’s new. Any follow-up the job created goes onto the one shared list, with a name, before the job closes. Not buried in the note. On the list.

  3. What needs me, and what doesn’t. Anything waiting on a decision goes in a short numbered list, so I can answer by number. Everything else is already decided, and the note says so.

The next morning, the notes get read before anything else. That’s the last step in I-PASS, and it may matter most: the person taking over says back what they heard before the handover counts. A note nobody reads is just a diary. Nothing lives only in my head overnight.

The step leaders skip

DELEGATE, the framework in CLUTCH, has eight steps. The one leaders skip most is Transfer: the moment ownership actually changes hands.

Most delegation fails right there. The leader explains the work, sets the expectation, then hands it over in passing. The context stays in their head. Two weeks later, the work comes back wrong, and everyone concludes the person wasn’t ready.

Usually the person was fine. The handover wasn’t. You don’t have a delegation problem. You have a handover problem.

A good handover carries the same three things the note does: what’s already been decided, what’s still open, and what the new owner can decide without asking. That last one is the License. Leave it out, and the work boomerangs back to your desk, because the new owner can’t move without checking.

The same is true when the new owner is future you. If Thursday you doesn’t know what Monday you already decided, Thursday you will either redo the thinking or stall. Both feel like being busy. Neither is progress.

What changed

Here’s a small one. LinkedIn supposedly rewards you for posting the link to your essay a day later, in the comments, rather than with the post itself. I have never once remembered to do that. It’s too small a detail, and by the next day I’m onto something else. So I gave up and pasted the link in the comments as I posted, every time.

It isn’t even creative work. The comment is the same every time: “Click here for the full essay,” and the link. LinkedIn will let you schedule a post, but not a comment on it. So for now the comment gets its own task, with a name and a date, and when I miss it, the miss doesn’t vanish. It’s still on the list the next morning. I’m still working out how to automate the rest.

The surprise for me was that the note isn’t really for the system. It’s for the next person, and most days the next person is me.

Try it this week

You don’t need software for this. Take the next five things you finish this week, and before you call each one done, write three lines:

  1. What did I do, and what did I decide?

  2. What new tasks did this create, and where did I put them?

  3. What still needs a decision, and what can go ahead without one?

  4. If any of those take longer than a minute to answer, you’ve found where your work goes missing.
