I kept the shelf and enforced the policy

Better talent identification makes the problem worse

Matt Goode22 September 20264 min read Get new pieces by email

# Growing faster than it's getting clearer# My role is changing

I kept a bookshelf in my room at McDonogh. Not a library, not a program. A shelf, in the room where I met my middle school advisees, stocked with whatever I thought might land on somebody. Novels, comics, books about cars.

Our librarians were excellent, and most of what sat on that shelf came from asking them what to get. But a kid had to walk to the library, and the librarians couldn't be everywhere. The shelf didn't need anyone to walk anywhere. It was already in the room.

One of my advisees hated reading and loved baseball, so I handed him Heat. He read it. Then he read the next one.

Nothing about that required him to be anything first. No reading level, no referral, no conversation about what he'd struggled with. He liked baseball, there was a book about baseball, and the shelf was right there.

Meanwhile the school ran DEAR. Drop Everything And Read, Thursdays, everyone at once, building-wide. I don't know a better way to make a kid hate reading than to require it on a schedule. But to support my English colleagues… (but the math teacher and I would often quip, when is “drop everything and do equations!” day?)

And once reading was “mandatory, not reading became misconduct. I was also the Dean. So I was the one standing there asking, "Why don't you have a book today?"

I hated that part.

Here's the part that should bother you.

Your company has a DEAR. Several, probably. Mandatory training, a development framework, a mentoring program with an application form. It probably also has good people whose whole job is helping other people get better, the way our librarians were good.

What it may not have is anyone's shelf. The question was never whether the expertise exists. It's whether someone has to cross the building to reach it.

Run the list. Who gets a mentor? Someone a manager put forward. Who gets the stretch assignment? Someone already on the list. Who gets the development budget? Someone whose last review cleared a bar. Who gets coached? Either a star or a problem, and the conversation that decided which one is a conversation you weren't in.

You'll want to say this is what a finite budget looks like. That gates allocate scarce development toward expected return, and that's arithmetic, not a moral failure.

That defense would hold if gates measured expected return. They measure legibility. A gate sorts for who's readable by the instrument you happen to own, and the distance between readable and valuable is far wider than the people running the gate believe. My advisee wasn't a reluctant reader. He was a baseball reader in a building with no instrument for baseball readers.

Now the harder part. You cannot audit this by looking at who you developed. You can only look at who you did not, and those people are, by construction, the ones you have no data on.

Except that isn't quite true, and it's where most leadership teams stop too early. The missing leave traces. Start with who volunteers for the optional things, then write down five capable people who never appear on any of those lists. Somewhere between year two and year four you'll find people who haven't changed role or scope in eighteen months and aren't underperforming; they're still employed, which is exactly what makes them invisible. Internal transfer records will tell you who applied twice and then stopped. And in an exit interview, the useful answer arrives around the twenty-minute mark, once the rehearsed part is done.

None of that measures the people you missed. All of it measures the shape of the hole.

And the standard remedy makes the hole bigger. When a leadership team senses it's missing people, the reflex is to improve identification: a better talent review, tighter calibration, clearer criteria for who counts as high potential. All of that sharpens the instrument. None of it reaches the person the instrument can't read. You can calibrate a gate for a decade, and it's still a gate.

What helps is one thing in the building with no gate on it.

Not a program. Programs have intake. One ungated thing, reachable without a nomination, a rating, or somebody's yes. Office hours anyone can book, three levels down included, not routed through a manager. A channel where people put a hand up for work before anyone has assessed whether they should. A budget line small enough to need no business case.

It'll feel inefficient. That's the price. The gates were installed to prevent exactly this waste, and they do. They also prevent the other thing.

The gate audit

  1. Name every route to support, growth, or visibility here. For each, write down what is required to get in.

  2. How many require someone else's judgment first? A nomination, a rating, a manager's yes.

  3. What is left with nothing in front of it? If the answer is nothing, that is the finding.

  4. Build one ungated thing this quarter. Then name the person who has to agree never to put a gate on it later.

The fourth is the real work. Ungated things don't stay ungated. They accumulate criteria quietly, because every individual criterion is sensible and whoever adds it is trying to help.

My shelf stayed open because nobody ever wrote it down. That's also why it left when I did.

Most of your people are fine inside the gates. They get nominated, they clear the bar, the system finds them.

Pick one route this week and write down what it costs to enter. You'll know straight away whether anyone here keeps a shelf.
