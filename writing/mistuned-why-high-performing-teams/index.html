Mistuned? Why High-Performing Teams Still Lose Their Best People

Essay 4: Neurodiversity Series

Matt Goode12 January 202619 min read Get new pieces by email

# Neuroinclusion# The culture is drifting

A few years ago, I was working with a leadership team trying to understand why their best people kept leaving after 18 months.

Exit interviews were polite. “Pursuing other opportunities.” “Better fit elsewhere.” The usual scripts.

But when I dug deeper – when I asked the question behind the question – a pattern emerged.

It wasn’t the workload. It wasn’t compensation. It wasn’t even the mission.

It was the constant cognitive dissonance of working in a system where nothing was ever quite explained.

“How do we make decisions here?” “It depends.”

“Who has final say when there’s disagreement?” “We all kind of figure it out together.”

“What does success look like in this role?” “You’ll know it when you see it.”

For neurotypical employees, this ambiguity felt like trust. Like autonomy. Like a collaborative, non-hierarchical culture.

For neurodivergent employees, it felt like a daily cognitive tax they couldn’t afford to keep paying.

One person put it this way: “I can do my job. I’m good at my job. But I’m spending half my energy trying to reverse-engineer the invisible rules of how things work here. And I’m exhausted.”

They didn’t need empathy training. They needed architectural clarity.

That’s when I realized: most leaders think building neuroinclusive organizations requires a whole new framework – new policies, new training programs, new HR initiatives.

They don’t.

They need to understand how the framework they’re already using works differently for different brains.

CREATE – Culture, Relationships, Execution, Agility, Teaching, Ethics – isn’t neurotypical by default. It’s a systems model. And systems can be tuned. The question isn’t whether CREATE works for neurodivergent teams. It’s whether you’ve tuned each cylinder to unlock cognitive capacity instead of demanding cognitive conformity.

Because here’s the thing about engines: you can have all six cylinders firing – Culture, Relationships, Execution, Agility, Teaching, Ethics – and still have half your team running on fumes. Not because the cylinders don’t work. But because they’re calibrated for one kind of brain, and you never realized it was a choice.

The CREATE Engine: A Brief Primer

Before we tune each cylinder for cognitive diversity, let me explain what CREATE is and why the engine metaphor matters.

CREATE is a diagnostic framework I developed for understanding how organizations actually function – not how they say they function in vision statements, but how the daily architecture of work either enables or constrains human potential. It stands for Culture, Relationships, Execution, Agility, Teaching, and Ethics – six interconnected systems that determine whether your organization can perform under pressure or breaks down when it matters most.

I think of CREATE as a six-cylinder engine. Each cylinder does distinct work, but they’re not independent. They fire in sequence, and they depend on each other. Culture sets the norms that shape Relationships. Relationships determine who has authority in Execution. Execution reveals whether you have the Agility to adapt when plans hit reality. Agility requires Teaching – the capacity to learn and adjust. And Ethics governs whether all of this serves people or just extracts from them.

When all six cylinders are firing in sync, the engine hums. Decisions get made with clarity. Teams adapt without chaos. People grow without burning out.

But here’s what most leaders miss: you can have all six cylinders technically functioning and still have half your team running on fumes. Not because the cylinders don’t work, but because they’re calibrated for one kind of brain. And if you’ve never questioned that calibration – if you’ve assumed your systems are neutral when they’re actually optimized for neurotypical processing – you’re not just leaving performance on the table. You’re systematically burning out the cognitive profiles you need most.

What follows is a guide to tuning each cylinder for cognitive diversity. Not as an accommodation strategy, but as operational intelligence. Because when you design systems that work for different brains, you don’t just help neurodivergent employees. You build a more resilient, more adaptive, more capable engine for everyone.

So let’s tune the engine. Starting with the cylinder where everything either begins – or quietly dies.

Culture: From Vibe to Visibility

Let’s start with Culture – because this is where neuroinclusive design either takes root or dies quietly.

For neurotypical employees, culture often feels intuitive. It’s “the vibe.” It’s “how we do things around here,” absorbed through osmosis, through watching, through pattern-matching social cues and adjusting accordingly. For many neurodivergent employees – especially autistic professionals – that same “vibe” feels like paralyzing ambiguity.

Culture audit: Can someone succeed in your organization if they:

  • Never attend happy hour?

  • Don’t make small talk before meetings?

  • Ask for written agendas instead of “just rolling with it”?

  • Wear headphones to manage sensory input?

If you answered “probably not” to any of these, your culture isn’t inclusive – it’s neurotypical-exclusive.

“Be collaborative” – but what does that actually mean? Respond to every Slack message within the hour? Attend every optional meeting? Volunteer ideas in real-time even when you need processing time? “Take initiative” – but how much? And in which domains? And do I ask first, or will that look like I lack confidence? “We value authenticity” – but which parts of authenticity? The parts that look like extroversion? Or actual authenticity, which for some people is quiet, task-focused, and energized by depth rather than breadth?

When culture is implicit, neurotypical employees navigate through social fluency. Neurodivergent employees navigate through constant cognitive modeling – reverse-engineering the unwritten rules while simultaneously trying to do their actual jobs. That’s not culture. That’s a decoding tax.

The cost shows up everywhere. They spend more energy managing social uncertainty than solving problems. They second-guess every interaction. They rehearse before speaking. They burn out not from the work, but from the invisible labor of figuring out how to work here. And because the exhaustion doesn’t announce itself as “I’m trying to decode your implicit norms,” it gets misread as disengagement, low initiative, or poor culture fit.

What neuroinclusive Culture looks like: You make the invisible visible. You document “how we do things here” – not as bureaucracy, but as clarity. You create explicit communication norms: “We respond to Slack within 24 hours, not 24 minutes.” You define what collaboration actually means in your context: “Collaboration here means co-creating solutions in shared docs, not performing enthusiasm in meetings.” You specify decision rights: “On project X, you own the decision. On project Y, you provide input but Leader Z decides.”

This isn’t micromanagement. It’s cognitive relief. Because when the rules are explicit, people can stop spending energy guessing and start spending it contributing. If “culture fit” is part of your hiring process, define it in behavioral terms, not vibes. If “executive presence” is part of your promotion criteria, specify what you actually mean – and make sure it’s measuring leadership impact, not neurotypical communication style.

Culture done right doesn’t just prevent burnout. It transforms who gets to show up as their full selves without constant translation.

Relationships: From Social Fluency to Structural Clarity

The Relationships cylinder is where role clarity becomes neuroinclusive design, and where most organizations accidentally create cognitive landmines.

Here’s what happens in most organizations: relationships are managed through social navigation. You figure out who really makes decisions by watching meeting dynamics. You learn whose opinion matters by noticing who gets deferred to. You decode power structures through subtext, through who interrupts whom, through who gets invited to which conversations. For neurotypical employees, this is just how work works. For neurodivergent employees – especially those who struggle with social cue interpretation or find office politics cognitively expensive – it’s a persistent drain on executive function.

Every ambiguous “we all own this” project becomes a minefield of invisible expectations. Every “let’s collaborate” initiative becomes a guessing game about who’s actually responsible for what. Every org change becomes an exercise in re-mapping informal power structures no one will explicitly explain. The cognitive budget spent decoding these dynamics is budget not spent on expertise, insight, or innovation.

They avoid asking for help because they can’t figure out who to ask. They miss opportunities because they didn’t realize certain conversations were the real decision-making forums. They’re told they need to “build relationships” but can’t decode which relationships matter or how to build them in ways that feel authentic rather than performative.

What neuroinclusive Relationships look like: You eliminate the social decoding tax by making authority and accountability structurally explicit.

Here’s what explicit decision rights look like:

  • “You own this decision. Consult me, but it’s yours.”

  • “I’m deciding this. Your input shapes the decision, but I’m accountable.”

  • “We’re deciding together. We don’t move until we agree.”

When “who owns what” is clear, autistic professionals especially can focus on competence instead of politics. When decision rights are documented, ADHD brains don’t have to track informal power dynamics – they can direct their questions to the right person and move forward.

Use RACI (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed) for everything. Not because you’re bureaucratic, but because clarity is kind. Eliminate “we all own it” ambiguity – that phrase is neurotypical code for “figure it out through social cues.” Protect expertise-based authority. Let people lead from their strengths without having to perform dominance to be heard.

When Relationships are tuned correctly, trust doesn’t come from reading the room. It comes from knowing exactly where you stand and who you can count on. That’s not just neuroinclusive design – that’s how high-functioning teams operate regardless of neurotype.

Execution: From Process Conformity to Outcome Diversity

The Execution cylinder is where cognitive diversity either compounds productivity or creates friction, and where most leaders accidentally filter out their best work by optimizing for process uniformity.

Most execution systems are designed around a single assumption: everyone processes information the same way. But they don’t. Some brains need written instructions before they can start. Others need to talk it through first. Some need hard deadlines to activate focus. Others need autonomy and will deliver early if you just get out of the way. Some need structure and predictability. Others need novelty and will disengage if the process feels repetitive.

When you design execution systems that assume uniform processing, you’re not creating efficiency. You’re creating a cognitive filter that only lets one kind of brain work at full capacity.

ADHD brains stall in rigid, sequential workflows that don’t allow for iterative problem-solving. Autistic brains struggle when instructions are vague or change mid-process without explanation. Dyslexic brains lose access to their strategic brilliance when forced to document everything in writing before they’ve had a chance to talk it through.

You get compliance. But you don’t get their best work. And because the process looks efficient on paper – everyone following the same workflow, everyone hitting the same checkpoints – you don’t see what you’re losing. You just notice that certain people seem “less engaged” or “struggle with follow-through,” never questioning whether the workflow itself is the problem.

What neuroinclusive Execution looks like: You design multiple pathways to the same outcome. You focus on deliverables, not process conformity. You let people choose their workflow as long as the output meets the standard. You offer both written and verbal briefing options. You separate “what needs to happen” from “how you make it happen.”

Process flexibility in practice:

  • Outcome-defined work: “Deliver X by Friday, work when/how you process best”

  • Multiple input channels: Written, verbal, visual – take your pick

  • Async default: Real-time only when synchronous is actually necessary

One leader told me: “I thought this would create confusion. Instead, everyone got clearer on what actually mattered.”

This doesn’t mean chaos. It means calibrated autonomy – enough structure to create alignment, enough flexibility to let different brains do what they do best. Stop measuring process. Start measuring outcomes. If someone delivers excellent work but never speaks in the standup, that’s not a problem – that’s a brain working the way it works. If someone needs noise-canceling headphones and flexible hours to produce their best thinking, that’s not an accommodation. That’s operational intelligence.

Execution done right doesn’t just reduce friction. It unlocks capacity you didn’t know you had.

Agility: From Pivoting to Preparing

The Agility cylinder is where most neuroinclusive design efforts fail – because leaders confuse speed with adaptability and end up rewarding one kind of brain while destabilizing another.

Here’s the thing about pivots: for neurotypical employees, they can feel energizing. A change of direction. A new challenge. Momentum. For many neurodivergent employees – especially autistic and ADHD professionals – sudden pivots aren’t agility. They’re dysregulation. Because transitions are neurologically expensive. Shifting contexts burns executive function. Changing plans mid-process isn’t just inconvenient – it’s cognitively destabilizing.

And when you treat “moving fast” as synonymous with “being agile,” you’re actually creating a system that rewards one kind of brain and punishes another. You announce a pivot in Monday’s all-hands and expect everyone to adjust by Tuesday. While neurotypical team members are already brainstorming the new direction, neurodivergent team members are still processing what just happened – not because they’re slow, but because their brains need time to reorient the entire system they’d built around the previous plan. By the time they’ve caught up, they’re labeled “resistant to change.”

I once watched a product team pivot from feature A to feature B in a Monday standup. By Wednesday, the engineering lead – autistic, brilliant systems thinker – was being called “slow to adapt” because he hadn’t contributed to the new direction yet. But he wasn’t being slow. He was doing what his brain required: reorienting his entire mental model before he could assess the implications. When he finally spoke on Friday, his input revealed three structural problems the team had missed in their rush to “move fast.” But by then, the damage to his reputation was done.

The real cost isn’t the pivot itself. It’s the cognitive whiplash of being expected to adapt, ideate, and contribute simultaneously. And because the neurodivergent employees who struggle with this often don’t say anything – they’ve learned that naming this difficulty gets read as inflexibility – you lose their insight entirely. They check out. They stop offering ideas. They become the “reliable executors” who never push back, and you mistake their silence for alignment.

What neuroinclusive Agility looks like: You build transition buffers. You give advance notice when possible – even 48 hours makes a difference. You explain why the change is happening, not just what’s changing. You offer asynchronous processing time – a shared doc where people can think through implications before the live discussion. You separate “we’re changing direction” from “we need your input on how” so people aren’t expected to ideate and integrate simultaneously.

Real agility isn’t speed. It’s adaptive capacity. And adaptive capacity requires time to metabolize change – not just react to it. Build transition protocols. When plans change, don’t just announce – prepare. Give people time to process before you expect contribution. Normalize “I need a day to think about this” as strategic, not resistant.

Agility done right doesn’t slow you down. It ensures everyone’s moving in the same direction instead of half your team sprinting forward while the other half is still reorienting.

Teaching: From Uniform Feedback to Personalized Development

The Teaching cylinder is where leaders either unlock growth or accidentally reinforce masking, and where most feedback systems optimize for performance theater rather than actual development.

Here’s the problem with most feedback systems: they’re designed for one kind of processing. Real-time. Verbal. Delivered in 1:1s where the expectation is that you’ll respond, process, and adjust on the spot. For neurotypical employees, this often works. They can think and talk simultaneously. They can metabolize feedback in the moment and articulate next steps.

For many neurodivergent employees, this same format is cognitively expensive. Some brains need time to process feedback separately from the social dynamics of receiving it. Some need it in writing so they can re-read and integrate without the pressure of real-time response. Some need concrete examples, not abstract principles, because their brains don’t generalize the same way.

When you design feedback systems that assume one processing style, you’re not developing people. You’re teaching them to perform development in the format you recognize. They leave 1:1s nodding and smiling – because they’ve learned that’s the expected response – but they haven’t actually integrated the feedback. Or worse, they integrate it too literally, applying a specific correction in one context to every context, because you didn’t explicitly map the boundary conditions.

And because you’re measuring receptiveness to feedback rather than application of feedback, you miss the gap entirely. You think they’re developing. They’re actually just getting better at looking like they’re developing.

What neuroinclusive Teaching looks like: You adapt your feedback delivery to their processing mode – not the other way around. You offer written feedback options because some brains process text better than speech. You separate “what happened” from “what it means” because some brains need the data before the interpretation. You use concrete examples, not abstract principles, because generalizing from abstraction is harder for some cognitive profiles. You give processing time between feedback delivery and action planning.

This isn’t lowering standards. This is increasing feedback uptake by meeting people where their brains actually work. Ask people how they receive feedback best – then honor it. Create multiple formats: live 1:1s, written summaries, recorded video walkthroughs. Let people choose the one that lets them actually learn, not just perform receptiveness.

Teaching done right doesn’t just improve individual performance. It creates a culture where growth is real, not performed.

Ethics: From Inclusion Theater to Protection of Difference

The Ethics cylinder is where neuroinclusive leadership becomes a moral architecture, not just a strategy, and where organizations reveal whether they actually mean what they say about valuing diversity.

Because here’s what most leaders miss: neuroinclusive ethics isn’t about being nice. It’s about protecting the right to work differently – even when it looks like “low engagement” to neurotypical observers. Even when it doesn’t photograph well for the company culture deck. Even when it challenges your assumptions about what “good leadership” looks like.

This is where the rubber meets the road. Organizations say they value diversity – then penalize people for not showing up the way they expect. They say outcomes matter – then dock someone’s performance review for “not being visible enough.” They say they want innovation – then filter out the cognitive profiles most likely to see what they’re missing because they don’t “fit the culture.”

The Ethics cylinder asks: are we building systems that serve people, or demanding that people serve our systems? Are we measuring impact, or measuring conformity? Are we protecting the right to cognitive difference, or are we just performing inclusion while quietly rewarding sameness?

What neuroinclusive Ethics look like: You measure outcomes, not “face time.” You protect asynchronous work as a legitimate mode of contribution. You challenge “culture fit” as a hiring criterion because “fit” is often neurotype conformity in disguise. You redesign “executive presence” criteria to assess leadership impact, not communication style.

Make it explicit: “We protect the right to work differently.” Not as a policy add-on, but as a design principle that shapes how every other cylinder operates. Then audit your systems against it. Every time you see “professionalism,” “presence,” “collaboration,” ask: are we measuring impact, or are we measuring conformity?

This is the Ethics cylinder doing what it’s designed to do: ensuring that the system serves people, not the other way around. And when you get this right, something shifts. Neurodivergent employees stop spending energy on camouflage and start spending it on contribution. Neurotypical employees benefit too, because clarity, autonomy, and outcome-focus aren’t accommodations – they’re good design for everyone.

Ethics done right doesn’t just protect difference. It transforms what excellence looks like.

Six Diagnostic Questions

Before we close, six questions to surface where your CREATE engine might be demanding cognitive conformity without realizing it:

1. Culture – Does “fitting in” actually mean “thinking like us”?

Look at your last three exits. Did the people who left process information differently than the people who stayed? If your culture feels intuitive to some and exhausting to others, you’re not building trust through ambiguity – you’re creating a decoding tax that only some brains can afford to pay.

2. Relationships – How much cognitive energy are people spending just figuring out who to ask?

When someone needs help, can they look it up – or do they have to decode informal hierarchies through social cues? If “building relationships” is treated as a core competency but you’ve never documented who owns what, you’re demanding political fluency as a prerequisite for contribution.

3. Execution – Are you rewarding outcomes, or rewarding the performance of a specific work style?

Look at your last performance review cycle. Did anyone get feedback about “how” they work that had nothing to do with the quality of what they delivered? If “doesn’t speak up in standups” or “works odd hours” shows up as a concern, you’re measuring process conformity, not impact.

4. Agility – When was the last time you called someone “resistant to change” who just needed time to process it?

Think about your last major pivot. Who adapted fastest? Who went quiet? If the people who went quiet were also your deepest thinkers, you didn’t have a resistance problem – you had a dysregulation problem. Real agility includes everyone, not just the brains that pivot in real-time.

5. Teaching – Are people actually integrating your feedback, or just performing receptiveness?

Look at your feedback loops. Do people nod, smile, and then not change? That’s not a motivation problem – that’s a format problem. If you’re only offering one mode of feedback delivery, you’re teaching people to perform growth in the style you recognize, not actually grow.

6. Ethics – How many of your “high performers” are running on fumes?

When was the last time someone burned out after getting a good performance review? If the answer is “recently,” you have a camouflage problem. They were meeting your standards – but the cost of meeting those standards became unsustainable. And you didn’t see it because you were measuring output, not energy.

There’s no such thing as a neutral system. Just design choices that unlock some brains and constrain others.

The question isn’t whether CREATE works for neurodivergent teams. It’s whether you’ve tuned it to let different brains do what they do best – without demanding they pretend to be someone else first.

Because the organizations that figure this out won’t just be more inclusive. They’ll be more resilient, more innovative, and more capable of solving the problems that cognitively homogeneous teams keep missing.

And the leaders who learn to tune their systems for cognitive diversity won’t just retain more talent. They’ll unlock capacity they never knew existed – in the people who were already there, just waiting for the architecture to catch up to their potential.

Rate your agreement with each statement on a scale of 1-5: (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree)

  • 1 = Completely implicit / 2 = Mostly implicit / 3 = Partially explicit / 4 = Mostly explicit / 5 = Fully documented

2. RELATIONSHIPS: Decision authority and role ownership are structurally clear, not decoded through social fluency.

  • 1 = Very ambiguous / 2 = Mostly ambiguous / 3 = Somewhat clear / 4 = Mostly clear / 5 = Completely explicit

3. EXECUTION: People can deliver excellent work using different processes, as long as outcomes meet standards.

  • 1 = One way only / 2 = Limited flexibility / 3 = Some flexibility / 4 = High flexibility / 5 = Outcome-focused design

4. AGILITY: When we pivot or change direction, we build in transition time before expecting contribution.

  • 1 = Never / 2 = Rarely / 3 = Sometimes / 4 = Usually / 5 = Always

5. TEACHING: We adapt feedback delivery to match how people process information, not just our preferred style.

  • 1 = One format only / 2 = Minimal adaptation / 3 = Some adaptation / 4 = Regular adaptation / 5 = Fully personalized

6. ETHICS: We explicitly protect the right to work differently, even when it challenges our norms.

  • 1 = Not protected / 2 = Minimally / 3 = Somewhat / 4 = Significantly / 5 = Core design principle

Scoring Guide:

25-30 points: Your CREATE engine is likely well-tuned for cognitive diversity. Consider: Which specific practices create this environment? How can you document and share them?

18-24 points: Some cylinders are tuned well, others may be creating friction. Consider: Which cylinder scored lowest? That’s your highest-leverage intervention point.

12-17 points: Multiple cylinders are likely demanding conformity. Consider: Start with Culture and Relationships – these two shape everything else.

6-11 points: Your engine may be calibrated exclusively for neurotypical processing. Consider: This isn’t permanent – it’s just current state. Pick one cylinder to retune as a pilot.
