HAL 9000 Didn't Write My Novel. I Did.

Matt Goode27 March 202610 min read Get new pieces by email

# AI# Off the clock

Go ahead. Open a new AI chat window. Type this prompt:

“Write me the next great American novel in the style of The Great Gatsby.”

I’ll wait.

What you’ll get back is competent and might tap-out at about 2000 words.

It’ll have the green light, a narrator with just enough wistfulness, sentences that gesture toward elegance. It will also be immediately, obviously, hollow. Because Fitzgerald wasn’t generating prose. He was processing the specific psychic wound of watching America confuse wealth with virtue, spectacle with meaning, and reinvention with redemption. That wound was his. The novel came from it.

And by the way, tell me the 2020s don’t mirror the Gilded Age. Unprecedented wealth concentration, technological disruption upending every industry at once, a new class of barons deciding the rules don’t quite apply to them, and an old guard furiously insisting that the new arrivals don’t deserve what they’ve built.

Someone is working on the next great American novel, and I can’t wait to read it.

In the meantime, Gatsby remains my favorite book.

I am a published author.

Boom.

I’m going to let that sit there for a second, because even typing it still feels a little surreal – and because somewhere, my old middle school English teachers are reading this and slowly dying inside. I still don’t know what a dangling participle is. I was a dyslexic kid who was not, by any traditional measure, destined to be a writer.

Last week, I published Balance – a love story about a CRISPR researcher named Ellie, and a promise I kept to my wife after thirty years to write her a romance novel “someday” – and now it is out in the world. Real pages. Real readers. I don’t think I understood what “someday” was costing her until the day I handed her a copy with her name in it. Thirty years is a long time to believe in someone who hasn’t quite believed in himself yet. Turns out having something to say mattered more than knowing the rules for saying it. She knew that before I did. Since then, my brain has hardly been able to contain my ideas. I have a leadership book coming out shortly, and in the pipeline, a retelling of the Iliad in the trenches of WW1, and my biography of the number Seven.

Thanks, hun, for giving me permission and the encouragement to do this. I’m a writer.

And lately I keep getting looped into what I’ll call the authorsphere – writers, publishing folks, people with strong opinions about craft and legitimacy, and who gets to call themselves a “writer.” There’s a freakout happening in those circles right now. Publishers pulling contracts, see “Shy Girl.” Accusations flying. And the forensic evidence being marshaled against suspected AI frauds?

The em dash.

Apparently, if your prose contains an em dash, HAL9000 wrote it. You are a fraud. You did not touch grass, live a life, or feel anything. You typed a prompt and laundered the output as literature.

I use em dashes constantly; for me, they are a break – I probably – should – use a comma, but this is how I think – therefore – also how I write. I also wrote my novel myself – every scene, every character, every plot decision, every late night wondering if any of this was any good. The em dash thing would be funny if it weren’t a stand-in for a much lazier argument: that using any AI tool, at any point, for any purpose, disqualifies the work.

That’s not a position about craft. It’s a position about gatekeeping. There’s a difference.

Here’s exactly what I used AI for.

LinkedIn intros. Instagram captions. When I finish a Substack piece of writing I’m proud of, I’ve already done the hard creative work – the thinking, the drafting, the revising. What I haven’t done is figure out how to game LinkedIn’s engagement algorithm or write a caption that stops the scroll on Instagram. I don’t know how to do that. I’m not sure I want to spend the time learning. Claude does know, so I ask: “Hey, create an optimized Instagram caption for this essay.” The creative thought is mine. The platform optimization is logistics. Nobody accuses a novelist of fraud for having a publicist.

Character revision – and this is the one that might horrify some people. Character revision. Early readers told me one character needed to show greater growth in confidence across the novel. They were right – I could feel it the moment they said it. What I couldn’t see was exactly where the gaps were, scene by scene, across 100,000 words I’d been living inside for years.

So I asked Claude to do what AI is genuinely good at: interpret data. I fed it the manuscript and asked it to track one specific character’s language across every scene – word choice, hedging versus assertion, the linguistic markers of confidence or its absence – and plot that arc linearly. What I got back wasn’t a rewrite suggestion. It was a graph.

The line zigzagged. Up, then down, then up again – confidence spiking and dipping in a pattern that felt accidental rather than earned. I wanted something different: a line that climbed slowly and distinctly, with the occasional setback that still read as forward motion. So I went back into the manuscript and started tinkering. The words were always mine. But treating those words as data – seeing where the character had been quietly dipping in scenes I thought were neutral – gave me a precision I couldn’t have found on my own. Each revision, I’d run it again. Watch the line respond. Adjust.

The tool showed me the data. I made the call.

The distinction I keep coming back to is this:

  • A shortcut replaces the creative process.

  • A tool serves it.

I had a story to tell. I believed it was worth telling. I sat down and told it, imperfect comma usage and all. When I needed help seeing something I couldn’t see on my own, I used tools that helped me see it. Then I made my own decisions.

That’s not so different from a filmmaker using editing software, or a musician using Pro Tools, or an architect using CAD. The software didn’t design the building. It made the designer’s vision executable.

The authorsphere’s anxiety is understandable.

The tools are new, the pace of change is disorienting, and yes, there are absolutely people using AI to generate hollow slop and calling it literature. That’s real, and it’s worth taking seriously. But the response has been to treat any use of AI as contamination – which means conflating the person who asked Claude to write their novel with the person who asked Claude to help them write a better one.

Those are not the same person.

Every knowledge worker is navigating some version of this right now. And the institutions meant to evaluate their work are quietly panicking. Not because the work is worse. Because they can’t tell anymore.

Think about what that actually means in practice. The admissions committee reading a personal essay that might be entirely the applicant’s voice or might be the applicant’s ideas routed through a tool that cleaned up the syntax and tightened the structure – and having no reliable way to know. The hiring manager reviewing a portfolio piece that reflects genuine expertise or reflects the ability to prompt-engineer a convincing simulation of it – and having no reliable way to know. The editor, the professor, the credentialing board, the peer reviewer – all of them holding work up to the light, looking for the watermark, and finding that watermarks don’t work the way they used to.

The panic isn’t aesthetic. It’s epistemological.

These institutions were not built to evaluate outputs in isolation. They were built to evaluate outputs as evidence of process – as proof that a particular kind of thinking happened, in a particular kind of person, over a particular kind of time. The résumé doesn’t just list what you did. It traces how you became someone who can do it. The dissertation isn’t just an argument – it’s a record of initiation. When the output can be produced without the process, the entire evaluation architecture starts to wobble. Not because the work is bad. Because the work is unreadable in the way these institutions were designed to read it.

That’s the real crisis. Not quality – legibility.

For generations, professional credentialing systems have been built around process as much as outcome. You prove you belong by demonstrating you did it the hard way. The bar exam. The dissertation. The portfolio. The byline history. These aren’t just measures of competence – they’re proof of initiation. You went through the thing. You earned the right to be evaluated by people who have also gone through it.

AI breaks that. Not because it makes the work worse, but because it decouples the outcome from the initiation ritual. And institutions that built their authority on administering the ritual don’t quite know what to do when the ritual becomes optional. So they call it fraud. They reach for the em dash as evidence. What they’re actually saying is: we don’t know how to evaluate you anymore, and that frightens us, and it’s easier to disqualify you than to admit we need new frameworks.

Meanwhile, the anxiety within the knowledge worker is entirely its own thing. The lawyer who used AI to draft the first pass of a brief and then spent three hours making it actually good. The teacher who generated a rubric outline and then rewrote every line of it to fit her kids. The consultant asked Claude to summarize 40 pages of research so he could spend his hours on insight rather than synthesis. The marketer who hasn’t written an unassisted first draft in two years and hasn’t told anyone.

The anxiety underneath it all is the same: If I use AI to do part of this, am I still the one doing it? Does the work still count? Do I still count?

We’re asking an identity question and calling it an ethics question. They’re not the same thing.

The ethics question is real and worth asking: Are you transparent about your process? Are you submitting AI output as your own thinking when it isn’t? Are you using the tool to avoid the hard work or to do harder work? Those matter. Answer them honestly, and you’ll know where you stand.

Hence, my mea culpa in this.

But the identity question – do I still count – that one’s a trap. Nobody asks whether a surgeon “really” performed the operation if she used robotic assistance. Nobody asks whether an architect “really” designed the building if he used CAD. The tool receded into the background because we had time to build consensus about what the human’s irreplaceable contribution actually was. The judgment. The training. The accountability. The call.

We haven’t built that consensus yet for knowledge work and AI. We’re in the uncomfortable middle, where the tools are here but the frameworks for thinking about them aren’t. So we’re improvising – some people pretending the tools don’t exist, some people pretending the tools are the work, and most people somewhere in the anxious middle, quietly using them and not quite saying so.

What I know from my own work – in coaching, in leadership consulting, in writing – is that the thing worth paying for was never the production of the output. It was the thinking behind it. The pattern recognition was built over years spent in rooms where hard decisions were made. The ability to ask the question that reframes the whole problem. The judgment about which of the ten possible paths is the right one for this person, this organization, this moment.

AI cannot do that. It can serve it. There’s a version of every knowledge worker’s job that gets better with these tools – more bandwidth for the irreplaceable parts, less time lost to the parts that were always just logistics.

The gatekeepers aren’t protecting quality.

They’re protecting legibility.

They know how to evaluate work made the old way. Work made with new tools, in new combinations, by people who don’t fit the traditional mold – that’s harder to categorize. And what can’t be categorized can’t be controlled. So it gets called fraudulent instead.

That’s the Gilded Age move, isn’t it? New money, new technologies, new methods of building something, and an old guard insisting the new arrivals don’t deserve what they’ve made because they made it differently. The anxiety was never really about standards. It was about who gets to set them.

That’s not a standard. That’s a tell.

I’m not a master craftsman of literary fiction. I know that. I was a dyslexic kid who couldn’t diagram a sentence, and I still can’t. But I had a story, and I told it, and I used every honest tool available to tell it better.

The em dash was mine.

The work was mine.

So here’s what I want to know:

Are you using AI as a tool, avoiding it entirely, or somewhere in the anxious middle – quietly using it and not quite saying so?

The comments are open. I promise not to check your em dashes.
