The Humanities Were Never Soft

Matt Goode29 March 20266 min read Get new pieces by email

# Schools

Five lessons. All of them are described as skills. None of them actually are skills.

Rolling Stone ran a piece this week making the case that business schools don’t teach what leadership actually requires. Narrative intelligence. Moral imagination. Tolerance for ambiguity. Deep listening. Systems thinking through human context. The list is right. The framing is the problem.

Here is that original piece.

Calling these things skills is how we keep mishandling them – treating them as optional, acquirable, subject to a two-day offsite and a Kirkpatrick Level 2 evaluation. A skill is something you acquire. Infrastructure is something you build into – something that holds weight even when you’re not consciously operating it.

These aren’t skills. They’re formation. And formation is different.

I have a sociology degree from LSE. I spent twenty years in classrooms and several more in leadership development within a federal humanitarian agency – rooms where people made decisions under pressure that most of us will never feel.

The capacities the Rolling Stone piece describes? I didn’t learn them in those rooms. I used them. They were already structural. Already load-bearing. The distinction matters because it changes what we think can be done about any of this.

What follows isn’t a counter-list. The RS piece had five items; I’m not replacing them with five better ones – and I’m not claiming the list is complete. These are lessons. Not exhaustive, not exclusive. Formation in the humanities tends to produce them, but the point isn’t the taxonomy. The point is that a lesson is different from a skill. A skill is something you acquire. A lesson is something that changes the structure of how you see. One gets installed. The other gets built in. And built-in is different.

Let’s walk through them – not to validate them, but to show what they actually do inside an organization.

Narrative intelligence is the capacity to understand that every decision is a story, and that stories land emotionally before they land logically. A leader with this capacity doesn’t just communicate clearly – they author coherence. They give people a frame for why the change matters and who they are inside it. Without it, culture runs cold. People execute without meaning. They comply without committing. Most communication training teaches delivery. Narrative intelligence is about what you’re actually building when you speak.

Moral imagination is the ability to see the ethical dimension of a decision early, before the compromise is made and the cost is accumulating. Culture crises don’t arrive overnight. They’re the compounded result of small exceptions, each of which seemed locally reasonable. A leader without moral imagination makes every one of those exceptions and calls it pragmatism. The humanities build this through the uncomfortable discipline of asking: who benefits, who’s invisible, what does this cost that we’re not counting. That’s not softness. That’s foresight with a longer horizon.

Tolerance for ambiguity is what lets a leader make a good decision when the information is incomplete, the map is wrong, and the terrain is changing. Most leaders who appear decisive are actually just intolerant of ambiguity. Confidence is a coping mechanism, not a capability. Literature rarely resolves cleanly. Neither does a scaling organization. The leaders who can hold uncertainty without collapsing into false clarity – or frozen inaction – are running a different internal architecture than the ones who can’t.

Deep listening is not active listening, which is a technique. It’s the interpretive muscle built by years of learning to notice what’s unsaid – tone, structure, silence, contradiction. The leader who hears fatigue before burnout, resentment before revolt, and innovation before it’s fully formed isn’t reading minds. They’ve developed the same attentional discipline a novelist uses to build a character. They know that what’s on the surface is never the whole story. Most organizations dramatically overvalue assertiveness. The ones that survive disruption tend to have someone in the room who can actually hear what’s happening.

Systems thinking through human context is the capacity to understand that culture determines behavior, behavior determines results, and that nothing in an organization is as isolated as it appears. The humanities teach this through history, philosophy, the long arc – they situate decisions in time and consequence. It’s the leadership stance most people never shift into, because it requires the uncomfortable admission that the system they built might be the problem.

Five lessons. None of them “teachable” in a twelve-week program. And none of them end of the list – just the five the RS piece named that are worth taking seriously.

Which creates an obvious problem.

Because by the time someone is in a management role, the formation has either happened or it hasn’t. You cannot install moral imagination in a workshop. You cannot build tolerance for ambiguity with a competency model. That formation takes years – difficult texts, uncomfortable seminars, the particular discipline of being wrong in front of people who are paying attention.

But formation and infrastructure are not the same thing. You can’t give someone twenty years of difficult texts at 35. What you can do is build systems that make those capacities possible – even in people who didn’t come up through the humanities. The formation happened, or it didn’t. The infrastructure is still yours to design: systems that reward moral imagination rather than punish it, decision frameworks that create space for ambiguity rather than demand premature resolution, cultures that make deep listening structurally safe.

That’s not retrofitting the person. That’s designing the conditions.

The Rolling Stone piece closes with a line worth keeping. “The future will belong to leaders who can calculate and contemplate.”

It’s right. But the way we’ll get there isn’t by adding contemplation to the curriculum. The organizations struggling most right now built their leadership infrastructure on a monoculture – one kind of knowing, one kind of formation, one framework for what rigor looks like.

The humanities produce a different kind of rigor. Slower, less legible, harder to certify. But it’s the rigor that holds when the situation has no precedent, the data is incomplete, and the human cost of a wrong decision is real and irreversible.

That’s not soft. That’s load-bearing.

I’m not arguing that every executive needs a literature degree or that philosophy seminars substitute for operational experience. I’m arguing that we’ve systematically excluded a certain kind of knowing from leadership formation – and that exclusion has a cost we’ve been paying in slow motion for twenty years. Trust deficits. Culture failures. Leaders who can optimize a system but cannot read it.

The humanities teach you to read.

Not text. Reality.

In a world moving faster than any playbook can track, the leader who can feel the moral temperature of a room – who can sense the fragile architecture of trust before it fractures – is doing something that can’t be automated and can’t be taught in a weekend.

What it can be is designed around.

One of my favorite books is Plato’s Republic. Not because it’s comforting – it isn’t. But it keeps asking the question most organizations never ask long enough to answer: what does it actually mean for something to function well? There’s an entire essay in that. I’ll be back on Tuesday.
