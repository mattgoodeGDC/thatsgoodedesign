What Holds Under Load

Matt Goode28 June 20269 min read Get new pieces by email

# Winning feels like a near miss# CREATE

Formation doesn’t show up on a resume, survive an interview, or pass a test. That isn’t because it can’t be measured. It’s because you’re using the wrong instrument.

The last essay ended where this one has to begin. The judgment your organization needs is already inside it, sitting in people the system was built to overlook, and the instruments you’d use to find them can’t see them. So this is the essay about the instruments: why they’re broken, what a working one looks like, and a concession the philosopher N. Ángel Pinillos made along the way that he didn’t have to.

Here’s the concession. Pinillos splits the humanities into two cases and keeps only one. There’s the skills case, verifiable, material, backed by grading and standardized-test data, and the plain fact that philosophy majors outscore nearly everyone on the GRE verbal. And there’s the formative case, the intrinsic, soul-deep transformation the philosopher Jennifer Frey spent a whole New York Times interview defending. Pinillos believes in the second one. He says so directly: he has spent his life inside it. But he hands it back to faith. The intrinsic argument, he writes, tells you to take it on trust, and the data tells you that you don’t have to. So he builds his public case on the measurable half and lets the other half go, because he thinks it can’t be proven, and he’s tired of losing arguments that depend on it.

I understand why. But he gave away the most important thing in the room, and he gave it away because he accepted a premise he should have questioned. That formation is unverifiable.

It isn’t. It’s verifiable. Everyone in this argument, Frey, Pinillos, and the company running its promotion cycle next quarter, is trying to measure it with the wrong instrument.

The instrument they’re all reaching for is the test. A test – and grades – is a demonstration on command, great if you are measuring skills. You set a standard in advance, you ask the person to perform against it, and an evaluator who already holds the answer key scores the result. That’s what the GRE measures. That’s what a graded paper measures, mostly. And it works, for skill. Skill is precisely the thing that shows up on command, because skill is acquired and demonstrable, and a test is built to catch exactly that. Notice that all of Pinillos’s evidence is of this kind: test scores, graded essays, and LSAT placement. Of course it is. He’s writing from inside the academy, and the test is the instrument the academy owns. It’s the right tool for his room. It just doesn’t travel to yours.

Formation doesn’t show up on command. That’s not a flaw in it; it’s the definition of it. Formation, the slow remaking of how a person sees and responds, is the architecture that’s there when there’s no command: no standard set in advance, no answer key, no precedent, no time to rehearse the performance. It’s what the Roman emperor Marcus Aurelius was circling when he wrote that you don’t rise to the occasion, you fall to the level of your preparation. I spent a whole essay on that line, on the interior architecture a leader builds in the ordinary moments. You can read it here. “What Are You Practicing?”

Formation is that architecture, and the thing available to you under pressure is whatever you built before it arrived. That isn’t a skill you perform. It’s a structure you stand on, and you only find out it’s there when the weight comes down. You can’t catch it with a test, for the same reason you can’t inspect a bridge by asking it to describe itself. You have to put weight on it.

Pinillos looked at the formation, saw that it didn’t survive a test, and concluded it can’t be measured. The right conclusion is that it can’t be tested. Those are different. A thing can be entirely real, entirely verifiable, and invisible to the one instrument you happened to bring.

There’s another instrument. I described it in the essay on creativity without flagging it as the answer to this. It’s the difference between a test and a Showcase. A test asks you to reproduce what’s already known. A Showcase asks you to make something that didn’t exist before you made it, in front of a real audience whose reactions you can’t script, where the frame has to be authored in the moment, and originality can’t be pre-scored because no one wrote the rubric in advance. The test verifies reproduction. The Showcase verifies generation. And generation, the capacity to build the frame before you optimize inside it, is formation made visible.

In the work I do, the Showcase has a less academic name. Solo Drive. It’s the last phase of a three-part progression, and the progression is the whole point. First, the Ride-Along, where the experienced person drives and the learner watches, and the work is to demystify the judgment, to say out loud the tacit calls a veteran makes without noticing they’re making them. Then the Learner’s Permit, where the learner drives and you coach from the passenger seat, close enough to catch a real mistake. Then Solo Drive: full license, real stakes, no one else in the car. That last phase isn’t a graduation ceremony. It’s the load test. You find out what someone is made of by watching what holds when you finally get out.

You already know what this looks like, because you’ve watched people reveal it and watched people fail to. It looks like how someone behaves when they’re wrong in public, whether they get curious or get defensive. It looks like what happens to the people around them over a year, whether those people grow or quietly shrink to fit. It looks like someone in the meeting questions the frame before everyone rushes to solve inside it. None of those are interview questions. You can’t ask them in thirty minutes. They’re observation questions, and they only answer themselves under load, over time, in conditions you have to deliberately build.

Which is exactly what most organizations refuse to build. This is the problem I named through Plato a few weeks back, and it’s worth naming plainly now. Plato’s philosopher-king problem is that the people most fit to lead are the least likely to be chosen, because the system rewards the appetite for the role over the capacity to hold it. Read about that here, “The Philosopher-King Problem.” It’s a description of your promotion process, and the reason it misfires is mechanical. Your promotion is a test: a thirty-minute interview, a calibration meeting, a performance review scored against a plan set in advance. Every one of those measures the demonstrable, the thing that shows up on command. So your system reliably verifies skill and reliably misses formation. It promotes the person who arrives with the answer, performs confidently, and signals ambition, because those are the legible signals, the ones that survive a calibration meeting.

Here’s the distinction that promotion blurs, and you’ve already watched it play out at full tragic scale. The Iliad, I argued a few weeks ago, is the oldest management failure on record, and the failure is this exact one. Agamemnon holds the title: the authority, the command, the standing. Achilles holds the capacity: he’s the one whose judgment changes the outcome. The army runs for ten years on the fiction that those are the same thing, that the person with the title is the person whose judgment matters most, and the whole poem is what happens when the fiction breaks. Read about that one here, “The Oldest Management Failure on Record.”

A promotion grants a Title, which is final accountability, handed over on command, in a meeting, on a Tuesday. It can’t grant the License, the operational ownership that only proves out in operation. You can give someone the title of VP and find out two quarters later that the license was never held, that the authority was real, but the judgment underneath it was never load-tested. Homer drew you the worst case. Patroclus puts on Achilles’ armor, the symbols of an authority the system never transferred to him, the Trojans see the armor and answer the title, and the gap between the title and the capacity is the exact place Hector kills him. That’s the underprepared promotion in its oldest form, the role without the transfer. The handoff that works moves real weight to a person in stages and watches what they do with it, long before the title is on the door. Not what they say in the room. What holds when the weight is theirs.

It gets sharper. The person most likely to question the frame before solving inside it is often the one the pipeline already flagged as difficult – the generative cognitive style, read one more time as a problem to manage rather than a capacity to find. Build the instrument to catch formation under load instead of performance on command, and you stop screening that person out. An instrument tuned to the mind your old one overlooked sees judgment better in everyone.

So the work is not another defense of the humanities, though this clearly is; sorry, not sorry. The humanities don’t need defending; they need an organization with a working instrument. And a working instrument is not a better test. It’s a different kind of thing: a leadership operating system that makes capacity visible the way it moves, through the decisions people make, the gears they shift, the way authority travels through the company over time. It designs the load-bearing moments on purpose, watches who holds and who doesn’t, and refuses to mistake the performance of confidence for the substance of judgment.

And here’s the part I’ve been holding back. I said formation can’t be installed. That’s true. The two-day offsite was never going to deliver it. But an organization, of all the institutions left standing, still has what formation requires: time, stakes, and load.

This is why the person you grew tends to outperform the person you bought. Not because internal candidates are smarter, or more loyal, or a safer bet. Because they have already been under your load, and held. An external hire arrives with a resume, which is a record of outcomes. The internal candidate arrives with a formation, which is a record of how they behave when the outcome is still in doubt. One of those is far harder to fake, and far more useful when the next hard thing lands.

So the investment that looks slow – growing your own bench, is the one compounding underneath everything else. Every stretch assignment, every owned failure, every decision made without you in the room is a deposit. You don’t see the return on the day you promote someone. You see it for the first time when they hold something you would have had to hold yourself.

You can’t send your people back to school for the formation they missed. Though I suspect you can nurture it by encouraging a learning culture. You can build the one environment still capable of forming it. That isn’t a training program. It’s infrastructure, and it’s the most valuable thing you can build in the decade the machine just handed us.

Three essays, one argument. The capacities the humanities build are not skills; they are formation, and the lever you hold is the infrastructure you build around and into your people. Formation is now the scarce input on every kind of knowledge work, because the institutions that should supply it were built to manufacture its opposite. The shortage feels unfixable only because you’ve been hunting for formation with an instrument built to find skill – measuring what shows up on command, missing what shows up under load.

Frey was right that education is formation. Pinillos was right that you can’t win the public on faith. They were both missing the same thing. Formation can be verified, and it can be built. Not on a resume. Not in an interview. Not on a test.

Under load. Over time.

That’s not soft. It’s the only place it was ever going to show.

Three questions to sit with this week:

  • Who in your organization holds the title without the license, and how would you know?

  • Where could you move real weight to someone in stages, and watch what holds?

  • What judgment are you screening out at the interview that would show itself under load?

CLUTCH is the operating system I built for this, the infrastructure that forms judgment the way judgment is really formed: under weight, over time, with the handoff made on purpose. Marcus Aurelius wrote his way toward the same idea twenty centuries ago. You don’t rise to the occasion. You fall to the level of what you built before it.
