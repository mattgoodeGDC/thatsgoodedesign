The Clarity Move: Name the Mess First

Matt Goode4 October 20253 min read Get new pieces by email

# 6C

One Clear Idea
You can’t lead your team out of chaos if you haven’t named the kind you’re in.

Leadership doesn’t fall apart when things get messy.
It falls apart when leaders treat every mess the same.

That’s where the VUCA lens comes in.

Originally coined by the military, VUCA breaks down four distinct kinds of uncertainty:

  • Volatile – fast-moving and unstable

  • Uncertain – lacking predictability

  • Complex – full of moving parts

  • Ambiguous – hard to interpret or define

Each one requires a different leadership move. But most leaders skip the first - and most crucial - step: naming the storm before trying to calm it.

This week, I worked with a team that was spinning its wheels on a significant cross-functional initiative.

It had all the symptoms:

  • Conflicting signals

  • Overlapping roles

  • Friction between departments

  • No clear “why” behind the plan

People were stressed – but no one knew what kind of help to ask for. Then, during a coaching moment, someone paused and said:

“This doesn’t feel chaotic because it’s fast. It feels chaotic because we don’t understand it.”

That one sentence helped the team realize:
This wasn’t volatility. It was ambiguity.
And it needed a different response.

Once they named the mess, their leadership got sharper.

This Week’s Coaching Prompt:

“What kind of VUCA am I facing – and have I named it out loud?”

Here’s how to break it down:

Volatile → “It’s unstable, but we understand it.”

  • Think: sudden changes, shake-ups, staff exits

  • Move: Act fast, stabilize, over-communicate

Uncertain → “We see patterns, but can’t predict.”

  • Think: new markets, policy shifts, unclear reactions

  • Move: Stay curious (had to slip in a Ted Lasso reference), gather data, create small experiments

Complex → “Too many variables, too intertwined.”

  • Think: multi-team projects, global initiatives

  • Move: Simplify, clarify roles, align cross-functionally

Ambiguous → “We don’t even know what we’re solving.”

  • Think: team tension, unclear strategy, foggy goals

  • Move: Name what’s missing, ask meaning-making questions, slow down the spin

One Actionable Practice: Run a VUCA Check-In

Take 10 minutes this weekend:

  1. Identify a situation that feels “off” or stuck.

  2. Ask: Which VUCA type is this?

  3. Write down the best leadership posture for that kind of challenge.

  4. Bonus: Bring it to your team. Ask them what they see – and what kind of leadership they need from you.

You’ll be surprised how much clarity comes from that one act of framing.

What Shifts When You Name the Mess

Too often, leaders default to action without first assessing the situation.
When things feel urgent, we speed up. Over-communicate. Hold tighter.

But if the storm you’re facing is complexity or ambiguity, those moves add static. You end up:

  • Making decisions in the wrong key

  • Creating urgency where steadiness is needed

  • Micromanaging when the team needs meaning

Naming the mess doesn’t make it go away.
But it makes it leadable.

It shifts you from reaction to orientation - and that’s a powerful leadership move.

Why This Matters Right Now

We’re not short on chaos.
We’re short on framing.

When leaders name the kind of “hard” they’re in, three things happen:

  1. Teams exhale.
    Naming uncertainty gives people language - and permission - not to have all the answers.

  2. Strategy sharpens.
    You stop treating ambiguity like volatility (or vice versa), and start making context-appropriate choices.

  3. Trust deepens.
    Your team learns: “Even when things are foggy, my leader helps us see the road.”

That’s real leadership.
Not knowing everything. Just helping others make sense of what’s in front of them.

Your Move: Practice Framing Over Fixing

Set aside 5–10 minutes this weekend:

  • Choose a situation that feels complex, slow, or emotionally charged

  • Ask: Is this volatile, uncertain, complex, or ambiguous?

  • Share that diagnosis with a peer or teammate – and watch the conversation shift

You don’t need a plan yet.
You need a shared map.

Because your team can handle turbulence.
They just need to know which storm they’re in - and that you see it too.

Forward this to someone leading through fog. Better yet, print it and bring it to your next leadership meeting.

You don’t need to control the chaos.
You just need to name it first.
