The open door is a lottery

Why the habits that made a small team fast start making it slow around 50

Matt Goode19 October 20265 min read Get new pieces by email

# Growing faster than it's getting clearer# The culture is drifting

At ten people, you don’t have to tell anyone anything. They were in the room.

At ten, there are 45 possible one-to-one conversations in the company. You’re part of most of them. A decision gets made on Tuesday, and by Wednesday lunch everyone knows. Nobody announced it. The building is just small enough that news travels on its own.

At fifty, there are 1,225.

Nobody can hold 1,225 relationships. Not you, not your best operator, not the person at the front desk who seems to know everything. So information stops moving because people are around, and starts moving by rumor, by proximity, and by whoever happened to be in the kitchen. Fred Brooks made this point about software teams in 1975. When you add people, the communication load doesn’t grow in a straight line. It grows in a curve. The arithmetic hasn’t changed since.

Why the number keeps coming up

I’m wary of magic numbers. (Confession: I’m writing another book, Seven: the biography of a number. See As You Like It. IYKYK.) Sorry, back. Nothing happens at exactly 50 that didn’t happen at 47. But several unrelated lines of evidence land in the same neighborhood, and when that happens I pay attention.

Robin Dunbar, the Oxford anthropologist who first proposed 150 as the limit on stable relationships, also found that human relationships cluster in layers of roughly 5, 15, 50 and 150. Each layer is about three times the size of the one before, and closeness drops at every step. The 50 layer is people you know but don’t see often. Grow past it, and the founder has quietly moved most of the company into the outer ring. People still say hello to you. They no longer know what you’re thinking.

The law notices too. In the US, family leave, the Affordable Care Act’s employer mandate and, for federal contractors, a written affirmative action plan for workers with disabilities all start at 50. In France, so many labor rules kick in at 50 that economists Luis Garicano, Claire Lelarge, and John Van Reenen found more than twice as many firms with 49 employees as with 50: companies choosing not to grow rather than cross the line.

So the claim isn’t “exactly 50.” It’s around 50. Somewhere past 40, the way you’ve been running things starts costing more than it gives.

What breaks first

Two habits break first, and they break together.

The first is the open door. Every founder is proud of it: anyone can walk in, any time. At ten people, that’s a gift. At fifty, it’s a lottery. The people who use it are already close, sit nearby, or feel confident enough to knock. Everyone else gets their information secondhand.

The second is word-of-mouth updates. At ten, telling three people is telling everyone. At fifty, telling three people starts a rumor, and by the time it reaches the far end of the building, someone has edited it at every stop.

The handbook answer is the familiar one: write it down, hold a regular all-hands, publish a weekly update, open a channel. Do all of that. None of it is wrong. The founder-mode answer is to keep the door open and walk the floor more. That isn’t wrong either.

Both miss that the open door was never a communication system. It was you. You were the hub, and the company ran on how close people sat to you. A newsletter doesn’t change that. If you keep making real decisions in the doorway and announcing them later, the all-hands becomes the place where people learn what the insiders knew last week.

I call this keeping the license. The all-hands has the title: it’s officially where decisions get shared. You’ve kept the license: the real decision still happens wherever you happen to be standing. Everyone can tell. You’ll see this pattern again every day this week.

Closing the door isn’t the fix either. I once had a new boss who rarely left her office and kept her door shut on principle. She didn’t last.

The shift is in you, not the org chart

Around 50, the leader’s job changes from being available to being deliberate. Being available means: come and find me. Being deliberate means: I’ll tell you first, myself, and then I’ll tell you again.

Founders hate that second telling. It feels redundant. You said it in the leadership meeting and mentioned it in Slack, so surely people know. They don’t. By the time you’re sick of saying something, the far end of the company is just hearing it for the first time, usually from someone who heard it from someone else.

Heads of school and nonprofit directors hit the same wall: the building got bigger, but the habit didn’t change.

The move this week

Each issue this week ends with one page of a five-page audit. Keep the pages together. Friday’s page uses the other four.

Page one. Take the last significant decision you made: a hire, a change in priorities, a budget call, anything people would care about.

Make two lists.

  1. Who heard about it directly from you, before they heard it anywhere else?

  2. Who heard about it secondhand, or found out because they noticed?

Be honest about the second list. If you’re not sure how someone found out, put them on it.

Then look at what separates the two lists. I’d bet it isn’t seniority or need-to-know. It’s distance: who sits near you, who you already talk to, who knocked.

That’s your open door, measured. Most leaders find the first list is shorter than they expected, and that the people on the second list are the ones who most needed to hear it from them. Keep both lists. That’s page one.
