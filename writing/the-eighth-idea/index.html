The Eighth Idea

Experts ranked teacher coaching eighth of thirty ways to fix schools. That’s why the top two work.

Matt Goode30 September 20267 min read Get new pieces by email

# Schools# Coaching

Thirty-seven education researchers ranked thirty ways to fix American schools. Then they added a warning: choosing the right idea isn’t enough. None of them work unless schools carry them out well. Duh.

That caveat, tucked into the New York Times ranking last week, is its most important finding. Nobody needs another chart to know American students are slipping, and nobody needs another pilgrimage to Finland to come home with more recess. The Times asked a more useful question: of the things American schools could actually do, what works? Small-group tutoring and the science of reading came out on top, with high-quality preschool, research-backed curriculum, and school accountability also rated highly.

Coaching for teachers came in eighth. I think it belongs on a different list altogether. Coaching isn’t a rival to tutoring or the science of reading, competing for the same budget line. It’s how either of them actually works in a classroom, which is the part the experts said matters most.

We already know this everywhere else. When you want to get into shape, you don’t just buy a gym membership. You hire a trainer. When an executive wants to lead better, they hire a coach. I’m one of those coaches now. Before that, I spent twenty years in schools as a teacher and administrator, which is why I keep coming back to education here. From both sides, the pattern never changes: knowing what works was never the hard part. Doing it well, week after week, with someone watching and adjusting alongside you, is.

Schools have been learning this slowly for a long time. Every wave of reform since A Nation at Risk in 1983, from No Child Left Behind to the post-COVID relief money meant to reinvent school, changed the what: new standards, new tests, new curricula. Almost none changed the how. My friend Jennifer Smith, who has led change in schools for years, draws the line cleanly in her essayYour Initiative Isn’t the Change: an initiative is not change. The initiative is the goal. Change is the slow, collaborative work of getting there, and it’s the part schools keep skipping.

A program is not a practice

The science of reading is a body of evidence about how children learn to decode. Schools learned the hard way what happens when the evidence and the classroom drift apart. For two decades, Lucy Calkins’s Units of Study trained thousands of teachers to have young readers guess at words from pictures and context, a habit the research says belongs to struggling readers. After APM’s Sold a Story brought that to national attention, Calkins revised her materials, Teachers College dissolved her project, and districts including New York City moved away from her approach.

Here’s the part that rarely gets said. Swapping the binder doesn’t swap the habits. A teacher who spent fifteen years prompting “look at the picture” doesn’t unlearn it over a summer. The science of reading becomes a practice only when a second-grade teacher, with twenty-four kids and a fire drill at 10:40, runs a phonics block with fidelity, notices who is stalling, and adjusts. Tutoring is the same. It’s a structure. What happens inside the forty minutes is craft.

Schools usually close that gap with training. A summer workshop, a webinar series, a day of in-service. Then everyone goes back to their classroom alone, and within weeks the new method has eroded into the old habit.

That’s not a teacher problem. It’s a design problem.

Why eighth undersells it

Coaching is the mechanism that turns a program into a practice. In 2018, Matthew Kraft, David Blazar and Dylan Hogan pooled 60 rigorous studies of teacher coaching, and the results are easiest to see if you picture a line of 100 teachers, ranked from weakest to strongest. An average teacher stands at number 50. After coaching, that same teacher moves up to about number 69. That’s roughly the jump from a first-year teacher to one with five to ten years in the classroom, gained without waiting five to ten years.

Now picture 100 students lined up the same way. A typical student in a coached teacher’s class moves from number 50 to about number 57. That may sound modest, but by the researchers’ own yardstick, it sits near the top of what any single change in a school usually delivers. Much of that evidence comes from literacy coaching in the early grades, exactly where the science of reading lives.

Next to tutoring, that student gain looks smaller, and that may be why coaching landed eighth. But the experts scored it as a standalone program. Scored as the thing that makes the top two work, it looks very different. Tutoring reaches the few students at the table. A coached teacher reaches every student she will ever teach.

Even Google had to learn this. A company run by engineers set out to prove its managers didn’t matter, assuming technical skill was what counted. Its own data said otherwise. At the top of its list of what the best managers do: being a good coach. And being a brilliant engineer didn’t make someone good at it. The same is true in schools. Coaching is a different craft from teaching. A teacher who can hold a room of twenty-four seven-year-olds may have no idea how to sit beside a colleague, watch a lesson, and help them see what to change. That skill has to be chosen for, taught, and practiced like any other.

Last week I asked Leadership Lab readers what leadership problem is taking up the most space on their desk. One answer named this essay’s argument better than I could: “Reclaiming the professionalism of the profession of teaching.” Professionals don’t get a workshop. They get watched closely by someone who knows the work, and they get better because of it.

Why it fades at scale

Here’s the catch. The same research found that coaching works best small. In pilot programs with a handful of carefully chosen coaches, the gains were large. When districts rolled coaching out to hundreds of teachers, the gains shrank to a fraction. Other studies find the same story one coach at a time: some coaches change how teachers teach, and some barely move the needle.

That’s not a sign coaching doesn’t work. It’s the fingerprint of an infrastructure failure. The idea holds up. The system built around it doesn’t. Districts hire coaches, then pull them onto testing schedules and lunch duty. One coach ends up responsible for sixty teachers. They were picked for being great teachers, not great coaches, and nobody taught them the difference. With no single focus and no follow-up visit on the calendar, coaching turns into a pleasant chat that changes nothing.

Before the next idea

Kraft, who was one of the experts the Times surveyed, put it plainly: the real lesson isn’t which ideas scored highest. It’s how schools decide.

Schools are very good at deciding. Every summer, somewhere, a leadership team picks a new reading program, a new math curriculum, a new tutoring model. The August in-service is built around it. By spring, the idea that was going to change everything sits on a shelf next to the last one.

The ideas weren’t wrong. Most of them were on the list for good reason. What was missing was the person whose job it was to carry the idea into the classroom, stay beside the teachers using it, and make sure it was still working in October, not just announced in August. That’s not a curriculum decision. It’s a leadership decision, and it’s the one most schools never make.

That’s why the eighth idea matters more than its ranking. The first seven tell you what to do. The eighth explains how to do any of them.

So before your school adopts the next idea on the list, ask two questions. Who will carry it into the classroom? And have you given them the time and the skill to do it well?
