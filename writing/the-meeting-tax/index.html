The Meeting Tax

Matt Goode8 April 20264 min read Get new pieces by email

# Winning feels like a near miss

Your organization lost approximately 12 hours this week to a cost that doesn’t appear on any balance sheet. That’s five people, one shadow conversation per day, thirty minutes each, across a five-day week. It was processed in a parking lot, a Slack thread, and someone’s kitchen at dinner.

No one tracked those hours. No one will.

It’s 5:47 pm and Sarah is sitting in her car in the parking garage, phone out, typing the message she didn’t send from the conference room. Not because she forgot. Because the room didn’t feel like the place. Eleven minutes earlier, in the standup, she said “sounds good.”

She means neither word.

The Slack DM goes to the one colleague she trusts to read it without it becoming a thing. Her colleague will respond. They’ll reach a conclusion. It will inform what they both do tomorrow. None of it will appear in the meeting notes.

This pattern isn’t isolated. It’s a system.

The parking lot debrief, the Slack thread that opens eleven minutes after the standup, the dinner conversation that ends with a spouse knowing more about the organizational dysfunction than the leader does – these aren’t random. They’re the same channel, running in parallel, processing what the formal one wouldn’t.

This isn’t disloyalty. It’s architecture failure.

When formal channels don’t process what they need to, informal channels adapt. Every time, in every organization, without exception. This isn’t a character flaw in your team. It’s a design outcome. The shadow channel exists because the formal one left something unprocessed, and the organization found another way to carry it.

Keith Davis mapped this dynamic in his 1953 Harvard Business Review paper “Management Communication and the Grapevine” and found that shadow channels weren’t random gossip systems. They were highly structured, often more accurate than formal communications, and almost always filling a specific gap the formal system had created. The question organizational anthropologists keep asking isn’t how to eliminate shadow channels. It’s what gap they’re filling.

Dual-channel organizations pay twice for the same work.

The formal channel runs meetings, produces agendas, generates notes. The informal channel runs the actual decision-making, surfaces the real dissent, builds the alignment the formal channel couldn’t produce. Decisions made in the parking lot don’t appear in the meeting notes. Dissent processed in a Slack DM disappears when the thread does. The informal channel carries real organizational work without any of the accountability structures of the formal one – no record, no shared understanding, no way to know if what got decided actually held.

Three failure modes reliably produce shadow channels, and if you’re honest, you’ll recognize at least one of them in your last month of meetings.

The first is the meeting where dissent isn’t safe. The room reads the leader’s body language, calibrates to what’s wanted, and produces agreement. The parking lot produces the actual response. Amy Edmondson’s research on psychological safety documented this pattern precisely: teams in low-safety environments don’t stop having the real conversation – they relocate it. What looks like alignment in the room is often compliance, and compliance routes around the formal channel the moment the meeting ends. In CLUTCH’s 6C framework, this is what Command mode overstay produces: an authority gradient so steep that accurate dissent can’t surface where it would actually be useful.

The second is the agenda that never includes the real issue. Every organization has a topic that’s been on the informal agenda for months and never appeared on a formal one – the restructure nobody’s named, the performance issue everyone’s working around, the strategic question the leader hasn’t opened. When the formal channel consistently avoids what the organization most needs to process, the informal one picks it up by default.

The third is the leader who signals they’ve already decided. The question is asked. The room knows the answer is predetermined. The meeting becomes theater, and the shadow channel becomes the place where people process what it felt like to sit through it.

Each failure mode produces a different shadow channel. Together they tell you exactly what the formal system is failing to do.

Before your next all-hands, one question:

What would your team say in the parking lot after this meeting that they won’t say in the room?

If you can answer it, you already know where the formal channel is failing. If you can’t, that’s the more important answer.

Shadow channel audit – three signals:

  1. The signal that people agreed in the room but recalibrated in execution maps to the first failure mode: dissent isn’t safe, and compliance isn’t commitment.

  2. The signal that the same topic keeps surfacing informally but never formally maps to the second: the agenda is consistently avoiding what the organization most needs to process.

  3. The signal that your team stops asking questions once a direction is set maps to the third: the leader signaled the outcome before the room opened, and the meeting was theater.

If you recognize more than one, the formal channel has more than one gap.

You already know the topic. The one your team has been processing informally for months that has never appeared on a formal agenda.

Tell me which failure mode is producing it. I read every response.
