2. Safety Isn’t Soft

Essay 2 of 5

Matt Goode18 November 202511 min read Get new pieces by email

# Psychological safety

Why trust is your system’s most underrated accelerant

Not long ago, I sat with a senior leadership team at a federal agency. Brilliant people. Deeply committed. And carrying the kind of fatigue that comes from holding a mission together while the ground keeps shifting under their feet.

In a quiet moment, the room fell still. A director leaned back, exhaled, and said: We keep telling people everything is fine, but nothing in the system feels stable enough for that to be true.

I felt that sentence land.

Because it captured something I’ve seen across every sector – from crisis operations at USAID to corporate teams navigating transformation, reorgs, and impossible deadlines.

Most leaders say they want trust. Very few design for it.

They talk about psychological safety as if it’s an interpersonal preference – a cultural “nice-to-have,” something to cultivate once the real work is done. They treat it like tone. Like personality. Like the emotional backdrop of the organization.

But trust isn’t a vibe. It’s an input. A system variable. A performance lever hiding in plain sight.

And when leaders overlook that – when they assume trust emerges organically or can be willed into existence – that’s where the damage begins. Not loudly, not dramatically, but systemically. Decision by decision. Meeting by meeting. Missed conversation by missed conversation.

The irony is that most teams aren’t suffering from a lack of goodwill.

They’re suffering from the absence of infrastructure that makes trust operational rather than aspirational.

That’s the real work.
And it’s where most systems quietly fall apart.

Trust is not soft. It’s structural.

Let’s reframe the conversation: psychological safety is not about being nice – it’s about reducing system drag. In every team, there are invisible friction points that slow work down long before anyone notices performance slipping. And trust – or the lack of it – is one of the most significant sources of that friction.

When trust is low, everything takes longer than it should:

  • Teams over-prepare to avoid being wrong.

  • Decisions get delayed, rerouted, or double-checked unnecessarily.

  • Conflict doesn’t disappear; it hides under practiced politeness.

  • Talented people start to shrink their contributions, playing small instead of bold.

Researchers at Harvard, MIT Sloan, and Google have documented this phenomenon. High fear cultures produce decision latency – the measurable delay between when someone notices a risk and when they feel safe enough to name it. And that delay compounds. Not emotionally – operationally.

But when trust is high?

Teams move differently. People ship faster because they aren’t spending emotional bandwidth on impression management. They test ideas in public rather than rehearse them in private. They surface red flags while they’re still small. They self-correct without being prompted. The energy in the system doesn’t leak – it loops.

This isn’t motivational sentiment; it’s what Stephen M.R. Covey calls operational physics. In The Speed of Trust, he argues – and subsequent organizational research supports – that when trust increases, speed goes up and cost goes down. The inverse is equally true, and far more pervasive than most leaders acknowledge.

So if your team feels inexplicably slow, cautious, or heavy despite strong résumés and clear goals, pause before diagnosing a skill gap. Often, what you’re feeling isn’t incompetence – it’s the drag created by a low-safety environment.

The question becomes:

What would move faster if people didn’t feel the need to protect themselves first?

Five Questions to Measure the Trust Pulse

(Adapted in part from Amy Edmondson’s Psychological Safety Index)

Rate yourself or your team (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree):

  1. It is safe to take a risk on this team.

  2. My unique skills and talents are valued.

  3. Members of this team respect and value one another’s contributions.

  4. I can be myself on this team without fear of judgment.

  5. My manager encourages open dialogue, especially during hard conversations.

I’ve also included a simple Google Form – built on Edmondson’s five questions – that you can copy and use to track this dimension of psychological safety with your team. Click here for a copy of that form.

At first glance, these may look like “soft” questions – the kind leaders skim past on engagement surveys. But the data tells a different story. Edmondson’s research at Harvard, Google’s Project Aristotle, and multiple meta-analyses published in Organization Science and Academy of Management Journal consistently show that psychological safety is one of the strongest predictors of learning behavior, adaptive performance, and innovation velocity.

These items aren’t emotional temperature checks; they are leading indicators of how your system will perform under pressure. Low scores tell you that your team is losing energy in places you can’t see: holding back ideas, second-guessing decisions, rehearsing instead of collaborating, or speaking only in “safe” ways that avoid friction. Stanford researcher Jeff Pfeffer calls this phenomenon organizational quieting – the slow retreat from contribution when people believe candor carries a cost.

When these scores dip, it doesn’t mean your people lack courage. It means your system is signaling a design flaw – one that constrains stretch performance long before it affects outcomes.

So before you treat low scores as an HR concern, pause and ask yourself:

What if this isn’t about confidence at all? What if it’s about conditions?

Because when these numbers move, your culture is telling you something. And the leaders who listen early are the ones who never have to clean up the consequences later.

What Project Aristotle proved – and what leaders missed

When Google conducted its landmark study on team effectiveness – the multi-year research initiative known as Project Aristotle – the findings surprised even the researchers. They began with the same assumptions many leaders still hold: that the highest-performing teams share characteristics such as deep technical expertise, strong individual leadership, clearly defined goals, or overlapping personalities. Those variables mattered, but none of them predicted success as reliably as one core condition: psychological safety.

The real differentiator wasn’t IQ, tenure, or talent density. It was whether people felt safe enough to say what others wouldn’t, to admit what they didn’t know, and to surface the small concerns that prevent significant failures. Teams that performed at the top of Google’s analysis weren’t simply competent – they were candid, curious, and willing to be seen.

What’s even more critical – and often missed when leaders cite this study – is that the safety these teams experienced wasn’t accidental. It didn’t emerge from a charismatic manager or a naturally cohesive group. It was intentionally and repeatedly designed through the structures and behaviors of the leaders who shaped the environment.

Every meeting format either reinforced that safety or chipped away at it. Every 1:1 either expanded the space for truth or subtly narrowed it. Every decision cadence – who was consulted, who decided, who was merely informed – signaled something about voice, power, and permission.

This is the systems-level insight most leaders overlook: psychological safety is not a mood; it’s infrastructure. McKinsey’s 2021 Organizational Health research echoes the same conclusion – the highest-performing companies operate within cultures “where dissent is normalized and voice is structurally supported,” not merely encouraged.

So ask yourself:

  • What are your structures teaching people about what is safe to say

  • Where is your system rewarding clarity – and where is it rewarding caution?

  • And if someone on your team were holding a dissenting truth, would your design make it easy or costly for them to speak?

Because at scale, safety doesn’t show up in big gestures.
It shows up in the tiny design choices that tell your people:

Your voice is not an interruption – it’s the operating system.

Real Talk: What happens when trust is missing?

In low-safety systems, performance doesn’t just drop – it distorts. The work still gets done, but not in the way you think. People adapt, but they adapt around the truth instead of through it. And the patterns are remarkably consistent across industries, maturity levels, and organizational types.

Here’s what I’ve observed again and again – and what the research echoes:

People talk more in the hallways than in the meetings.

Decades ago, researcher Chris Argyris called this dynamic “espoused theory vs. theory-in-use” – the difference between what an organization says it values and what people actually feel safe practicing. When psychological safety is low, that gap widens. The real conversations don’t disappear; they relocate. They move to the hallway after the meeting, the group chat without supervisors, the quiet walk to the parking lot, and the “watercooler truth” people share only with those they trust.

Those spaces feel safer – fewer consequences, fewer eyes, fewer risks. But they’re also where the honesty becomes least helpful. Nothing discussed there changes decisions, shapes direction, or improves the work.

That’s the cost Argyris was pointing to:

When candor retreats into the shadows, the organization loses access to the information it needs most.

Meetings start to feel like performance, not participation.

Stanford research on impression management shows that in high-threat environments, people shift their energy away from problem-solving and toward image protection. The meeting becomes a stage, not a workshop. Everyone is speaking, but fewer are contributing.

Conflicts don’t disappear; they go underground or get personal.

Project Aristotle found that high-performing teams weren’t free of conflict – they were free to surface conflict. In low-safety systems, disagreements get sanitized or suppressed, only to reappear later as interpersonal tension, triangulation, or fragmented decision-making.

Team members ask for permission they don’t need.

McKinsey’s Organizational Health Index shows that low-safety climates increase “decision paralysis” – the unnecessary upward delegation of routine choices. People aren’t seeking clarity; they’re seeking protection.

Talented leaders start self-editing, second-guessing, and burning out quietly.

When people don’t feel safe contributing fully, they compensate by overworking, being perfectionistic, and doing emotional labor. Burnout doesn’t always look like collapse; sometimes it seems like quiet compliance.

And here’s the part leaders often misunderstand: The fix isn’t another all-hands about transparency or an inspirational speech about “open culture.” Those might feel good, but they don’t alter the underlying incentives, rituals, or power dynamics that shape behavior.

The fix is systemic – and systems are design problems, not personality problems. You can’t train your way out of hallway truth-telling. But you can redesign meetings, feedback loops, and decision rituals so the real conversations happen in the room, not at the watercooler.

It lives in meeting architecture, decision design, psychological contracts, and leadership consistency. Safety isn’t restored through sentiment – it’s restored through structure.

So ask yourself:

  • Which of these distortions do you recognize – and what might your system be trying to tell you?

  • What conversations would return to the meeting room if your design made them safe again?

Because when safety is missing, the work doesn’t stop.
It just gets more expensive.

Tool in Focus: The Fear Audit

If you want to understand where trust is leaking on your team, don’t start with people. Start with patterns. Psychological safety fails in rituals long before it fails in relationships.

This is a practical audit any team leader can run – quietly, quickly, and without theatrics.

Step 1: Identify Your High-Frequency Rituals

Make a simple list of anything your team does weekly or biweekly: check-ins, standups, project kickoffs, retrospectives, 1:1s, etc.

These routine touchpoints are where fear hides. As McKinsey notes, “high-heat” moments tend to show up in the ordinary, not the dramatic.

Step 2: Ask Three Private Questions

Send a short, private survey or ask in confidential 1:1s:

  • What feels safe to say in this ritual?

  • What do you rehearse before saying?

  • What do you not say – and why?

These questions reveal the internal risk calculations people make but never voice. They’re drawn from Edmondson’s diagnostic work and research on voice suppression in Administrative Science Quarterly.

Don’t summarize. Don’t defend. Just listen.

Step 3: Observe the Design, Not the Dialogue

Next time you run the ritual, pay attention to structure:

  • Who speaks first? It sets the tone for acceptable candor.

  • Who rarely speaks? Don’t assume fear – first ask whether the format favors fast talkers over deep processors. For neurodiverse teammates, real-time participation can privilege speed over thought. The signal isn’t quietness itself – it’s when you’ve created multiple ways to contribute (pre-reads, written input, post-processing channels) and someone still holds back.

  • Is feedback flowing in all directions, or only downward?

  • Are decisions delayed because people are cautious, not strategic?

Project Aristotle made this clear: balanced turn-taking predicts performance more than expertise. These patterns aren’t cosmetic – they’re design signals.

Step 4: Diagnose the Architecture

Ask yourself:

  • What about this ritual invites trust?

  • What unintentionally suppresses it?

  • Where do people speed up, tense up, or check out?

This is where most leaders stop – but this is where the work actually starts. Your goal isn’t to interpret personality; it’s to inspect the container.

Step 5: Redesign One Ritual

Choose one ritual and make a small design shift:

  • Rotate facilitation

  • Add blind input before open dialogue

  • Ask the most junior voice to give the “last word”

  • Add a 60-second “red flags or risks” segment

  • Clarify decision roles (who decides, who gives input, who is informed)

  • Offer a 24-hour “second thoughts” submission option for post-processing thinkers.

You’re not trying to overhaul culture. You’re trying to prove – through structure – that voice is safe here.

Step 6: Ask the Two Questions Leaders Avoid

  • If this ritual were redesigned for courage rather than compliance, what would change first?

  • What would my team say if it were truly safe to tell me the truth?

Those two answers typically reveal the entire system.

The Purpose of a Fear Audit

A Fear Audit isn’t about blame. It’s about finding the places where your system is asking people to protect themselves instead of contributing fully – and then redesigning those spaces so trust can grow again.

Culture shifts at the speed of rituals.
Change those, and the rest follows.

Coaching Reflection:

As a leader, you shape the climate – and your system either scales that or suffocates it. Every choice you make about how time is used, how decisions are made, and how people participate either widens or narrows the space for voice. Research from MIT’s Human Dynamics Lab shows that leaders set the “interaction rhythm” of a team – and that rhythm becomes culture long before anyone names it.

So ask yourself:

Where is my culture slowing down because trust is missing?

Is it in decisions that take too long because people are checking for permission?
Is it in meetings that feel overly polite but underproductive?
Is it in the quiet hesitation you sense before someone offers dissent?

And then narrow the lens:

What’s one ritual I could redesign this quarter to reduce friction and invite voice?

Just one. Not a transformation plan – a recalibration.

Maybe it’s restructuring your stand-up so the most junior voices speak first, a practice supported by research in the Harvard Business Review showing that “status-ordering” is one of the earliest inhibitors of psychological safety.

Maybe it’s shifting 1:1s from status updates to sensemaking conversations. Maybe it’s adding structured turn-taking to key discussions so thinking isn’t dominated by the fastest or most extroverted voice.

Because when you reduce fear, you don’t just create safety – you create speed.

You reduce decision latency.
You shorten the gap between insight and action.
You free your team from the cognitive load of self-protection, allowing them to reallocate that energy toward creativity, problem-solving, and joint ownership.

And the work starts with you – with your willingness to look at the architecture you’ve inherited or unconsciously built, and ask what it’s signaling about who gets to speak, stretch, challenge, or lead.

Leaders don’t create psychological safety through inspiration.

They do it through design.
