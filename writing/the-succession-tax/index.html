The Succession Tax

Matt Goode10 April 20266 min read Get new pieces by email

# My role is changing# Everything runs through me

Your organization lost approximately 14 hours this week to decisions that were held, deferred, or made badly because there was no one authorized to make them well. That’s 28 decisions sitting in a holding pattern, thirty minutes of organizational friction each.

That’s before you count what left with your best person. Gallup’s research on employee turnover places the conservative replacement cost for a senior leader at 100 to 150% of annual salary, once you account for recruiting, onboarding, lost productivity, and the institutional knowledge that doesn’t transfer. As the Transition Tax established, external replacement runs three to five times the cost of developing from within. No one ran either number. No one will.

The Succession Tax doesn’t start when someone leaves.

It starts the moment the organization has no viable answer to a simple question: if the founder stepped back for ninety days, who runs this? Not who would try. Who actually could.

Most founders never ask it. Not because they don’t care – because the question feels like a trap. Naming a number two feels like rehearsing your own replacement. And the rationalization that holds the trap in place is usually this: the organization needs me fully present. That’s true, which is exactly what makes the trap effective. The more the organization needs you, the more logical it feels to stay, and the less space there is to build the infrastructure that would eventually make your constant presence unnecessary.

So the question doesn’t get asked. And the tax runs.

Here’s what it looks like before anyone leaves.

Jonathan has been at the company for four years. He’s the founder’s most trusted operator – the person who can read the room, make the call, hold the thread. In February, the founder takes two weeks off. Not catastrophically: a long-deferred vacation, a real one, phone mostly down.

By day three, Jonathan has a list of eleven decisions he’s holding. Not because he can’t make them. Because he doesn’t know which ones he’s supposed to make, and asking feels like admitting the founder left without a handoff. Two members of the team have quietly stopped bringing him problems – not out of disrespect, but because the informal authority map said every hard thing goes up. The org chart didn’t change. But something contracted.

By day ten, two proposals have missed their windows. One vendor has moved to a competitor – the same vendor Jonathan had spent eight months building a relationship with, the one who’d given the company favorable terms twice because he trusted Jonathan specifically. The founder comes back, clears the queue in a week, and calls it a good trip.

Jonathan puts his resume on LinkedIn six weeks later.

The founder doesn’t connect the two weeks to the six weeks. That’s the design of the tax. The departure reads as a retention problem, a compensation issue, a better offer somewhere else. What it actually was: a man who spent four years building real organizational capacity, discovered in two weeks that the capacity wasn’t structurally his, and drew the only rational conclusion available.

The Succession Tax runs on everything the accounting system can’t see. The eight months of relationship capital that walked out with Jonathan. The institutional memory of why that vendor deal was structured the way it was. Which nobody else fully understood, and which nobody thought to document, because Jonathan was always there.

The talent retention cost is the one nobody runs until it’s too late.

Your best people can see the ceiling from the moment they arrive. They’re not impatient. They’re accurate. When it becomes clear that the trajectory ends at the founder’s shoulder, they leave for somewhere with headroom.

This isn’t disloyalty. It’s arithmetic. And it compounds. The second-best person watches the best person leave, does the same calculation, and arrives at the same answer. The ceiling doesn’t just stop people from rising. It accelerates the rate at which the people most capable of raising it decide to go raise it somewhere else.

Succession isn’t retirement planning. It’s resilience infrastructure.

A number two isn’t a replacement. It’s a redundancy layer. You don’t configure a backup the week the primary fails. You configure it when everything is working, because that’s the only time you have the capacity to do it right.

Building that layer requires a designed progression – not a title change, not a mentorship conversation, but a deliberate expansion of authority in stages. Each stage requires the leader to release something real, and most organizations stall at the first one indefinitely.

The first stage moves the successor from executing your decisions to making their own. Most handoff failures stop here. The authority hasn’t moved – only the task. The successor is still routing every call upward because nobody established what they actually own.

The second stage moves them from consulting you to acting and informing. They make the call, then tell you. Not ask. The shift is small. The effect on organizational capacity is not. This is the stage that requires explicit decision rights – the kind the 6C Decision Compass maps precisely.

The third stage moves them from holding your framework to building theirs. This is where you give up something real: the assumption that your way of reading a situation is the only reliable one. Amy Edmondson’s research is instructive here – people don’t develop independent judgment in environments where exercising it feels risky. If the successor knows you’ll second-guess the call, they’ll keep asking before they make it. The Jonathan scenario is what happens when an organization never gets out of stage one.

Three signals the Succession Tax is running right now:

  1. The two-week test: if you stepped back tomorrow for two weeks – real absence, not checking in – would your team know which decisions to make and which to hold? If the answer requires you to brief anyone before you leave, the infrastructure isn’t there.

  2. The ceiling signal: when you look at your top two or three performers, can you describe a specific path upward for each of them that doesn’t require you to leave? If the path is vague, they’ve already mapped it, and it ends at the door.

  3. The handoff gap: when you’re in a meeting and you’re about to make a call, is there anyone in the room you’d trust to make it without you? Not someone you’re developing – someone who could step in tomorrow without a briefing. If the honest answer is no, you’re a single point of failure, and you’ve been paying the tax since the moment that became true.

You already know whether the tax is running. The two-week test told you. The ceiling signal told you. The handoff gap told you.

The harder question: which signal are you most tempted to explain away?
