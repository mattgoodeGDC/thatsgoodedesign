The Hidden Drag of Fast Teams

Essay 4 of 5

Matt Goode4 December 202510 min read Get new pieces by email

# Growing faster than it's getting clearer

When Speed Backfires

Speed is one of the great seductions of early-stage leadership. In the beginning, it becomes the thing that sets you apart. You spotted an opening in the market before others even recognized it. You moved quickly, built quickly, shipped quickly. You won customers while more established competitors were still workshopping their pitch decks. For a while, that pace didn’t just fuel the company – it defined it. Speed created momentum. Momentum created confidence. And the confidence reinforced an identity: we move fast because we can.

But somewhere along the way – and it almost always happens quietly – the experience of speed begins to shift. What once felt like clean velocity now feels more like noise. Agility becomes unpredictability. People are still active, still producing, still “moving,” but not always in the same direction. Decisions get revisited because they weren’t made with shared context. Priorities lose their edges and start blending into one another. Standups expand, calendars bloat, and the culture that once made progress feel effortless suddenly feels like it’s generating more friction than flow.

And then there’s the personal impact: your own calendar no longer feels fast. It just feels full. Instead of experiencing the thrill of momentum, you experience the weight of managing it. You begin doing the subtle emotional math leaders learn too late – the faster we go, the less aligned we seem to be.

This is the quiet paradox no one warns founders or operators about: the instinct that gets you off the ground is rarely the instinct that scales you. In the early stage, speed is oxygen. But as complexity increases, speed without structure becomes drag. The very force that once propelled the company forward slowly becomes the thing that slows it down – not because people changed, but because the system never evolved to carry the weight of fast motion.

The Hidden Fragility Inside Fast Teams

Speed isn’t the enemy of scale. Unstructured speed is.

In the early days, clarity is almost effortless. Everyone is close enough to share the same pulse and feel the same urgency. Priorities don’t need to be written down because the founder is right there – reinforcing direction through hallway conversations, late-night decisions, and the natural coherence that comes from everyone touching the work at the same time. Alignment happens through proximity, not process.

But as the team expands, that early instinct for speed begins to create its own kind of drag – not because people resist change, but because the system simply can’t absorb fast, uncoordinated action anymore. What used to energize now disorients. What used to accelerate progress now produces rework, confusion, and quiet second-guessing.

This is the predictable pattern MIT Sloan calls “the speed–alignment trap” – the point where organizations are still moving quickly, but no longer moving together. Google’s Project Aristotle saw the same dynamic: teams without shared structure may appear fast, but they erode trust, psychological safety, and consistency over time. And HBR’s studies on scaling organizations echo it clearly: companies that grow successfully don’t slow down – they stabilize their speed through alignment. They turn speed from a personal instinct into a collective rhythm.

Inside a scaling company, unstructured speed looks like this:

  • Teams duplicating effort because priorities weren’t explicit.

  • Leaders shifting direction mid-week without building shared context.

  • Projects launching without success metrics, boundaries, or true ownership.

  • Fire drills becoming the operating model instead of the exception.

None of this signals laziness or disengagement. If anything, it reflects how hard people are trying to keep up. But effort cannot compensate for missing architecture. It is simply the outcome of a system built for immediacy – not for scale.

When decisions move faster than the structure designed to support them, flow collapses and fragility takes its place. The organization keeps moving, but it moves with strain rather than stride – a kind of low-grade organizational drag that leaders feel long before they can name its cause.

The Emotional Cost of Moving Too Fast

Speed without structure eventually breeds anxiety – even in the most capable and well-intentioned teams. People begin to hesitate, not because they lack initiative, but because they don’t want to duplicate effort or unintentionally step on someone else’s work. They wait for clarity that never fully lands. They escalate decisions that should live comfortably at their level. And they pull back from ownership because no one has ever defined what “wrong” actually means – or how reversible a misstep would be.

The impact builds quietly. Trust doesn’t collapse in a dramatic moment; it thins out in subtle ways. Momentum doesn’t disappear overnight; it loses its edge, replaced by a low-grade caution that permeates otherwise strong teams. Leaders feel this before anyone else: they start reviewing work that shouldn’t need their attention, clarifying decisions that should already be understood, stitching together alignment that the system itself should be producing.

And the research is unambiguous. As MIT Sloan, HBR, and the McKinsey Organizational Health Index have shown, companies don’t scale by moving slower or working harder – they scale by converting speed from a founder-driven reflex into a team-wide operating rhythm. High-performing organizations create structural clarity that absorbs complexity so people can move quickly without colliding. In those environments, speed doesn’t create drag; it creates coherence.

This is the real cost of unstructured speed: it forces leaders to carry personally what the organization cannot yet stabilize structurally. The load gets heavier not because the team is incapable or unwilling, but because the architecture underneath them hasn’t evolved to support the pace the company is now trying to sustain.

Design is What Makes Speed Safe

The most effective operators I work with don’t slow their teams down with process – they speed them up by removing the friction that accumulates when people move fast without a shared frame of reference. They understand something McKinsey’s organizational research has demonstrated for more than a decade: companies with clear decision rhythms and explicit ownership make faster, higher-quality decisions than those relying on ad-hoc communication and constant clarification. Predictability isn’t bureaucracy; predictability is acceleration.

These leaders know that consistency is almost always quicker than chaos. They’ve learned, often through experience, that defined swim lanes don’t restrict people – they free them. When expectations are explicit, teams spend far less energy navigating each other and far more energy moving the work forward. Harvard’s Amy Edmondson has written extensively about this pattern: psychological safety isn’t created by being “nice,” it’s created by giving people the conditions to take smart risks without fear of stepping on unseen landmines.

They also build predictable triggers for escalation – not because they want more oversight, but because they want fewer surprises. MIT Sloan and Stanford’s research on decision friction shows that unclear escalation pathways are one of the most persistent sources of organizational drag, not a lack of talent or initiative. When escalation is principled instead of emotional, teams stop performing for proximity. They stop waiting for the most senior person in the room to weigh in. They start acting with confidence rather than caution.

This is why design matters. Not as red tape, but as alignment. Alignment is what allows teams to move with speed that doesn’t collapse into reactivity. Alignment is what replaces frantic urgency with coordinated momentum. Alignment is what turns speed from a personal instinct into an organizational capability.

When leaders invest in the architecture beneath the work – the decision rhythms, the ownership pathways, the conditions that make clarity predictable – speed stops feeling like a crisis response. It becomes something sturdier, steadier, and far more scalable: a shared capacity the entire organization can carry together.

Three Design Principles That Scale Speed Without Stress

1. Cadence Over Chaos

Fast companies don’t stay aligned because they communicate constantly; they stay aligned because they communicate predictably. A steady rhythm for decisions, priorities, and strategic resets gives teams the psychological safety to move quickly without guessing.

McKinsey’s research on operating models reinforces this relentlessly: teams with consistent cadences surface friction earlier, make cleaner decisions, and dramatically reduce the time they spend circling old conversations. A reliable rhythm creates the conditions for speed by making expectations visible and reducing the cognitive load that comes from constant recalibration.

When everything is handled “as needed,” nothing is truly intentional. A predictable cadence becomes the place where clarity lives – and where the velocity of the team stops depending on the vigilance of the leader.

2. Define “Done” Before You Start

Speed can create the illusion of progress even when the destination is unclear. People move, update, share, build – but without a shared definition of success, everyone is running in parallel rather than in alignment.

MIT Sloan identifies this as one of the most common sources of organizational drag: unclear or shifting outcomes multiply effort while diluting results. Teams don’t slow down because they’re unfocused; they slow down because they’re trying to hit a moving target.

Defining “done” isn’t bureaucracy – it’s generosity. It saves people from overworking, reworking, or waiting for confirmation. It turns motion into momentum by anchoring the work to a stable point everyone understands and can trust. Clear outcomes also reduce escalation by allowing people to decide with confidence rather than seeking reassurance.

3. Build Triggers Into the System

In healthy systems, escalation isn’t dramatic – it’s designed. Pivots aren’t emotional – they’re principled. Teams don’t wait for the founder to weigh in because they already understand the thresholds that guide the work.

Stanford’s research on decision architecture shows that explicit decision criteria, escalation triggers, and clear thresholds significantly reduce cycle time and increase decision quality. When people know what signals a pivot, when a “no” is required, or when a project needs visibility at the next level, they act with far more decisiveness.

Clear triggers also answer the quiet questions that slow teams down:
Is this mine to decide?
Has the moment arrived to elevate this?
What tells us it’s time to pivot?
Who holds the authority to say no?

When those answers are embedded into the operating system, people stop seeking reassurance and start acting with conviction. The result is speed that feels coordinated rather than chaotic – the kind of speed that scales.

When Speed Turns Into Stress, It’s Not a Motivation Problem

Every growing company eventually reaches a moment when the speed of the work begins to outpace the system built to support it. Leaders feel it first: the subtle drag in the day-to-day, the mounting rework that shouldn’t be necessary, the sense that clarity evaporates faster than they can rebuild it. Teams feel it soon after, often through mixed expectations, repeated conversations, or work that drifts just slightly off course despite everyone’s best intentions.

What’s important to name is this: the strain doesn’t emerge because people are suddenly less capable. It emerges because the system is carrying a load it was never designed to hold.

In these moments, the instinct is remarkably consistent. Leaders try harder. They respond faster. They carry more of the ambiguity themselves, believing that the surge of effort will steady the team. But the problem isn’t effort. The problem is design.

If you’re resetting expectations every week, rewriting work that should have landed cleanly, or revisiting decisions that were supposedly made, you’re not witnessing a failure of leadership – you’re witnessing the natural limits of an operating model that hasn’t kept pace with growth. These are architectural signals, not personal ones.

The good news is that architecture can be rebuilt.

When the right scaffolding is in place – clear decision pathways, stable rhythms, explicit ownership, shared definitions of success – the same team becomes lighter, clearer, and faster almost overnight. The pressure eases not because anyone worked harder, but because the structure finally supports the speed you’ve been asking the organization to maintain. What once felt like strain becomes flow again, and momentum starts to feel earned rather than forced.

Before You Sprint Again…

Sometimes the bravest move a leader can make is to pause long enough to understand why the work suddenly feels heavier. When speed begins generating more confusion than clarity, when decisions keep circling back to you despite thoughtful delegation, or when the team is endlessly busy without building real momentum, it’s easy to assume the problem is effort – that if you just push a little harder, everything will snap back into place.

But heaviness is rarely a sign of personal failure. It’s almost always a sign that the system is carrying a load it was never designed to hold.

Most leaders respond to this tension by tightening their grip – jumping in faster, reviewing more work, adding extra check-ins, or trying to personally absorb the complexity. But what’s happening underneath is far simpler: the architecture hasn’t caught up to the velocity you’ve created. You’re compensating personally for what the system can’t yet stabilize structurally.

And here’s the good news: systems can be redesigned.

When the underlying scaffolding evolves, the same team becomes lighter, clearer, and faster almost overnight. The strain lifts not because people suddenly change – but because the structure finally supports the pace you’ve been asking it to carry.
