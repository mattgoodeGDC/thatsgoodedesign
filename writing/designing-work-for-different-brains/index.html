Designing Work for Different Brains – The Neuroinclusive Workflow

Essay 7: Neurodiversity Series

Matt Goode15 January 202616 min read Get new pieces by email

# Neuroinclusion

I was sitting in a conference room watching a senior product designer – brilliant, award-winning, the kind of talent companies fight over – get performance-managed out of an organization.

Not for poor work. Her designs were exceptional.

Not for missing deadlines. She shipped early, consistently.

The issue, according to her manager, was “engagement.”

She didn’t speak up enough in meetings. She worked with headphones on most of the day. She declined optional social events. She preferred Slack over Zoom. She asked for written agendas before discussions and often sent her thoughts via email afterward instead of contributing in real time.

“She’s just not collaborative,” the manager said.

I asked to see her work.

It was some of the best design thinking I’d seen in years – thoughtful, user-centered, systematically rigorous. Her async contributions in shared docs were thorough, insightful, often the thing that unstuck the team when they’d been circling the same problem for days.

But none of that mattered.

Because the performance that counted wasn’t the work.

It was the performance of engagement in formats the organization had decided were “professional.”

Real-time presence. Vocal participation. Visible enthusiasm. The aesthetics of collaboration.

She could do exceptional work quietly – or she could perform collaboration loudly.

The system had decided only one of those was leadership material.

Here’s what actually happened next: I asked the CEO to run an experiment. Keep her performance metrics (quality, timeliness, impact) exactly the same. But stop measuring “engagement” in meetings. Instead, count her async contributions in shared docs as collaboration.

Three months later, she’d contributed to four major product decisions that wouldn’t have happened without her input. But in the old system, none of that would have counted – because it didn’t happen in real time, in front of people, with visible enthusiasm.

That’s when I asked the question that shifted the conversation:

“What if the system is the problem – not the person?”

Because here’s the thing about “reasonable accommodations”:

The phrase itself presumes the system is fine and the person needs adjustment.

But what if the system was never neutral to begin with?

What if the workflows, the meeting structures, the performance norms we’ve institutionalized as “best practices” are just cognitive preferences that got mistaken for universal standards?

We’ve mapped how organizations are built around a neurological fiction. We’ve explored how different cognitive profiles solve different problems. We’ve tuned the CREATE engine, navigated decision friction with 6C, and learned to shift leadership gears with VOICES. Now we arrive at the design question that makes all of it operational: How do we actually structure work so different brains can contribute without camouflage?

From Accommodation to Architecture

The dominant frame for neurodiversity at work is accommodation.

Someone discloses. HR approves adjustments. The person gets noise-canceling headphones, a quieter desk, permission to skip some meetings.

It’s well-intentioned.

But it’s still operating from a deficit model.

The message – implicit but unmistakable – is: The system is correct. You need help fitting into it.

And every “accommodation” reinforces that framing.

But here’s what happens when you shift from accommodation to universal design:

You stop asking, “How do we help this person succeed despite their brain?”

You start asking, “How do we design work so all brains can contribute without camouflage?”

Because universal design principles – borrowed from architecture, adapted to knowledge work – don’t create special exceptions.

They create better defaults.

Curb cuts were designed for wheelchairs. But they help parents with strollers, travelers with luggage, delivery workers with carts. They make the system work better for everyone, not just the people who needed them most.

The same logic applies to neuroinclusive workflows.

Async-first communication doesn’t just help autistic employees who need processing time. It helps parents managing school pickup, global teams across time zones, anyone who thinks more clearly in writing than in real-time speech.

Explicit decision rights don’t just reduce anxiety for neurodivergent professionals. They eliminate ambiguity for everyone – especially new hires, remote workers, and anyone navigating a complex org structure.

Outcome-focused performance doesn’t just protect ADHD employees who work in bursts. It raises the bar for everyone by making results – not optics – the measure that matters.

This isn’t about making work easier.

It’s about making work accessible to more kinds of intelligence.

And when you do that, you don’t just include more people.

You unlock capacity you didn’t know you were missing.

The Five Neuroinclusive Workflow Design Principles

Let’s make this concrete.

These aren’t accommodations. They’re architecture.

1. Asynchronous-First Communication

Why it matters:

Real-time communication favors brains that process verbally, quickly, and socially. But some of the best thinking happens slowly, in writing, alone.

For autistic and anxious processors especially, async communication isn’t a workaround – it’s how they access their full cognitive capacity. The pressure to respond in real time splits attention between what to say and how to say it without misreading social cues. Remove that pressure, and suddenly the depth is available.

But this benefits everyone.

Async communication creates a written record. It respects different time zones. It lets people contribute during their peak cognitive hours instead of during the scheduled meeting slot.

It turns “being responsive” into “producing thoughtful input” – which is what actually moves work forward.

Before/After:

Before: Daily standups require everyone to report verbally. The pattern-detector who needs time to synthesize stays silent. The fast-talker dominates. Decisions get made without the deepest thinking.

After: Standup prompts posted in Slack 24 hours ahead. People contribute live OR async within 24 hours. Both count equally. The pattern-detector contributes a written analysis that shifts the entire sprint direction – something that never would have surfaced in the 15-minute live meeting.

How to build it:

Default to written updates. Use Slack threads, shared docs, recorded video walkthroughs.

Make synchronous meetings the exception, not the rule. Reserve real-time for decisions that genuinely require live debate – not for information that could’ve been a memo.

Create norms: “We respond within 24 hours, not 24 minutes.” “Meetings have agendas sent 48 hours in advance.” “You can contribute live or async – both count.”

Framework connection:

This is Execution cylinder work. You’re redesigning how work flows – and shifting from measuring real-time performance to measuring outcomes.

2. Multiple Input Channels

Why it matters:

Different brains process differently.

Some think by talking. Some think by writing. Some think spatially and need to draw it. Some think kinetically and need to move while they process.

If your collaboration design only recognizes one mode – usually verbal, real-time discussion – you’ve just filtered out every brain that works differently.

And you don’t even know what you’re missing, because those ideas never made it into the room.

Before/After:

Before: Strategy session = whiteboard brainstorm. Verbal processors dominate. Written processors stay quiet or force themselves to “participate” in a format that suppresses their thinking. Best ideas never surface.

After: Strategy session = three pathways: (1) Live discussion for verbal processors, (2) Shared doc for written processors, (3) Visual board for spatial thinkers. Synthesis happens after all three contribute. The written processor’s risk analysis prevents a $2M mistake. The spatial thinker’s diagram becomes the new product architecture.

How to build it:

Offer pre-reads before meetings so people can process the content before the discussion.

Provide written summaries after so people who couldn’t track the conversation in real time can still integrate the outcome.

Use visual dashboards so people who think spatially can see the system at a glance.

Let people choose their mode: contribute live, contribute in writing, contribute via recorded video. All three are valid.

Framework connection:

This is Inclusive gear in action. You’re not just inviting everyone to participate – you’re designing systems where all voices can contribute in their native mode.

3. Explicit Clarity Rituals

Why it matters:

Ambiguity is cognitively expensive – especially for autistic professionals who struggle with implicit social cues and anxiety-driven brains who spiral in uncertainty.

“We’ll figure it out as we go” feels empowering to some. To others, it feels like being asked to navigate without a map.

And every moment spent reverse-engineering “who decides this?” is a moment not spent doing the work.

Before/After:

Before: “We all own this project.” Translation: no one knows who decides what. The autistic engineer spends hours trying to figure out if she’s allowed to make technical decisions. She’s paralyzed by ambiguity. The project stalls.

After: RACI for every project. Decision log after every major decision. The engineer sees “Technical architecture = Core Expertise (Engineering Lead)” and moves forward confidently. Project ships two weeks early because no one’s spending cognitive budget on social decoding.

How to build it:

Decision logs. After every major decision, document: What was decided? Who decided? Why? What’s next?

Visible priorities. Not just OKRs in a slide deck – live dashboards that show what’s in flight, what’s blocked, what’s next.

Documented “who owns what.” Use RACI (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed) for every project. Eliminate “we all own it” ambiguity.

Framework connection:

This makes decision authority visible without forcing people to ask. It eliminates the social decoding tax and lets people focus on contributing their expertise instead of navigating invisible hierarchies. This is 6C in practice – making decision modes explicit so people know where they stand.

4. Sensory Flexibility

Why it matters:

Open offices are productivity theater.

They look collaborative. But for many neurodivergent brains – especially autistic and ADHD professionals – they’re sensory assault.

Fluorescent lights. Ambient noise. Visual chaos. Unpredictable interruptions. The constant hum of other people’s conversations.

For neurotypical employees, these might be minor irritations. For neurodivergent employees, they’re constant cognitive drains. Every moment spent managing sensory input is a moment not spent thinking.

And the compounding effect over eight hours? Exhaustion that looks like disengagement but is actually just overload.

Before/After:

Before: Open office. Mandatory in-person attendance. The autistic analyst produces decent work but seems “disengaged” by 2pm. Manager assumes motivation problem.

After: Quiet zones with booking system. Remote work option 2 days/week. Headphone norms (”headphones = focus mode, not antisocial”). Same analyst now produces exceptional work consistently because she’s not burning cognitive budget on sensory management. Promotion follows.

How to build it:

Quiet zones with booking systems. Let people work in silence when they need to focus.

Movement options. Standing desks. Walking meetings. Permission to pace while on calls.

Headphone norms. Normalize “headphones on = focus mode, not antisocial behavior.”

Lighting control. Dimmable lights. Windows. Let people adjust their sensory environment.

Framework connection:

This is Culture cylinder work. Because belonging doesn’t mean “can you socialize here?” It means “can you regulate here?”

Can your nervous system settle enough to do your best work – or is the environment itself a barrier?

5. Outcome-Focused Performance

Why it matters:

Process conformity punishes cognitive difference.

If your performance reviews measure “executive presence,” “visibility,” “enthusiasm,” or “collaboration style,” you’re not measuring work quality.

You’re measuring how well someone performs neurotypicality.

And every time you penalize someone for working differently – even when their outcomes are exceptional – you’re teaching them that camouflage is more valuable than contribution.

Before/After:

Before: Performance review: “Strong technical skills, but needs to work on visibility and speaking up in meetings.” Translation: your work is excellent but you don’t perform engagement the way we recognize it. No promotion.

After: Performance review redesigned: “Impact: Led three critical system redesigns that improved reliability 40%. Quality: Zero production incidents in 18 months. Collaboration: Provides thorough async analysis that catches edge cases others miss.” Result: Promotion based on actual contribution, not performance of charisma.

How to build it:

Judge deliverables, not optics.

Does the work meet the standard? Did they move the project forward? Did they solve the problem?

If yes, stop docking them for “not looking engaged enough in the standup.”

Separate impact from style. Leadership has many faces. Execution has many modes. Stop requiring just one.

Framework connection:

This is Ethics cylinder work. You’re protecting the right to work differently – not as a favor, but as a design principle.

The Explorer vs. Exploiter Model Applied to Role Design

These five principles redesign how work flows. But there’s a deeper design question: how do we match people to roles based on their cognitive operating system? That’s where the explorer/exploiter model becomes essential.

Here’s where cognitive neuroscience meets org design.

There’s a model in evolutionary psychology – explorer vs. exploiter – that maps beautifully onto neurodivergent cognitive styles.

Some brains are wired for exploration:

Scanning horizons. Detecting patterns across domains. Navigating uncertainty. Thriving in novelty. Seeing possibilities others miss.

These are often dyslexic and ADHD brains – the ones who get bored in stable systems, who start experimenting before the data says it’s safe, who connect ideas no one else thought to put together.

Other brains are wired for exploitation:

Precision. Consistency. Deep-system optimization. Quality control that doesn’t fatigue. Turning ambiguity into clarity through rigorous process.

These are often autistic and detail-oriented brains – the ones who see structural flaws, who build systems that scale, who make sure the innovation doesn’t break when it hits production.

Both are essential.

Explorers find new territory. Exploiters make it livable.

But here’s where most organizations fail:

They force explorers to exploit – putting ADHD brains in repetitive, process-heavy roles and wondering why they disengage.

And they force exploiters to explore – putting autistic brains in ambiguous, rapidly-shifting contexts and wondering why they struggle.

The turn:

Stop forcing cognitive mismatch.

Design roles that match cognitive operating systems.

Put explorer brains in R&D, strategy, business development – domains where novelty is the resource and pattern-detection across uncertainty is the skill.

Put exploiter brains in systems architecture, quality assurance, operations – domains where precision compounds and structural integrity matters more than speed.

Then let them collaborate across their differences instead of fighting against their wiring.

Concrete example:

Product team redesign: Explorer brain (ADHD product manager) owns vision and market scanning. Exploiter brain (autistic engineer) owns architecture and quality gates. They use 6C Consultative mode: PM decides timing, but must gather engineer’s input on technical feasibility. Result: Fast innovation that doesn’t break production. Both brains working at full capacity.

Framework Integration: CREATE, VOICES, 6C

Let’s connect this back to the full system.

CREATE Execution:

This cylinder misfires when execution systems assume uniform processing.

When you default to real-time synchronous communication, you’ve just designed execution for one kind of brain.

Neuroinclusive execution redesigns the workflow itself – async-first, multiple input channels, outcome-focused performance.

VOICES Sculptor:

Workflow design is Sculptor work.

You’re not coaching individuals to adapt to the system. You’re reshaping the system so adaptation isn’t required.

This is the gear most leaders skip – because it requires admitting that “the way we’ve always done it” might not be universal best practice. It might just be familiar.

6C Choice:

Neuroinclusive workflows operate in Choice mode by default.

Set the outcome. Define the guardrails. Then let people choose their path.

Some will work 9-5. Some will work in bursts. Some will process live. Some will process async.

All deliver the same quality – just through different cognitive routes.

The Neuroinclusive Workflow Checklist

Here’s how you know if your workflows are designed for cognitive diversity – or quietly demanding cognitive conformity.

Ask yourself these 12 questions. Be honest. The goal isn’t perfection – it’s awareness of where the friction lives.

Culture & Norms:

  1. Do we require real-time presence or real-time results? → Are people judged for being online during work hours, or for delivering outcomes?

  2. Can people contribute asynchronously and still be considered “engaged”? → Or is engagement only recognized when it’s visible and vocal?

  3. Do we judge “culture fit” or “contribution fit”? → Are we screening for cognitive conformity, or for impact?

  4. Are collaboration modes flexible or mandatory? → Can people choose how they collaborate, or is there one “right” way?

Clarity & Structure:

  1. Are decision rights explicit or inferred from social cues? → Can people see who decides what, or do they have to reverse-engineer it?

  2. Do we offer processing time before requiring responses? → Or do we expect instant contribution in every meeting?

Performance & Measurement:

  1. Do we measure “face time” or “output quality”? → Are people rewarded for being visible, or for doing excellent work?

  2. Are feedback formats flexible or standardized? → Can people receive coaching in writing, or is it always delivered live in 1:1s?

  3. Do we design roles for cognitive profiles or generic “skills”? → Are we matching brains to problems, or forcing square pegs into round holes?

Autonomy & Flexibility:

  1. Can people regulate sensory input without social penalty? → Can they wear headphones, work remotely, adjust lighting – or is that seen as “not being a team player”?

  2. Do we protect focus time or reward constant availability? → Is “deep work” valued, or is responsiveness the primary performance signal?

  3. Can people work in their peak cognitive hours? → Or do we require everyone to be “on” 9-5 regardless of when their brain works best?

Three Diagnostic Questions

Before we close, three questions to surface where your workflows might be demanding cognitive conformity:

1. When someone delivers exceptional work but doesn’t “look engaged,” what do you do?

If your first instinct is to coach them on presence, visibility, or communication style – rather than celebrating the quality of their output – you’re optimizing for optics over outcomes. That’s not performance management. That’s camouflage enforcement.

2. How many of your “collaboration rituals” are actually just real-time verbal processing requirements?

Walk through your weekly meeting rhythms. How many could function asynchronously? How many genuinely require everyone in the room at the same time? If the answer is “most of them,” you’re not designing for collaboration. You’re designing for one cognitive style and calling it teamwork.

3. When was the last time you redesigned a workflow instead of coaching someone to adapt to it?

If three people struggle with the same system, that’s not three people problems. That’s one system problem. Sculptor first – redesign the workflow. Then Coach for individual growth within better-designed systems.

There’s no such thing as a neutral workflow.

Just design choices that unlock some brains and constrain others.

The question isn’t whether your systems work.

It’s who they work for.

And when you shift from accommodation to architecture – from helping individuals adapt to broken systems to redesigning systems that don’t require adaptation – you don’t just create inclusion.

You create intelligent infrastructure.

Systems that let every brain contribute without camouflage.

Workflows that measure outcomes, not optics.

Organizations where cognitive difference isn’t tolerated – it’s leveraged.

Because the teams that figure this out won’t just be more humane.

They’ll be more capable. More adaptive. More innovative. More able to solve problems that cognitively homogeneous teams keep missing.

And the organizations that learn to design work for different brains won’t just retain more talent.

They’ll unlock the cognitive capacity they’ve been systematically filtering out – and discover that neuroinclusive design wasn’t accommodation at all.

It was competitive advantage they couldn’t see because they’d mistaken their own cognitive preferences for universal truth.

Workflow Design Self-Assessment

Rate your agreement with each statement on a scale of 1-5: (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree)

1. People can contribute meaningfully to decisions and projects without attending real-time meetings – async contributions count equally.

  • 1 = Must be live / 2 = Mostly live / 3 = Some async accepted / 4 = Async valued / 5 = Async equals live

2. We explicitly document decision rights (who decides what) rather than expecting people to infer authority through social cues.

  • 1 = All inferred / 2 = Mostly inferred / 3 = Some documented / 4 = Mostly documented / 5 = Fully explicit

3. Performance reviews measure outcomes and impact rather than “presence,” “visibility,” or communication style.

  • 1 = Style-focused / 2 = Mostly style / 3 = Mixed / 4 = Mostly outcomes / 5 = Outcomes only

4. People can regulate their sensory environment (headphones, quiet spaces, lighting, remote work) without being seen as “not a team player.”

  • 1 = Not acceptable / 2 = Discouraged / 3 = Tolerated / 4 = Supported / 5 = Normalized

5. We design roles and workflows to match cognitive operating systems (explorer vs. exploiter, verbal vs. written, etc.) rather than forcing uniform processes.

  • 1 = One way only / 2 = Minimal flexibility / 3 = Some matching / 4 = Regular matching / 5 = Systematic design

Scoring Guide:

20-25 points: Your workflows likely support cognitive diversity well. Consider: Which specific design choices create this flexibility? Can you document and share these practices?

15-19 points: Some workflows are inclusive but friction points remain. Consider: Which of the five principles (async-first, multiple input channels, explicit clarity, sensory flexibility, outcome-focus) is weakest in your organization?

10-14 points: Your workflows may be demanding significant cognitive conformity. Consider: Use the 12-question checklist above – which questions revealed the biggest gaps?

5-9 points: Workflows are likely optimized for one cognitive style. Consider: Your best performers may be succeeding despite your systems, not because of them.

Next Steps Based on Your Score:

If you scored 15+:

  • Document what makes your workflows neuroinclusive

  • Identify which teams or functions still have rigid structures

  • Share your workflow design practices with other leaders

If you scored 10-14:

  • Pick one of the five principles to implement first (async-first is often highest ROI)

  • Redesign one high-frequency ritual (daily standup, weekly planning, performance reviews)

  • Measure: Does contribution quality improve when you add flexibility?

If you scored below 10:

  • Start with outcome-focused performance: Remove one “style” criterion from next review cycle

  • Add async option to one recurring meeting: “Contribute live or via doc within 24 hours – both count”

  • Create one explicit decision log: Document who decided what and why for your next major decision

Regardless of score: Read Essay 8 to learn how to build neuroinclusive principles into hiring, team composition, and leadership development – because workflow design is just one piece of building organizations that leverage cognitive diversity as competitive advantage.

Organizations that get neuroinclusive leadership right reduce attrition, catch blind spots earlier, and build systems that don’t depend on heroic leadership.
