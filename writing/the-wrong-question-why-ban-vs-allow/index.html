The Wrong Question: Why “Ban vs. Allow” Misses the Point About AI in Schools

Matt Goode20 January 202611 min read Get new pieces by email

# AI# Schools

As promised, this isn’t really a new essay, but a revisitation and revision of a post I made on LinkedIn in August. The bones of the original post are there, but with some more edits – and probably some hot takes for other educators.

====

K-12 education isn’t just standing at a crossroads – it’s standing at one while asking for directions to the wrong destination.

The prevailing debate about AI in schools – should we ban it or allow it? – misses the question that actually matters: What kind of collaborator do we want our students to become?

Not whether they’ll use AI. They will. The question is whether they’ll learn to direct it or defer to it. Whether they’ll develop the metacognitive muscles to evaluate, refine, and override machine output – or whether they’ll become passive consumers of algorithmic suggestions.

This isn’t a distant workforce concern. It’s already here. And the clock is ticking.

The Evidence Is In – And It’s Decisive

When people talk about AI in schools, the examples often sound abstract: what if students use it to cheat on essays? But in the workplace, AI is already proving itself as a full-fledged teammate. One of the clearest demonstrations came from a landmark study at Procter & Gamble, in partnership with Wharton Business School.

P&G didn’t test AI in a lab. They asked employees to do what they already do in one of the world’s most competitive industries: develop new product ideas to solve real business challenges. These weren’t hypothetical exercises – they were authentic, high-stakes problems with the same constraints and market realities that drive billion-dollar brands.

The results were striking:

  • AI matched human teams in quality. Individuals using AI scored 0.37 standard deviations higher than solo workers without AI – putting them on par with full human teams.

  • AI made work faster. Individuals with AI finished tasks about 16% faster.

  • AI unlocked breakthrough ideas. Teams using AI were three times more likely to generate solutions ranked in the top 10% for quality.

  • AI improved collaboration and morale. By helping dissolve professional silos, AI led both R&D and commercial experts to produce more balanced, cross-functional solutions. Participants reported feeling more excited, energized, and less anxious.

But here’s where we need nuance. A recent paper from MIT’s Center for Collective Intelligence found that, on average, AI-human combinations do not outperform the best human-only or AI-only systems. Before you dismiss the P&G findings, though, read the fine print: the MIT researchers found that creative tasks showed significant positive effects for human-AI collaboration, while decision-making tasks often showed negative effects.

  • Here is the P&G study.

Product ideation – exactly what P&G tested – is a creative task. The MIT research doesn’t undercut the P&G findings; it clarifies them. Human-AI collaboration wins where it matters most for 21st-century work: creation, innovation, synthesis. The iterative loop possible with generative AI makes it better suited for human collaboration than earlier AI systems designed primarily to complete specific tasks.

If AI can deliver these results in one of the world’s most competitive corporate environments, the question for education becomes urgent: why wouldn’t we equip students with the skills to use it the same way?

But this raises a deeper question – one I’ll explore tomorrow: Can AI actually be creative?

Sure, AI can analyze datasets, optimize processes, and generate summaries. But creativity? The skeptic’s objection is fair: isn’t AI just recombining patterns from its training data? Can a system without subjective experience produce genuine novelty – or only sophisticated mimicry?

Here’s my preview of where that argument lands: the Turing test is the wrong test. Turing asked whether a machine could pass as human – a test of mimicry, not creativity. The more interesting question isn’t “can AI fool us?” but “can AI surprise us in ways that matter?” And perhaps most importantly: does creativity live in either the human or the AI, or does it emerge in the iterative loop between them?

The student who uses AI to generate ten interpretations of a Shakespeare sonnet, then argues for why one captures the original intent – where does the creativity live? In the generation? The selection? The argument? The refinement?

More on that tomorrow in my next post... For now, the practical point stands: whatever we decide about machine creativity, we know that human-AI collaboration produces outcomes neither achieves alone. That’s the skill worth teaching.

The Hidden Stakes: Neuroinclusion

Here’s an angle the mainstream debate almost entirely ignores: AI may be the most powerful neuroinclusive technology education has ever seen.

Traditional classrooms are built on an industrial-age model: standardized pacing, uniform instruction, and identical expectations. For neurodiverse learners – students with ADHD, dyslexia, autism, processing differences – this model has always been a poor architectural fit. We’ve responded with accommodations: extra time, separate testing rooms, and modified assignments. But accommodations are retrofits. They’re band-aids on a system that wasn’t designed for cognitive diversity in the first place.

AI changes the architectural possibilities entirely.

AI’s strength lies in pattern recognition and personalization at scale. It can adapt content and delivery in real time based on how a student is interacting with a lesson. For neurodiverse learners who may need more repetition, multi-sensory engagement, or pacing adjustments, this adaptability is transformational.

Consider the specifics:

  • A student with ADHD may benefit from shorter, interactive modules that reward progress quickly, while a learner with dyslexia might receive visual and audio cues alongside text to reinforce comprehension.

  • AI-powered tools can break complex tasks into manageable steps for students with executive function challenges, addressing the task paralysis that derails so many capable minds.

  • Real-time dashboards can show educators which students are struggling, where they’re excelling, and how their engagement levels fluctuate – enabling intervention before frustration sets in.

Early evidence suggests that AI tools can provide real-time, personalized support while reducing educators’ administrative burdens. Adaptive learning platforms adjust complexity, pacing, and modality to meet individual needs. For students who become overwhelmed by long assignments, AI can break work into shorter sets, celebrate progress, and provide instant feedback – building confidence alongside competence.

This reframes the entire debate. Schools that ban AI aren’t just leaving students unprepared for the workforce – they’re removing scaffolding that neurodiverse learners desperately need. They’re choosing accommodation over architecture. They’re treating AI as a cheating risk when it could be the most equalizing force in education since the Americans with Disabilities Act.

The neuroinclusive argument isn’t separate from the workforce argument. It’s the same argument, sharpened. We’re not just asking: will students be ready for AI-enabled work? We’re asking: will all students have access to the tools that let them demonstrate what they actually know?

The Policy Landscape Has Shifted

When I first wrote about this topic last summer, the policy environment was fragmented and cautious. That’s changed dramatically.

As of early 2025, 33 states and their departments of education now have official guidance or policy on the use of AI in schools. In April 2025, President Trump signed an executive order to create new educational and workforce development opportunities for America’s youth centered on AI literacy. The Department of Education followed with formal guidance affirming that AI use is allowable under existing federal education programs, provided it aligns with statutory requirements.

The National Science Foundation has issued funding opportunities specifically for K-12 AI education, prioritizing three themes: teacher professional development, curricula and instructional materials, and technology tools that bring AI into classrooms. States like Colorado have published K-12 AI Skills Progression Guides aligned with computer science standards. Utah launched an AI steering committee and hosts educator summits with stipends for lesson-plan development.

Yet even as policy catches up, the vision often lags. Ed-tech leaders see state policies still focused on tool management rather than a coherent vision for the workforce of the future. As one nonprofit leader put it: “The workforce isn’t at the center of the conversation yet, because truly nobody can describe the jobs of the future. And so it’s very hard to have a policy conversation about how we rethink the way we teach career pathways.”

He’s right – and wrong. We can’t predict specific jobs. But we can identify the skills that will matter: the ability to collaborate with AI as a partner, to direct rather than defer, to evaluate output rather than accept it. Those skills are teachable now.

The Equity Question We’re Not Asking

There’s a dimension to this debate that makes me uncomfortable – and should make you uncomfortable too.

If AI-enhanced learning is only available to students in well-funded districts, we risk widening educational inequalities rather than closing them. Independent schools pioneering AI integration may inadvertently increase the gap with under-resourced public schools. Students whose families can afford AI tutoring tools at home gain advantages that compound over time.

This isn’t hypothetical. The digital divide has already shown us how technology adoption without equity planning creates new forms of stratification. AI could be different – it could be the great equalizer, providing every student access to personalized instruction previously available only through expensive private tutoring. Or it could become the newest vector for inequality.

The school where I used to work prided itself on a curriculum philosophy they called #LifeReady. But so far, it’s still struggling with whether – let alone how – to embrace AI. My bottom line: you can’t be “life ready” while leaving neurodiverse students without adaptive tools, under-resourced schools without access to AI-enhanced learning, and all students without practice collaborating with the technology that will define their working lives.

Policymakers must prioritize making AI-driven learning tools accessible to all. Otherwise, the #LifeReady promise becomes #LifeReadyForSome.

From Tool to Teammate: What This Actually Looks Like

About fifteen years ago, I presented a bold proposal: we needed a computer science curriculum for Middle School. I envisioned it as a framework for teaching problem-solving, iteration, and design thinking – all the 21st-century skills students needed.

Fast forward to just before COVID. I went to our provost with a very different recommendation: we should sunset the Middle School CS program.

Why? Two reasons. First, if the real goal was problem-solving and iteration, those skills could be embedded across the curriculum, not siloed in a single course. Second – and more importantly – I had become convinced that coding itself was no longer the existential skill everyone assumed. AI was already on the horizon.

Some colleagues thought I was reckless. But look at the job market now:

  • CS graduate unemployment has risen to 7.8% in 2024–2025, more than double the rate from a decade ago.

  • Traditional software development postings are down nearly 17% year-over-year, as companies shift toward AI-enabled platforms that require fewer coders for the same output.

  • Demand for AI/ML-related roles has surged by 39%, especially in areas like data interpretation, AI integration, and human–machine collaboration.

  • 41% of CS graduates are underemployed, working in jobs that don’t require their degree.

This doesn’t mean computer science is obsolete. But it means that coding alone is no longer the existential skill. The new existential skill is the ability to work with AI as a collaborator: to prompt it, refine its ideas, and channel its strengths into creative, high-quality outcomes.

The P&G study underscores this point. The winning teams didn’t succeed because of their coding ability. AI doesn’t care where you put the semicolon. They succeeded because of their capacity to partner with AI as a teammate.

What AI Literacy Actually Requires

If AI is going to be a teammate, students must become fluent in AI literacy – the ability to understand what it can do, where it fails, and how to use it responsibly. That includes:

Prompt Engineering – crafting precise, detailed prompts to generate high-quality results. This is a form of metacognition: thinking carefully about what you want, what you know, and how to communicate both.

Bias Recognition – spotting and mitigating algorithmic or cultural biases in outputs. Students need to understand that AI systems reflect the data they were trained on, with all its historical inequities.

Source Verification – validating facts and tracing claims back to reliable origins. AI confidently produces plausible-sounding nonsense. Students must develop the epistemic humility and research skills to check.

Creative Collaboration – blending machine-generated ideas with original human thinking to produce stronger outcomes. This is not about using AI to replace thinking; it’s about using AI to extend it.

Ethical Reasoning – wrestling with questions of academic integrity, intellectual property, and the societal implications of human–machine work.

This is not an “extra.” It’s the new baseline for academic and workplace readiness. Just as digital literacy became essential in the internet era, AI literacy must become a core competency for every graduate.

A Day in the Life: 2035

Picture this:

A student starts their morning using AI to simulate the spread of a hypothetical virus across different countries, then collaborates with classmates to propose public health interventions.

In English, they use AI to generate multiple interpretations of a Shakespeare sonnet, then debate which one best captures the original intent.

In art, they co-create a short animated film with AI-generated storyboards, then refine character designs by hand.

A neurodiverse student uses AI-powered note-taking tools that summarize lectures in real time, generate practice quizzes, and provide multi-modal review options that match their learning preferences.

Throughout the day, all students log their AI interactions, noting which outputs they trusted, which they rejected, and why. Teachers use these logs to assess critical thinking, creativity, and ethical reasoning – not just the end product.

By graduation, they aren’t just AI users – they’re AI collaborators, equipped to enter a workforce where the best teammate might not be human.

The Real Choice

The next generation won’t be divided into those who “know the content” and those who don’t. It will be divided into those who can partner effectively with AI – and those who can’t.

If we want students to thrive, we must:

  1. Reevaluate curriculum to focus on skills that AI amplifies rather than replaces.

  2. Redesign assessments to measure higher-order thinking, creativity, and ethical judgment.

  3. Provide professional development so teachers can confidently integrate AI – not as an add-on, but as a partner.

  4. Ensure equitable access so AI-enhanced learning doesn’t become another vector for inequality.

  5. Build for neuroinclusion so that AI’s adaptive capabilities serve every learner, not just the neurotypical majority.

  6. Model responsible AI use at every level of the school system.

Banning AI might feel protective in the short term. But in the long term, it risks producing graduates who are underprepared for reality – and it denies neurodiverse students tools that could finally let them show what they’re capable of.

The choice isn’t between protecting students from AI or abandoning them to it. The choice is between teaching them to direct AI or leaving them to defer to it.

One path leads to obsolescence. The other leads to opportunity.
