The Snack Request as Leadership Metaphor

Answering the question: What's your next book about?

Matt Goode27 January 20264 min read Get new pieces by email

# Off the clock

Full disclosure, I’m not trying to spam you. But after so many inquiries recently, most notably my mom (Hi! 👋)… I figured I would explain in one simple post.

===

I’m writing a book about building systems that run without you.

In fact, it is done. Now I am putting the finishing touches on edits during a snow day. My third consecutive snow day. With two kids at home.

“Dad, I’m hungry.”

I look at my laptop. I’ve written half a sentence about delegation.

“Dad, can we have hot chocolate?”

I explain, patiently, that they know where the mugs are. They know where the packets are. The kettle is not, technically, difficult.

“But you make it better.”

Reader, I made the hot chocolate.

Here’s the thing nobody tells you about leadership development: the concepts are simple. The execution is relentless context-switching.

I know – I know – that if I took fifteen minutes to teach proper hot chocolate protocol, I’d buy myself freedom for the rest of the winter. Define the win. Establish the way. List the guardrails (the kettle is hot, one packet per mug, marshmallows are not a food group).

But it’s faster to just do it myself.

That sentence is the entire reason my book exists.

“It’s faster to just do it myself” is the most expensive thought in leadership. It’s how founders end up working weekends. It’s how managers become bottlenecks. It’s how I end up making seventeen cups of hot chocolate instead of finishing a chapter about how to stop making seventeen cups of hot chocolate.

Recently, several people have been asking me what my current project, CLUTCH, is actually about. So here it is – hot chocolate aside.

The book is called CLUTCH: How Leaders Build Systems That Run Without Them.

The core thesis is simple: leadership bottlenecks are design problems, not effort problems. The most impactful leaders don’t work harder – they design better (Or shameless plug: they hire me to help). They build systems that create clarity, distribute decision-making, and develop other leaders.

The goal isn’t to become indispensable. It’s to become – unnecessary.

Why a vehicle metaphor?

Because leadership, like driving, isn’t one skill. It’s the coordination of multiple systems. You need an engine that generates power. A gearbox that matches output to terrain. A compass that keeps you oriented. A GPS that gets others to the destination. And a clutch – the thing that makes shifting smooth instead of grinding.

Most leadership books hand you one tool and call it a solution. But you don’t drive a car with just a steering wheel. You need the whole vehicle.

Here’s how the system works:

CREATE is the engine – six cylinders that power organizational capacity: Culture, Relationships, Execution, Agility, Teaching, and Ethics. When these fire together, the organization generates momentum without depending on one person’s heroics. When one misfires, the whole vehicle stutters.

VOICES is the gearbox – six leadership stances you shift between as context demands: Visionary, Operator, Independent, Coach, Encourager, Sculptor. Most leaders get stuck in one or two default gears regardless of what the moment requires. First gear is great for starting uphill. It’ll burn out your engine on the highway.

6C is the compass – a decision-rights model spanning Command, Consultative, Collaborative, Consensus, Core Expert, and Choice. It answers the questions teams are afraid to ask: Who decides? How? When does that authority shift? Clarity eliminates the bottleneck at your desk. Ambiguity creates it.

DELEGATE is the GPS – eight steps to transfer real ownership without losing connection: Define the Win, Establish the Way, List the Guardrails, Expect the Process, Give the Right Support, Agree on Checkpoints, Transfer Ownership, Encourage Growth. Most delegations fail not from a lack of talent but from a lack of architecture.

And binding it all together? Emotional intelligence is the clutch – the mechanism that enables smooth transitions between gears, between stances, between modes of decision-making. Without it, you grind gears. You stall in transitions. You burn out from constant friction.

Which is why the cover art (to be revealed later) is a manual transmission stick. Because the real skill isn’t having power or knowing where you’re going. It’s knowing how to shift – smoothly, intentionally, without grinding – so the whole system moves together.

The job of a leader isn’t to grip the wheel forever. It’s to build a vehicle others can drive, adjust, and maintain – without losing momentum when you step away.

The snow will melt. The kids will go back to school. I’ll return to writing about systems, delegation, and leadership presence.

But today reminded me why this stuff is hard. Not because the frameworks are complicated. Because the moment is always right now, and the system is always later, and “later” keeps losing to “Dad, I’m hungry.”

The real work isn’t knowing what to build.

It’s building it while the kettle’s already boiling.

Snow days and hot chocolate notwithstanding, I’m finishing final revisions and pushing to KDP by Friday. Hard copies will be available soon.

Tell me in the comments: what’s your “hot chocolate” – the thing you keep doing yourself because it’s faster, even though you know better?

(No, Mom, you don’t need to comment. You’ll get one on the house. Thanks for the hot chocolate when I was a kid… ❤️ ☕)
