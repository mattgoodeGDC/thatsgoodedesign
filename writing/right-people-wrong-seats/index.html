Right People, Wrong Seats

Matt Goode1 October 20264 min read Get new pieces by email

# My role is changing# Growing faster than it's getting clearer

Yesterday I argued that coaching for teachers, ranked eighth of thirty school fixes in a New York Times survey of experts, is the reason the top ideas work. Tutoring and the science of reading only change outcomes when someone helps teachers do them well, week after week, in real classrooms.

Whenever I make that case to a school leader, two questions come back. Who would do it? And how would we pay for it?

Here’s my answer to both. Most schools don’t need to hire their coaches. They need to find them.

Right people, wrong seats

In Good to Great, Jim Collins argued that the best organizations start with who, not what: get the right people on the bus, then get them into the right seats. Schools are usually good at the first part. Walk into almost any faculty room and the right people are already there. What schools rarely do is move them.

Every school has teachers who could do this work. Some are close to retirement. Some want to step back from a full load without stepping away. Right now they either leave or carry on quietly in one classroom.

A friend of mine stepped back from full-time teaching and spent several years as a reading specialist. Think of the expertise she carried into one small room at a time. That work mattered. But imagine her working with a small group of teachers instead. That’s a multiplier. Another friend was our building’s expert in critical friends protocols. She ran after-school sessions for colleagues on her own time. She was already doing the job. She just didn’t have the seat.

The seat

The design is simple. Keep one section, so you stay a teacher and stay credible with colleagues. Spend the rest of the week observing, mentoring and coaching.

Then pair the role with your newest teachers. Hand the sections the veteran gave up to an early-career teacher, and let the veteran coach them every week in the classes they just inherited. The new teacher learns the course from the person who knows it best. The veteran who might have retired stays in the building. Every class those two teach gets better.

What they need to learn

This is where schools usually stumble. They find a great teacher, give her a coaching title, and assume the rest takes care of itself. It doesn’t. Coaching is a different craft from teaching, and as yesterday’s research showed, untrained coaches barely move the needle.

The good news is that the gap is specific, and it can be taught. Three distinctions matter most:

  • Observing is seeing what is actually happening in the room, not what the lesson plan says should happen.

  • Mentoring is passing on what you know: here’s how I’d handle that.

  • Coaching, as my own profession defines it, is partnering with someone so they find the answer themselves.

Much of what schools call coaching is really mentoring. Both are valuable. But a teacher who only ever gets advice learns to wait for it. A teacher who gets coached learns to see her own classroom.

This is where professional development finally earns its keep. Not another workshop for the whole faculty on the new program, the kind that fades within weeks. Focused, ongoing training for a small number of the right people, so they become the follow-up for everyone else. Most will take to it quickly. People go into teaching because they love learning, and after twenty years of teaching the same course, a new craft may be exactly the challenge they’ve been missing.

The objection

Every head or school CFO will raise the same objection: we can’t afford another adult in the building who isn’t teaching. It’s a fair point. Most schools already carry more overhead than they’d like. But this isn’t a new position. It’s a teacher you already employ, in a different seat. The real cost is the sections someone else picks up, and when that someone is an early-career teacher paired with the coach who used to teach them, the cost is lower than it looks.

Now set that against a bill you’re already paying. Richard Ingersoll’s research found 44 percent of new teachers leave within five years, and in 2022-23 alone, 30 percent of teachers with two years’ experience or less left their schools. The Learning Policy Institute puts the cost of replacing a public school teacher at up to $25,000 each. In the professional and technical sectors where I do much of my work, I see $60,000 to $80,000, plus nearly two years before a new hire is fully up to speed. Schools rarely see that bill, because it never arrives as one invoice. It hides in the job posting, the interview days, the onboarding, the mentor stipend, and a rookie year spent finding their feet. Then, too often, the teacher leaves, and the school pays it all again.

Keep two early-career teachers from walking out the door, and a hybrid coach pays for her own reduced load. Keep three, and she’s saving the school money.

So the question isn’t whether you can afford a coach. You’re already paying for its absence. And the people are already in your building. The only choice is whether that money keeps leaking out through turnover, or goes into putting the right people in the right seats.

Finding those people, designing the role, and training them in the craft is work I do with independent schools. If you’re weighing it for next year, I’d be glad to talk it through.
