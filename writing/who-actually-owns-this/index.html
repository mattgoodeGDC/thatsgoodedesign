Who actually owns this?

Resolution #8: Role Ambiguity Creates Friendly Fire – Not Collaboration

Matt Goode18 December 20256 min read Get new pieces by email

# DELEGATE# 6C

“If two people own it, no one owns it.”

A few months ago, in the middle of a retreat, I asked a leadership team a deceptively simple question:

“Who owns onboarding?”

Half the room raised their hands.

I looked around. “Okay... so who’s accountable when onboarding fails?”

The hands slowly went down.

Then one person said quietly: “I guess... all of us? None of us?”

Exactly.

This is Resolution #8 in my series on building a business that holds its shape. [Previous: Stop Setting Goals – Start Building Rhythms]

That’s the thing about role ambiguity: It doesn’t look like chaos. It looks like over-helping.

What Over-Helping Actually Looks Like

Role ambiguity looks like:

Three people are reviewing the same document because no one knows whose approval actually matters.

Someone is redoing work their teammate already finished because they didn’t know it was done.

A designer starting a deck, then discovering someone else had already begun one.

A PM is scheduling a client call, only to learn another PM already has it.

Good intentions create duplication. Collaboration creates confusion. Teamwork creates friction.

Nobody is being territorial. They’re just operating in different versions of “my job.”

The Friendly Fire Problem

Last spring, I worked with a team that was intensely collaborative, smart, and genuinely kind.

They liked each other. They communicated constantly. And they were accidentally creating conflict every week.

Why?

Because their roles overlapped like a Venn diagram drawn by a toddler.

Two people were leading client communication. Three people were leading program strategy. Nobody owned data. Everybody owned the “urgent” work. And the founder owned... all of it.

Nothing was broken. Everything was blurry.

The symptoms were subtle but corrosive:

People started apologizing constantly – for stepping on toes they didn’t know existed.

Handoffs got dropped because both people thought the other person had it.

Decisions stalled because no one knew whose call it was.

The founder told me: “We aren’t fighting. We’re accidentally stepping on each other.”

Exactly.

It wasn’t interpersonal friction. It was system friction.

The Mapping Session

We spent two hours mapping who owned what.

Not job descriptions – those are wish lists.

We mapped:

  • Who decides

  • Who executes

  • Who supplies input

  • Who gets informed

  • What “done” means

  • What “good” means

  • Where handoffs occur

By the end:

One person owned client communication (with input from the other). One person owned the program strategy (with the other two as consultants). One person finally owned data. And the founder stepped out of “all of it” and into “specific things.”

Here’s what that looked like in practice:

BEFORE: “Marketing and Product own the launch together.”

AFTER: “Product owns the launch timeline and deliverables (accountable). Marketing executes the campaign (responsible). Sales is informed of launch dates. The founder approves the budget.”

One sentence became four lines. But those four lines eliminated weeks of ambiguity.

What Changed

Three weeks later, the friendly fire stopped.

Not because people liked each other more – but because they finally knew whose call it was.

The founder sent me a note:

“We had our first project where no one apologized for ‘stepping on toes.’ Because there were no toes to step on – everyone knew their lane.”

Six months later, he told me:

“The work didn’t get easier. It just finally got simpler.”

Because clarity is the opposite of chaos. And simplicity is the opposite of overwhelm.

The Most Common Sentence I Hear in Teams

During facilitation sessions, there’s one line I hear more than any other:

“I thought that was my job.”

Sometimes said with pride. Sometimes with exhaustion. Sometimes with resignation. Sometimes with fear of stepping over a line.

Here’s the truth:

Teams rarely struggle because people don’t care. They struggle because people don’t know.

They don’t know:

  • Who owns the outcome

  • Who holds the pen

  • Who is consulted

  • Who gets informed

  • What “finished” looks like

  • What “good” looks like

People will work hard in ambiguity. They just won’t work efficiently.

And they’ll work emotionally.

Where Role Ambiguity Hides

Here’s where to look for role ambiguity in your own team:

1. Anything labeled “shared responsibility” Shared = blurred = bottlenecked

2. Any handoff between two people Every pass is an opportunity for misunderstanding

3. Any project where the founder is “involved” If you’re always involved, no one knows who truly leads

4. Legacy expectations Old roles never fully retired, new roles never fully defined

If you find these patterns, you’ve found your friction points.

Clarity Is Kindness

Here’s what most founders miss:

Clarity is kindness.

When you don’t define roles clearly, you’re not empowering people – you’re burdening them with constant uncertainty about whether they’re doing the right thing.

One of the biggest cognitive stressors inside teams is uncertainty – not big uncertainty, but everyday “Is this my lane?” uncertainty.

When people know their lane, they relax. When they relax, they contribute. When they contribute, the team performs.

This is why role clarity and psychological safety are inseparable (Resolution #6).

Clarity reduces conflict before it begins. Ambiguity creates conflict no one can name.

This Is Where Decision Clarity Becomes Operational

This is where your decision clarity from Resolution #3 becomes operational:

You’ve defined who decides. Now you need to define who owns the outcome of that decision – and who supports it.

Decision frameworks tell you the process. Role clarity tells you the people.

Both are required.

The “After” Picture

Teams with clear roles experience:

  • Fewer escalations to the founder

  • Fewer misunderstandings

  • Faster decisions

  • Less defensiveness

  • Cleaner accountability

  • Stronger collaboration

  • Reduced emotional labor

  • A calmer work culture

Not because the work got easier. But because when expectations are explicit, execution becomes automatic. When they’re implicit, execution becomes emotional.

Your Identity Shift for 2026

This resolution isn’t about becoming a more “organized” leader.

It’s about becoming someone who eliminates role ambiguity – because unclear expectations create invisible conflict.

It’s the difference between walking into a project kickoff thinking “Everyone should just collaborate” versus “Who owns what by when?”

Same project. Different operating system.

That identity creates structure. Structure creates clarity. Clarity creates peace.

Because once you see yourself as someone who defines expectations explicitly, everything downstream becomes cleaner, lighter, and less emotional.

Your team stops apologizing for stepping on toes. Your meetings stop circling around who’s responsible. Your culture stops running on anxiety about boundaries.

And your organization stops treating role confusion as a personality conflict.

The Question That Changes Everything

Here’s what I want you to ask yourself:

How much of your team’s conflict is actually interpersonal – and how much is just structural ambiguity wearing a human face?

Because most “people problems” are really “clarity problems.” And clarity problems have clarity solutions.

Take 30 seconds: What’s one area where you have “shared responsibility” that’s actually creating confusion? Drop it in the comments – I read every one.

Your 2026 Resolution

Say it out loud: “I will eliminate role ambiguity – because unclear expectations create invisible conflict.”

This resolution sticks because identity drives behavior. Not goals. Not intentions. Identity.

Once you see yourself as someone who creates explicit clarity around roles, your decisions automatically align with that reality.

This isn’t about defining more work. It’s about defining the work less emotionally.

It’s about giving everyone the psychological safety of knowing:

  • Where they stand

  • What is expected

  • What success looks like

  • Where support is needed

  • Where autonomy is earned

Clarity is kindness. Clarity is alignment. Clarity is performance.
