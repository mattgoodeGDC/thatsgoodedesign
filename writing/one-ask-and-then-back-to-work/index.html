One ask, and then back to work

Matt Goode14 August 20263 min read Get new pieces by email

# Off the clock

You’ve been reading this book for two years. Out of sequence, a piece at a time.

Everything on this page has been free that whole time, and it stays free. It is the teacher in me. That’s a commitment, not a phase. I think the ideas are worth circulating more than withholding, and the essays have never been a funnel toward anything.

But that model has a cost, and it isn’t money. It’s coherence. Work delivered on a schedule arrives in an order that has nothing to do with how the ideas actually relate. CREATE arrived one Saturday. VOICES came six weeks later. The 6C Compass showed up in the middle of something about decision latency.

The consequence isn’t about memory. A delegation failure in March traces back to a decision-rights problem installed in January. You can’t see that trace from inside a single essay, because the failure lives at the joint between two ideas rather than inside either one. Ideas delivered on a schedule get filed by date. Ideas delivered in relation to each other get filed by structure, and structure is what you can retrieve when something is going wrong at eleven at night.

So, I wrote a book about all that. Here’s what’s in it, and how much you’ve already seen.

You’ve met the four frameworks here in some form. In the book, each gets a full chapter – CREATE as the engine, VOICES as the gearbox, the 6C Compass as navigation, DELEGATE as the handoff – with the case studies, the diagnostics, and the failure modes I’ve never had room for in nine hundred words. Each of those chapters includes coaching prompts, a quick-reference table, and a reading list.

What you haven’t seen is most of the rest. Chapter 3 makes the argument I’ve circled for two years without ever writing directly: why leadership development as an industry fails, and what replaces it. Chapter 6 is the clutch itself, emotional intelligence treated as a mechanism rather than a virtue, which is the chapter that makes the other three shift without grinding – and the one I found hardest to write, because it doesn’t come naturally to me. Chapter 10 is where delegation turns into development, moving someone from watching you decide to deciding without you. Chapter 11 collapses all four systems into four questions you can run inside a live meeting. Chapter 9 is a rest stop, and it’s about a mountain in New Mexico.

278 pages, twelve chapters. Indexed and cross-referenced, so a symptom in one framework traces back to its origin in another. Case studies from startups to federal agencies. It reads two ways – straight through, or by whatever’s broken this week. Paperback only, because it’s built to be marked up and handed to someone.

I don’t have a Big Five publisher’s marketing department. I have this list. So here’s the ask, in three parts, smallest to largest.

  • Join the waitlist.

    One email, September 1, the morning it’s live. That’s the whole function.

  • Buy it that week.

    Amazon’s ranking is weighted toward velocity rather than volume – the same hundred copies sold in seven days outrank a hundred sold across three months, because concentration is what the algorithm reads as signal. Rank is how strangers find a book by an author they’ve never heard of. There’s no publicist behind this one to correct the trajectory later, which makes the first week the whole thing. It’s $9.99 during that window, half what it costs from September 8.

  • Then leave an honest review.

    Honest is the operative word – a specific, considered reaction is worth ten polite ones, and early reviews are what every reader who arrives in October uses to decide. If it didn’t work for you, say that. That’s still useful to me and more useful to them.

One email on September 1. Then I go quiet about it.

Thanks! - MG
