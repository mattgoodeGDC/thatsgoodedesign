The Leadership Crisis Inside Cybersecurity – and What the C-Suite Must Do Now

Matt Goode29 November 20258 min read Get new pieces by email

# The culture is drifting# Psychological safety

Over the past week, I had several conversations with my sister and just watched as she managed her team. She manages one of the largest cybersecurity teams in her company. She was trained as a mathematician before moving into cyber – someone wired for patterns, structure, and clarity – and she brings that same analytical rigor to her leadership. She’s exceptional in every way that matters – respected by her people, trusted by executives, and responsible for work that rarely slows down long enough to take a full breath. Her days stretch across escalation calls, cross-functional negotiations, shifting priorities, and a constant awareness that even small decisions carry real-world consequences.

Yet what stood out wasn’t the threat landscape, the tooling, or the complexity of her environment. It was something quieter, more human, and far more structural than any single incident or vulnerability.

Cybersecurity has a leadership pipeline problem.

Not a talent shortage.
Not a lack of technical capability.
A leadership pipeline problem.

And as my sister described her world, the symptoms came into focus – mid-level leaders carrying workloads that would normally be distributed across entire teams. High performers are edging toward burnout, not because they lack skill, but because the systems around them haven’t evolved quickly enough to bear the weight of their responsibilities. Decision rights are buried under layers of implied “accountability.” Promotion paths that reward technical mastery but not leadership readiness. Teams are doing mission-critical work without strategic access. And people are asked to lead other human beings without ever being taught how.

This is the invisible fragility inside an industry tasked with preventing visible disasters.

As my sister talked through her day-to-day reality – sometimes with clarity, sometimes with frustration – you could hear the tension that forms when capable leaders are placed in systems that no longer match the speed or complexity of their environment. She’s in meetings all day, moving from one escalation to the next, navigating demands from engineering, compliance, product, and the executive suite – each with its own urgency, each assuming someone else has already provided the context. She’s doing exceptional work within a system that hasn’t been updated to reflect the dramatic expansion of the role.

And the deeper truth becomes unavoidable: this isn’t a cybersecurity issue. It’s an executive design issue.

The structure is outdated.
The incentives are misaligned.
The expectations exceed the resources.
And the system is producing outcomes it was never built to sustain.

So what can COOs, CIOs, and CEOs actually do?

A great deal – if they’re willing to redesign the environment their leaders are asked to operate within. Not after the next re-org. Not when things “calm down.” Now.

Below is what they must address.

1. Stop Forcing Technical Excellence and People Leadership Into the Same Pipeline

As my sister described how her own managers grow inside the organization, a familiar pattern emerged: the best individual contributors are often pushed – or quietly funneled – into management. It sounds efficient on paper. It’s rarely efficient in practice.

The abilities that make someone exceptional in threat modeling, architecture, or incident response are not the abilities required to lead people. Technical mastery draws on precision, depth, and independent focus. People leadership requires influence, conflict navigation, prioritization, emotional steadiness, and the ability to shape culture and communication under stress.

These are different muscles. And more importantly, distinct identities.

Yet many organizations still treat them as though they belong to a single ladder. That’s how accidental managers are made – brilliant technical minds who never wanted to lead humans but accepted the role because the organization offered only one way to advance.

My sister sees this play out constantly. Some of her strongest architects quietly admit they never wanted direct reports. Others love mentoring people but have no desire to deepen their technical specialization. The system doesn’t distinguish these aspirations, so leaders inherit responsibilities that clash with who they are.

The solution is structural: two equal-status career paths.

Technical Mastery.
People Leadership.

Both valued. Both compensated. Both visible.

When companies build dual ladders, something powerful happens. Attrition falls because people can finally grow in ways aligned with their strengths. Technical experts stay in roles where they can make their greatest impact, rather than being diverted into work that drains them. And leadership roles become intentional rather than accidental.

The teams my sister leads offer clear proof. The groups that consistently thrive are the ones led by people who chose leadership because they genuinely want to develop and steward others – not because they had no other way to move forward.

Dual pathways aren’t perks. They’re the scaffolding that stabilizes the entire organization.

2. Close the Accountability–Authority Gap

Another theme my sister brought up – almost in passing, but with unmistakable weight – is how often her managers are held responsible for outcomes they cannot fully influence.

They’re accountable for policies written elsewhere.
They’re responsible for the tools selected, even though they weren’t consulted.
They inherit risks introduced by systems they don’t own.
They respond to incidents triggered by teams far outside their span of control.

This mismatch creates a kind of quiet instability – leaders who care deeply but feel perpetually exposed, never sure whether they’re succeeding or simply absorbing the consequences of decisions made above or around them.

The organizational impact is predictable. Decision-making slows because the ground beneath people feels uncertain. Risk aversion grows, and cross-functional collaboration frays. Turnover increases among the very leaders whose continuity matters most.

My sister’s stories make this clear. She describes managers who must defend timelines they didn’t commit to, enforce standards they weren’t consulted on, and clean up downstream consequences of upstream decisions. Not because anyone intends harm – but because the system hasn’t recalibrated.

Executives can fix this by realigning authority to match responsibility:

  • Clarify who truly owns what.

  • Give leaders actual influence over the systems they’re accountable for.

  • Eliminate “shadow accountability” – where someone is responsible in practice but not in structure.

A RACI chart won’t fix culture on its own, but it stops the erosion of trust that occurs when people absorb consequences without control. When responsibility and authority are back in alignment, leaders regain stability, clarity, and the ability to lead with steadiness rather than defensiveness.

3. Simplify the System: Tooling, Workload, and Expectations

My sister also talked about the sheer fragmentation surrounding cybersecurity work. The problem isn’t that teams lack capacity – it’s that the environment around them makes sustained focus almost impossible.

Tool sprawl.
Audit cycles are stacked so tightly that teams can’t incorporate what they’ve learned.
Shifting priorities without aligned resourcing.
Context switching that pulverizes attention.
Incident cycles that arrive in waves without time for a true reset.

None of this reflects the team’s capability. It’s a reflection of design.

Her days often include jumping between five different platforms before lunch, juggling alerts that range from trivial to urgent, and trying to safeguard deep work time that evaporates under relentless context switching. The fragmentation itself becomes the enemy.

Organizations must stop designing for peak load and start designing for sustainable load. That means:

  • consolidating tools

  • aligning workflows

  • tightening alerts to reduce noise

  • removing the cognitive tax of constant minor issues

  • creating staffing and time buffers for surge periods

When the environment is coherent, teams move from survival to strategy. Burnout recedes not because people toughen up, but because the architecture finally supports the work.

Burnout isn’t a personal failure. It’s an architectural failure. And architecture is the responsibility of the executive suite.

4. Redefine What Success Actually Means for Cyber Leaders

One of the most poignant themes from my conversation with my sister is this: cyber leaders don’t burn out because the work is too difficult. They burn out because the work is invisible.

Success is defined by the absence of disaster.
If nothing happens, people assume everything is fine.
If something goes wrong, people assume cybersecurity failed.

This logic rewards firefighting and ignores prevention – the work that actually protects organizations long before the crisis.

My sister talked about the pride her team feels when they quietly eliminate a category of risk or refactor an architecture to remove an entire class of vulnerabilities. But those wins rarely become visible outside the team. Prevention disappears into silence.

Executives can change this by measuring what truly reflects value:

  • time to patch

  • architecture hardening

  • measurable risk reduction

  • cross-functional alignment

  • leadership’s impact on team stability

These aren’t soft metrics. They are leading indicators of resilience. And when executives elevate them, something essential happens: the work becomes visible. People feel seen. The meaning returns.

Shift what you measure, and you shift what people feel. Shift what people feel, and the organization’s capacity changes.

5. Where I Come In

Everything my sister described is the same terrain I encounter inside organizations across industries: the unseen structures that quietly shape performance, the leadership systems that determine whether people thrive or slowly erode, and the design choices that either stabilize an organization or quietly drain it.

Cybersecurity will never stand still. Threats will evolve. Regulations will shift. Attack surfaces will grow.

But the human system surrounding cybersecurity doesn’t have to remain chaotic or brittle. It can be redesigned – intentionally, coherently, sustainably.

And when it is, the entire organization steadies.
The pressure redistributes.
The talent stays.
The work becomes strategic instead of frantic.

This is where the executive calculus becomes impossible to ignore: rebuilding leadership capacity internally isn’t just healthier – it’s fiscally responsible.

The research is unequivocal:

  • Externally hired leaders fail at nearly twice the rate of internally developed leaders (Harvard Business Review; Corporate Leadership Council).

  • External leaders require 18–24 months to reach the contextual fluency that internal successors already possess.

  • The actual cost of an external leadership hire is 1.5–3x salary once you factor in search fees, onboarding drag, cultural mismatch, and the productivity dip that accompanies every transition.

  • By contrast, companies that promote from within see 20–30% higher retention, faster ramp times, and significantly stronger long-term performance.

Put simply:
It is always more expensive to hire a leader than to build one. And far more dangerous to assume a stressed leadership pipeline will self-correct through recruitment.

Leaders like my sister don’t burn out because the work is too hard – they burn out because the structure around the work is too thin. Strengthen that structure, and you don’t just retain leaders; you unlock their full value.

That is the opportunity in front of the executive suite: Build the system around the people who protect your organization – not the other way around. When you do, performance rises, turnover falls, resilience compounds – and leaders like my sister don’t just carry the system.

They shape it.
