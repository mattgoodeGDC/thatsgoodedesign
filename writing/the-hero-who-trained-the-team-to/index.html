The Hero Who Trained the Team to Wait

Matt Goode22 June 20263 min read Get new pieces by email

# Everything runs through me# DELEGATE

The alarm goes off – a key deal about to collapse, a launch sliding, a customer threatening to walk – and you handle it. You step in, make the calls, pull the late night, and by morning, the crisis is gone. The team exhales. You’re the hero, and in that moment, it feels exactly like leadership.

And it is, the first time. Maybe the third. The problem is what it quietly teaches everyone watching.

The rescue that trains the waiting

Here’s what your team learns, without anyone deciding to teach it: when something gets hard, the move is to wait for you. Why wrestle a problem to the ground when the founder will swoop in and solve it cleaner and faster? So they bring you the problem instead of a solution. They escalate sooner. They stop reaching for the hard call because the hard call was always going to land on your desk anyway. Every time you swoop in to save the day, you train your team to wait for the next rescue.

Time-teller or clock-builder

Jim Collins and Jerry Porras spent six years at Stanford studying companies that lasted, and drew a line I’ve never been able to unsee. On one side is the time-teller: the brilliant individual who always has the answer, the person you ask because they always know. On the other is the clock-builder: the leader who builds something that can tell time without them – a system, a team, a set of habits that produces good answers whether or not the founder is in the room.

The hero is a time-teller. And a time-teller, however brilliant, is a ceiling. The organization can only ever be as good as the one person every answer routes through – and only ever as available as that person’s calendar.

Caption: Time-teller vs clock-builder – the hero has every answer; the clock-builder builds answers that outlast them.

Why can’t the hero scale?

The cost is quiet and compounding. Initiative fades. Your best people – the ones with the most options – get tired of waiting for the wheel and leave for somewhere they’ll get to drive. And the day you try to step back, into a Chair seat or a new venture or a real vacation, the thing you built sags, because it was never built to stand without you holding it up. I’ve watched a first CEO transition fail for exactly this reason: the founder had been the answer for so long that no one beneath him had ever been allowed to become one.

None of this means the rescue is always wrong. Some fires you do have to fight yourself. The shift is to notice the reflex and ask, before you swoop in: Is this a fire only I can fight, or a chance for someone else to learn to fight it? The clock-builder spends the same energy the hero does – just upstream, on the people and the systems – so that the next time the alarm sounds, it isn’t only your name being called.

Worth sitting with this week:

  • Where did you save the day this week – and who might have grown if you’d let them handle it instead?

  • What does your team bring you that they could decide themselves, if they trusted you’d back them?

  • If you stepped back for a season, what would sag – and what does that tell you about what you’ve built?
