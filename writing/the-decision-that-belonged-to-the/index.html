The Decision That Belonged to the Expert

Matt Goode3 July 20263 min read Get new pieces by email

# 6C

I picked this one thinking I had a case study in CLUTCH most people wouldn't know. Then I learned there's a film coming about the D-Day weatherman. Less dramatic than Saving Private Ryan, but make no mistake – far more Allied troops would have died if Eisenhower hadn't listened to his Core Expert. Kudos to the studio for finding the rare war story where the hero is quiet, technical, and right.

June 1944. The largest invasion in history is staged and waiting – a hundred and fifty thousand troops, thousands of ships, a weather window measured in hours. And the most consequential judgment in the entire operation, whether the skies would hold, rests with one man in the room. He was not the Supreme Commander. He was a Scottish meteorologist named James Stagg.

The most important call on D-Day

Eisenhower carried the authority and, ultimately, the accountability. But he could not read a North Atlantic weather system, and he was wise enough to know it. When Stagg identified a narrow break in a vicious storm – a thin window opening on June 6 – Eisenhower’s job was not to second-guess the science. It was to decide what to do with the expert’s read. He took it. The invasion went. Stagg was right.

The lesson isn’t simply that Eisenhower deferred. It’s that he knew exactly which part of the decision was his and which part wasn’t. The forecast belonged to the meteorologist. The go/no-go belonged to the commander. Confusing the two – overruling the expert on his own turf, or dodging the command call because it was heavy – would have been the failure.

Core Expert mode

This is Core Expert, one of the six modes on the 6C Compass: the call goes to the person closest to the problem, because proximity beats rank when the question is technical. The leader’s job in this mode isn’t to make the decision. It’s to name that someone else owns it, give them the authority to own it, and back the call they make.

It sounds obvious. It’s the mode leaders skip most.

Caption: Decide by rank vs decide by proximity – on D-Day, the call belonged to the meteorologist.

Why leaders hold calls that aren’t theirs

Because seniority feels like it should come with answers. You got the corner office by being good at decisions, so handing one off can feel like dodging the job. But rank is not expertise, and the higher you rise, the wider the gap between the two. The person three levels down often knows the actual terrain – the customer, the codebase, the classroom – far better than you do, and your “input” on their turf is frequently just confident noise that overrides someone who’s right.

The cost of holding those calls is double. You make worse decisions, because you’re deciding above your knowledge. And you tell your expert, in the clearest possible language, that their expertise stops at your door – which is exactly how you lose the people who actually know things. The leader who can say “this one’s yours, you know it better than I do” doesn’t look weaker. They look like Eisenhower.

Worth sitting with this week:

  • Which decision on your plate actually belongs to someone who knows the terrain better than you?

  • Where has your “input” on an expert’s turf really just been an override?

  • Who on your team would step up if you named, out loud, that the call was theirs?

Core Expert is one of six modes on the 6C Compass. Knowing which call is yours and which belongs to the person closest to the problem – and naming it out loud – is the whole skill. The full Compass lives in CLUTCH.
