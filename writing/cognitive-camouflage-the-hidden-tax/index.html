Cognitive Camouflage: The Hidden Tax on Your Best Performers

Essay 3: Neurodiversity Series

Matt Goode9 January 202615 min read Get new pieces by email

# Neuroinclusion

I was three months into coaching a VP of Engineering when she finally told me the truth.

We’d been working on leadership presence, stakeholder management, all the surface-level stuff her CEO flagged in her last review. She was technically brilliant – everyone agreed on that. But she “wasn’t showing up like a leader.” She “needed to be more visible.” She “could work on executive presence.”

In our fourth session, I asked a different question.

“What does a typical day cost you – energetically?”

Long pause.

Then she said something I’ll never forget:

“By 2pm, I’m done. Not because the work is hard. Because pretending isn’t.”

She wasn’t talking about imposter syndrome. She was talking about something far more expensive.

She was talking about the cognitive budget she spent every single day just to appear neurotypical.

Making eye contact in meetings even though it fractured her ability to listen. Smiling and nodding during small talk she found meaningless. Suppressing the hand movements that helped her think. Forcing herself to speak up in real-time even though her best ideas came hours later, alone, in writing. Performing enthusiasm in the exact register her manager expected, even when the thing being celebrated felt obvious or irrelevant.

“I’m good at my job,” she said quietly. “But I’m exhausted by the performance around my job.”

Before you read further, look at your attrition data differently. Pull the names of people who left in the last two years. Now ask: How many were described as “brilliant but struggled with executive presence”? How many were “great thinkers but didn’t play the political game well”? How many got positive performance reviews right before they quit? Those phrases? They’re masking fatigue disguised as performance problems.

I recognized what she was describing immediately – because I’d been watching my daughter do the same thing for years.

My daughter is autistic. Every day after school, she comes home and collapses. Not because the academics are hard – she’s brilliant. But because she’s spent seven hours monitoring her facial expressions, suppressing her stims, translating her thoughts into the communication style her teachers expect, and performing a version of herself that doesn’t trigger confusion or concern in the adults around her.

She calls it “keeping the mask on.” And by the time she gets home, she’s spent.

The psychologist who evaluated her explained that masking – the cognitive labor of appearing neurotypical when your brain processes the world fundamentally differently – isn’t just exhausting. It’s expensive. It taxes executive function, drains working memory, and leaves less capacity for everything else: learning, connecting, creating, thinking.

Sitting across from this VP, I realized: her performance review wasn’t measuring her leadership capacity.

It was measuring how well she could camouflage.

The Tax You’re Not Tracking

Here’s what most leaders don’t understand about neurodivergent employees:

Your highest performers aren’t just doing their jobs.

They’re doing their jobs while spending 30-40% of their cognitive capacity pretending to process information the way you do.

That’s not a metaphor. That’s math.

Here’s the business case you’re missing: If a neurodivergent professional spends 30% of their cognitive load on masking, and they’re working 50 hours a week, you’re paying for 50 hours but getting 35 hours of contribution. Multiply that by salary ($120K = $36K in lost capacity annually), by tenure, by the number of neurodivergent people in your organization. Now calculate the cost of replacing them when they burn out at 18 months. The “accommodation” wasn’t the expense – the masking tax was.

Every meeting where they have to track social cues instead of content.

Every performance review where “presence” matters more than outcomes.

Every open office where they’re managing sensory overload while trying to solve complex problems.

Every “quick sync” that derails three hours of deep work because your culture valorizes responsiveness over thought.

This isn’t discomfort.

It’s expensive.

And you’re paying for full cognitive capacity while getting a fraction – because the rest is being spent on camouflage.

Researchers call it “masking” – the sustained effort required to hide neurodivergent traits and perform neurotypical behavior. And the data is unambiguous: masking doesn’t just create fatigue. It creates a predictable burnout curve that shows up in every metric you claim to care about.

Decreased creativity. Slower problem-solving. Higher error rates. Increased sick days. And eventually – usually right around the 18-month mark – attrition.

Not because they couldn’t do the work.

Because the camouflage requirement became unsustainable.

What Camouflage Actually Looks Like

Let’s be specific about what we’re asking people to hide.

Appearing “engaged” in meetings designed for neurotypical communication.

For many autistic professionals, eye contact isn’t just uncomfortable – it’s cognitively expensive. It splits attention between tracking facial expressions and processing content. So they develop workarounds: looking at foreheads, nodding at precise intervals, mirroring body language. All of it burns executive function that could be solving the actual problem being discussed.

Hiding stims and self-regulation behaviors.

The hand movements, the fidgeting, the rocking, the need to pace while thinking – these aren’t distractions. They’re how certain brains regulate attention and process complexity. But because they don’t look “professional,” people learn to suppress them. And every minute spent suppressing a stim is a minute not spent thinking clearly.

Translating thoughts into expected formats.

Some brains think in systems, in spatial models, in non-linear webs of association. But if your culture demands linear, verbally-articulated, real-time responses, those brains have to do translation work before they can contribute. It’s like being forced to solve a calculus problem but only being allowed to show your work in prose. Possible? Sure. Efficient? Not even close.

Performing enthusiasm in ways that feel unnatural.

Not everyone signals excitement the same way. For some, enthusiasm is visible – vocal inflection, visible energy, spontaneous celebration. For others, it’s quiet – deep focus, meticulous follow-through, sustained intellectual engagement. But if your culture only reads the former as “engaged,” the latter learns to perform it. And performance is exhausting.

Real-time processing when you need reflection time.

There are brains that think best in motion – rapid-fire, associative, iterative. And there are brains that think best in stillness – deep, integrative, pattern-recognizing. If your meeting culture valorizes the first and pathologizes the second, you’re not measuring intelligence. You’re measuring processing speed. And you’re losing the insights that only emerge when given space to form.

None of this is optional for the person doing it.

It’s the price of entry.

And the cost accumulates – not just daily, but hourly – until the system breaks.

The Camouflage-Forcing Systems You’ve Normalized

Here’s the part that makes this so insidious:

The systems creating the camouflage requirement don’t feel like exclusion.

They feel like “professional standards.”

They feel like “best practices.”

They feel like “how work gets done here.”

But let’s name them for what they are – because until you see them as design choices rather than neutral defaults, you can’t redesign them.

Stand-up meetings that punish processing time.

The daily standup. The weekly all-hands. The “quick sync” that’s never actually quick. These rituals are built for brains that process verbally, in real-time, in front of others. But for anyone who needs time to integrate before articulating, or who thinks more clearly in writing, or who finds social performance cognitively expensive – these meetings aren’t “collaborative.” They’re camouflage requirements. You’re not getting their best thinking. You’re getting their best performance of thinking.

Open offices that assault sensory systems.

The fluorescent lights. The ambient noise. The lack of visual boundaries. The unpredictable interruptions. For neurotypical employees, these might be minor annoyances. For neurodivergent employees – especially autistic and ADHD professionals – they’re constant cognitive drains. Every moment spent managing sensory input is a moment not spent solving problems. And the compounding effect over eight hours? Exhaustion that looks like disengagement but is actually just overload.

“Culture fit” that means “communicates like the founder.”

This one shows up in hiring, in promotions, in performance reviews. “Not sure they’re a culture fit.” Translation: they don’t communicate the same way we do. They don’t socialize the same way. They don’t perform enthusiasm in the register we recognize. But culture fit isn’t neutral. It’s neurotype fit. And every time you screen for it, you’re filtering out cognitive difference before it ever gets a chance to contribute.

Real-time brainstorms that favor fast talkers over deep thinkers.

The whiteboard session. The “let’s riff on this” meeting. The “all ideas welcome” environment that actually means “all ideas that can be articulated in the next 90 seconds while making eye contact and not appearing too attached to them.” These formats don’t surface the best ideas. They surface the ideas that require the least translation time. And the pattern-recognizing, systems-thinking, deeply-integrative ideas? They don’t make it into the room. Not because they don’t exist – but because the format filtered them out.

Performance reviews that measure “presence” over outcomes.

“Could be more visible in meetings.” “Needs to work on executive presence.” “Should take more opportunities to build relationships across the org.”

None of these are about work quality. They’re about performance of engagement. And for neurodivergent employees, that’s the signal – the moment they realize that no matter how well they execute, they’ll always be penalized for not camouflaging effectively enough.

Every single one of these systems is a choice.

And every single one can be redesigned.

When Culture Under Pressure Reveals Who’s Safe

Let’s map this to CREATE – because this isn’t just an HR issue. It’s a systems design issue.

When I work with leaders on the masking tax, I trace it back through the CREATE framework. Culture enforced the neurotypical norm. Relationships stayed ambiguous because explicit communication felt “too rigid.” Execution didn’t flex for different cognitive styles. This isn’t six separate problems – it’s one systems failure expressing itself in six places.

The Culture cylinder is where this shows up most clearly – and most invisibly.

In my book Clutch, I use the NASA Challenger disaster as a case study. Engineers knew the O-rings would fail in cold weather. They said so. Explicitly. Repeatedly. But the organizational culture under pressure prioritized schedule over safety, perception over truth. And the people with the critical information learned – through a thousand small signals – that their input wasn’t welcome.

For neurodivergent employees, the dynamic is structural, not situational.

They don’t need a crisis to learn that their way of processing isn’t valued. They learn it in every meeting where “speaking up” is the only recognized form of contribution. In every performance review that penalizes depth for not looking like speed. In every “culture fit” assessment that treats neurotypical communication as the standard and everything else as deviation.

Culture under pressure reveals who feels safe telling the truth.

And for neurodivergent professionals, the pressure isn’t occasional.

It’s constant.

Because the camouflage requirement is the pressure.

This is why the Relationships cylinder matters so much in neuroinclusive design. Role clarity doesn’t just reduce ambiguity – it protects neurodivergent employees from having to navigate unwritten social rules.

When “who owns what” is explicit, autistic professionals especially can focus on the work instead of decoding the invisible protocols around how to ask for help, when to speak up, how much small talk is required before getting to the point.

Ambiguity isn’t just uncomfortable for some brains. It’s cognitively expensive. And every moment spent trying to reverse-engineer social expectations is a moment not spent doing the thing you hired them to do.

But here’s where most leaders stall out:

They Vision. They Operate. They move fast, they execute hard, they drive outcomes.

But they never shift into VOICES Sculptor gear – the mode where you actually redesign the systems creating the camouflage requirement.

Because that would require slowing down long enough to ask:

“What if the way we’ve always done things isn’t neutral – it’s just familiar?”

The Camouflage Audit: Five Rituals That Force Masking

Let’s make this actionable.

Here are five workplace rituals that – when examined closely – reveal themselves as camouflage requirements. And for each, a redesign option that doesn’t lower standards but expands capacity.

1. All-hands meetings with “popcorn sharing”

What it forces: Real-time verbal processing. Social performance. Spontaneous articulation with no prep time.

What it costs: Anyone who thinks before they speak, processes in writing, or finds public performance cognitively expensive either stays silent (and gets dinged for “not engaging”) or performs (and burns cognitive budget they needed for actual work).

Redesign option: Rotate between live discussion and async input. Post discussion prompts 24 hours in advance. Create a shared doc where people can contribute written responses. In the live meeting, synthesize themes and go deeper on a few – but stop treating “talked in the meeting” as the only valid form of participation.

2. Open-plan offices with “collaboration zones”

What it forces: Constant sensory management. Visual and auditory vigilance. The performance of “being collaborative” even when you need to focus.

What it costs: Sensory overload compounds hourly. For ADHD brains, every interruption resets focus. For autistic brains, unpredictable stimuli create constant low-grade stress. Productivity craters. Error rates climb. By midday, people are managing environment, not solving problems.

Redesign option: Create quiet zones with booking systems. Normalize headphones as “focus mode,” not antisocial behavior. Let people work from home on deep-work days without it counting against them in “collaboration” assessments. Stop treating physical presence as a proxy for engagement.

3. Mandatory video-on Zoom culture

What it forces: Performance of presence. Eye contact simulation (which for many means staring at a camera instead of actually listening). Managing how you look while trying to think.

What it costs: Split attention. Some people process better when they can look away, move, doodle, pace. Forcing video-on turns a cognitive tool (audio-only focus) into a performance requirement.

Redesign option: Default to video-optional. If someone’s contribution quality doesn’t depend on seeing their face, stop requiring it. Save video-on for moments where visual cues actually matter – client pitches, sensitive conversations – and make it the exception, not the default.

4. “Bring your whole self to work” messaging that actually means “perform extroversion”

What it forces: A specific style of authenticity – the kind that’s visible, verbal, emotionally expressive, and socially fluent.

What it costs: People whose “whole self” is quiet, task-focused, and energized by depth rather than breadth learn quickly that the invitation isn’t actually for them. It’s for the version of themselves that looks like the people who already fit.

Redesign option: Reframe authenticity as “do your best work in the way that works for you” – not “perform a specific kind of personality.” Measure outcomes, not performance of engagement. Let people bring their whole capacity, however it shows up.

5. Annual reviews that assess “executive presence” or “cultural ambassador” qualities

What it forces: Conformity to a communication style that’s been institutionalized as “leadership.”

What it costs: Brilliant strategists, systems thinkers, and execution engines get passed over for promotion – not because they can’t lead, but because they don’t look like leaders when “looking like a leader” means neurotypical communication norms.

Redesign option: Separate impact from style. Assess: Are they moving the work forward? Do people trust them? Are they solving problems others can’t? If yes, stop penalizing them for not performing charisma. Leadership has many faces. Stop requiring just one.

Three Diagnostic Questions

Before we close, three questions to surface where your systems might be forcing camouflage without realizing it:

1. Where does “professionalism” actually mean “neurotypical communication style”?

Look at your performance review language. Your hiring rubrics. Your promotion criteria. Every time you see “presence,” “polish,” “gravitas,” “cultural fit” – ask: are we measuring impact, or are we measuring how well someone mirrors the communication style we’re used to?

2. How many of your collaboration rituals actually require real-time, synchronous, verbal contribution to be effective?

Most meetings don’t. But we default to them because they feel collaborative. Meanwhile, the best ideas are getting filtered out before they’re ever voiced – not because people don’t have them, but because the format doesn’t allow time to form them.

3. When was the last time someone burned out after getting a good performance review?

If the answer is “recently,” you probably have a camouflage problem. They were meeting your standards – but the cost of meeting those standards became unsustainable. And you didn’t see it because you were measuring output, not energy.

There’s no such thing as a neutral workplace norm.

Just design choices that include some brains and exclude others.

The question isn’t whether neurodivergent employees can perform in your systems.

It’s whether your systems are designed to extract performance – or unlock capacity.

Because the cost of camouflage isn’t just human.

It’s economic.

You’re paying for talent you’re not fully accessing.

You’re burning out people you can’t afford to lose.

And you’re missing insights you’ll never even know existed – because the people who saw them learned long ago that their way of seeing doesn’t count.

Masking Tax Self-Assessment

Rate your agreement with each statement on a scale of 1-5: (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree)

1. Our workplace norms and expectations are explicitly documented rather than absorbed through social osmosis.

  • 1 = Not documented / 2 = Minimally documented / 3 = Partially documented / 4 = Well documented / 5 = Fully explicit

2. People on my team can succeed without masking neurodivergent traits (stimming, needing processing time, preferring written communication, managing sensory needs).

  • 1 = Cannot succeed / 2 = Very difficult / 3 = Possible but costly / 4 = Generally supported / 5 = Fully supported

3. Our performance reviews measure outcomes and impact rather than “presence,” “visibility,” or communication style.

  • 1 = Style-focused / 2 = Mostly style / 3 = Mixed / 4 = Mostly outcomes / 5 = Outcomes-focused

4. I can identify specific systems in our organization that require neurodivergent employees to spend cognitive energy on camouflage rather than contribution.

  • 1 = Cannot identify / 2 = Vague sense / 3 = Some clarity / 4 = Clear identification / 5 = Systematically mapped

5. When high performers leave after positive reviews, we investigate whether masking fatigue contributed to their departure.

  • 1 = Never / 2 = Rarely / 3 = Sometimes / 4 = Usually / 5 = Always

Scoring Guide:

20-25 points: Your systems likely minimize masking requirements. Consider: Can you document what makes your environment less camouflage-intensive? What practices could other leaders learn from?

15-19 points: You’re reducing some camouflage requirements but likely have blind spots. Consider: Which of the five rituals in the Camouflage Audit apply to your organization? What would it take to redesign one?

10-14 points: Your systems may be forcing significant masking without your awareness. Consider: Look at your last three exits of high performers. Were any described as “brilliant but...” or received coaching on “presence” before leaving?

5-9 points: Masking requirements are likely creating hidden costs throughout your organization. Consider: Calculate the cognitive load tax: if 30% of capacity is spent on camouflage, and average salary is $100K, you’re paying $30K per person annually for performance theater instead of contribution.

Next Steps Based on Your Score:

If you scored 15+:

  • Audit one system for camouflage requirements using the five rituals framework

  • Document what your team does well (explicit norms, flexible formats, outcome-focused assessment)

  • Share practices with other leaders – this is rare and valuable

If you scored 10-14:

  • Pull performance review language from the last year: count how many times you see “presence,” “visibility,” “polish,” “gravitas”

  • For each instance, ask: “Was this about impact or about communication style?”

  • Redesign one high-frequency ritual (daily standup, brainstorm format, review process)

If you scored below 10:

  • Review your attrition data: Who left in the last 18 months who was described as “brilliant but didn’t fit” or “struggled with executive presence”?

  • Calculate the replacement cost of one departure. That’s your baseline for what masking requirements are costing you.

  • Choose ONE camouflage-forcing system to redesign (start with meetings – highest frequency, clearest intervention)

Regardless of score: Read Essay 4 to see how the CREATE framework – the system you’re already using – can be tuned for cognitive diversity. Because you don’t need a new framework. You need to see how your existing one works differently for different brains.

The business case is clear.

Organizations that get neuroinclusive leadership right reduce attrition, catch blind spots earlier, and build systems that don’t depend on heroic leadership.
