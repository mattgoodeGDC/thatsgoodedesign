Your Decision Chart Answers the Wrong Question

Matt Goode17 June 20266 min read Get new pieces by email

# 6C

You have been in this meeting.

EVERYONE has been in this meeting. Yet they still exist. Why?

The decision was assigned. Someone owned it on paper. The roles were clear enough that nobody could claim confusion. And still, the room filled with people who had no real reason to be there, the conversation circled for an hour, and everyone walked out without a call being made.

The chart was not missing. The chart was fine.

What was missing was the mode.

Three researchers just published the definitive autopsy on why this happens. Lindy Greer and Maxim Sytch of Michigan’s Ross School and Jennifer Jordan of IMD examined decision-rights tools across more than a hundred companies and found they fail not because they are flawed, but because people misunderstand them, ignore them, or let them drift from how the team behaves. The line that traveled furthest in the coverage compares a decision chart to the position plan for a kids’ soccer game – a tidy diagram nobody understands or remembers.

Two pieces from the HBR that were the inspiration for this piece.

  1. What Companies Get Wrong About Decision Rights

  2. Why Decision-Making Frameworks Fail

I read that and stopped. Because underneath it sits a quiet surrender: the system is a nice idea on paper, and paper loses. I do not accept that. A well-built system does the opposite of lose. It makes decisions faster, it makes them clearer, and it makes them more trusted. The failure in that meeting is not evidence that systems do not work. It is evidence that this one was half-built.

A chart answers who. It never answers how, and how is the part that decides whether twelve people sit in the room or four, whether you are gathering input or building agreement, whether the meeting ends in a decision or a rematch. RACI, the most common of these tools, sorts people into responsible, accountable, consulted, and informed and hands you a seating chart: who holds the final say, who is in the room. Worth having. Not what breaks the meeting. What breaks the meeting is that nobody names how the call gets made before they walk in, so the room fills with people meant to be informed afterward and advisors who start arguing as if they hold the decision.

This is the question the 6C Decision Compass is built to answer. In CLUTCH it is the navigation system, the part that tells you which route a decision should take. It names six modes. Command, when one person must act now. Consultative, when the leader gathers input and then decides. Collaborative, when a stronger solution gets built together. Consensus, when shared agreement matters more than speed. Core Expert, when authority should flow to whoever knows most. And Choice, when the decision is handed off in full.

Each mode carries what a role on a chart cannot. It names how the call gets made – out loud, in the moment, for the decision in front of you.

Picture the same meeting run differently. A leader has to decide on a senior hire. Before anyone sits down, she says it plainly. “This is Consultative. I’m making the call. The three of you are here because you see angles I don’t, so push hard. Everyone else, I’ll walk you through it once it’s made.” Four people in the room instead of twelve. The conversation runs twenty minutes, not ninety. The three advisors argue freely because they know they are advising, not competing for the final say. She decides. She explains it to the rest the next morning.

Nothing about the org chart changed. One sentence changed. And look at what the one sentence bought. Speed, because the room emptied. Clarity, because everyone knew the mode. Trust, because the advisors could push hard without anyone reading it as a power grab. That is not the absence of a system. That is a system working, in the only place a system ever runs, which is the moment the decision arrives.

The authors’ own findings give the game away. Two of them break companies most often, and neither is a case for a better chart.

The first is that decision rights die in a file. A leader builds the chart, hands it down, and assumes people will play their parts because the document exists. They rarely do. The authors read this as a buy-in problem and prescribe co-creating the chart so people remember it. But a mode is not information you store. It is an instruction you speak. “This is a Core Expert call. You own it.” That sentence does work no document can do, because it reaches the person in the moment they need to act, not in a file they have already closed.

The second is the one I see most in founder-led companies. People get stuck in the same roles. The founder is always accountable. Everyone below is always responsible.

One founder I work with kept the renewal call with her largest account, the way she had since the company was eight people. Her head of customer success had flagged twice that the client’s new procurement lead was raising implementation questions the founder, four layers removed from the rollout, could not answer in the room. She ran the call anyway, gave a confident answer that proved wrong, and spent six weeks rebuilding a relationship her CS lead could have protected in one sentence. The account renewed, at a discount the client demanded as insurance against the gap they had just watched open.

That is Core Expert mode, missed. The chart did not fail her. She never named the decision as one where someone else should hold the call. The fix was not a cleaner spreadsheet. It was one question, asked before the call went on her calendar. Not who ranks highest. Who sees it clearest.

Which brings me back to the soccer field. The manager in the article is right that nobody remembered the chart. He is wrong about why. The lesson he draws, and the framing the coverage runs with, is that the players failed. People are undisciplined. People swarm the ball. People forget. But you cannot forget a mode the chart was never built to name. The advisors in that first meeting did not lose the plan. The plan never held the part that would have ended the meeting. Memory was never the problem. Design was.

The authors are right that decision tools fail. But a better chart will not save you. A chart answers who, and the meeting that runs long and produces nothing is rarely a who problem. It is a how problem. The answer is not less system. It is the part of the system these tools leave out: a single sentence, said before the meeting starts, that tells everyone how this call gets made.

Name the mode. Watch how much shorter the meeting gets.

THREE QUESTIONS BEFORE YOUR NEXT BIG DECISION

Before the next call that fills your calendar with a meeting, sit with these:

  1. Think back to the last decision that ate up a full meeting and still ended without a call. What got named before anyone sat down, and what got assumed?

  2. When you hand a decision to someone else, what do you say out loud – who owns it, or how the call gets made?

  3. Look at one decision on this week’s calendar. Which of the six modes does it call for, and who still needs to hear you say so?

Most of the organizations I work with – founders, leadership teams, schools, nonprofits, government agencies – arrive at the same place: clean charts of who decides, and no shared language for how. The work is building the infrastructure that closes that gap.
