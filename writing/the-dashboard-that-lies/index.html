The Dashboard That Lies

Matt Goode25 June 20262 min read Get new pieces by email

# Winning feels like a near miss

On paper, Jordan’s team looked flawless. Targets hit. Calendar full. Decks immaculate. Jordan herself was magnetic, composed, always on. Every signal you could measure read momentum.

Then a colleague said the quiet part out loud: “It’s like all the lights are green, but nobody’s actually driving.”

She wasn’t wrong. Turnover had started to climb. Meetings carried a low static of tension no one named. Jordan hadn’t checked out – she’d disconnected, holding the performance together while the thing underneath ran dry. The team felt it. They just didn’t feel safe saying it.

A dashboard can lie by telling the truth

Her dashboard wasn’t faking numbers. It was measuring the wrong ones. She was reading outputs – deliverables and deadlines and presence – and ignoring inputs, the reserves that made those outputs possible. Green light, empty tank.

Viktor Frankl, writing after the camps, located human freedom in a single gap: the space between what happens to us and how we respond. That gap is the most important mechanism a leader has, and the easiest to override. Emotional intelligence is the clutch that lives inside it. It’s what lets you shift gears without grinding, because it buys you the half-second between stimulus and reaction where the choice actually is. It’s also what lets you read the dashboard that matters – the one tracking whether your people’s capacity can sustain the pace – before something expensive breaks.

Outputs vs inputs – the dashboard most leaders watch, and the one that warns them in time.

And it does get expensive

Disengagement isn’t a mood. It’s a cost. Gallup has put the global productivity drag of it in the trillions, which is a number too big to feel. On your team it lands smaller and sharper: the A-player who stops offering ideas, the problem that stays buried until it’s a crisis, the resignation that blindsides you on a Tuesday. Every one of those was visible months earlier on the right dashboard. None of it was on the one Jordan was watching.

Here’s the uncomfortable part. The leaders most at risk of this are the high performers, because they’re the best at producing clean outputs while the inputs fail underneath. The dashboard rewards them right up until the engine seizes. Reading the real gauges isn’t softness. It’s the most practical thing a leader can do, because it’s the only version where you see the breakdown coming in time to prevent it.

For the skeptics: this isn’t feelings for their own sake. A resignation you didn’t see coming runs into six figures by the time you backfill and ramp, and the warning signs sat on the input gauges for months. EQ isn’t the soft corner of the dashboard. It’s the early-warning corner, and it’s the only one that buys you time to act before the cost is locked in.

Check the gauges you’ve been skipping:

  • When did your dashboard last show green while something underneath felt off?

  • What is someone on your team feeling right now that doesn’t feel safe to say to you?

  • Are you tracking your outputs or your reserves, and what would change if you started watching the second one?
