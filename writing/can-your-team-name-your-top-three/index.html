Can your team name your top three priorities?

Resolution #2: If Your Team Can't Name Your Priorities, You Don't Have Any

Matt Goode10 December 20255 min read Get new pieces by email

# Growing faster than it's getting clearer

Last week, I asked a CEO to name his top three priorities for 2026.

He paused. Gestured vaguely. Then said:

“Growth, excellence, and… being more strategic?”

I wrote them down. Then I asked his leadership team the exact same question.

I got nine different answers.

Nine.

This is Resolution #2 in my series on building a business that holds its shape. [Previous: Audit Your Leadership System – Not Your Org Chart]

And these weren’t junior folks. These were his closest advisors – smart, seasoned, aligned, loyal. People he trusted with the company’s direction.

I turned the sheet toward him. He stared at it for a long moment, then exhaled:

“I think I’ve been managing vibes, not priorities.”

He said it with a wry smile – half embarrassed, half relieved.

But here’s what I told him:

That’s not a failure. That’s a breakthrough.

Because clarity isn’t lost through incompetence. It’s lost through assumption. And once you see the fuzziness, you can finally fix it.

Where the Confusion Actually Lives

Most founders assume unclear priorities show up as chaos or disorganization.

They don’t.

They show up like this:

A VP standing in a hallway, debating whether to approve a $12K software purchase.

She has the authority. She has the budget. She has the context.

But she hesitates.

Not because she’s unsure of the decision. Because she’s unsure of the definition.

She doesn’t actually know what “strategic” means this quarter.

Is it revenue? Platform stability? New market expansion? Retention? Runway? Margin?

So she does what smart, responsible leaders do when the stakes are ambiguous:

She escalates it. Just to be safe. Just to avoid misalignment. Just to protect the business.

Multiply that hesitation across a forty-person company, and it’s no mystery why execution slows to a crawl.

Fuzzy owner priorities don’t create chaos. They create bottlenecks.

And bottlenecks create drag. And drag creates burnout. And burnout eventually turns into resentment.

Not because people lack commitment. But because the system is producing mixed signals – and everyone is just trying not to make the wrong call.

This Is Where System Clarity Actually Comes From

In my workshops, this pattern shows up in 80% of organizations:

  • Three different strategy decks… each with slightly different goals

  • A 2023 PowerPoint still quietly steering decisions

  • A spreadsheet someone built that accidentally became the operating plan

  • A Slack thread that somehow became “the thing we’re all doing now”

Your team isn’t confused because they’re careless. Your team is confused because the system is inconsistent.

Mixed signals create mixed execution. Mixed execution creates hesitation. Hesitation creates drag.

It’s not personal. It’s architectural.

Owner clarity → team clarity → organizational velocity. It’s a straight line.

When the system sends one signal, the team moves as one. When it sends five, everyone slows down trying not to break anything.

Why This Resolution Works (Psychology Perspective)

Behavioral researchers call this implementation intentions:

Not “we’ll be more focused,” but “If it’s Q2, then our top three priorities are X, Y, Z.”

It’s the difference between: A motivational slogan and a decision-making filter.

Teams don’t need inspiration. They need pre-decisions.

The more cues you establish – when X happens, we do Y – the faster your organization moves.

Because humans don’t follow concepts. Humans follow clarity.

Your Identity Shift for 2026

This resolution isn’t about becoming a more “organized” leader.

It’s about becoming someone who can name the work so clearly that the team could execute without you in the room.

It’s the difference between walking into a leadership meeting asking “Are we making progress?” versus “Which of our three priorities moved this week?”

Same meeting. Different operating system.

That identity creates structure. Structure creates clarity. Clarity creates speed.

Because once you see yourself as the leader who names the work, not the leader who rescues the work, everything downstream becomes cleaner, lighter, and faster.

Your team stops guessing. Your meetings stop drifting. Your priorities stop competing.

And execution stops depending on proximity.

The Alignment Test (And Its Teeth)

If you want to know how aligned your company actually is, try this:

Ask five people to write down your top five priorities for 2026. No discussion. No prompting. No hints.

I ran this exercise with a founder last month.

Five leaders. Five lists.

Priority #1 ranged from:

  • “Scale revenue”

  • “Improve retention”

  • “Launch the new platform”

Same company. Same context. Same leadership team.

Three completely different definitions of “what matters most.”

When he saw the mismatched answers, he went quiet for a moment and said:

“How did I let this happen?”

He didn’t. He simply assumed alignment without ever testing for it.

Most leaders do.

Because alignment feels obvious – until you ask people to write it down.

Stop Managing Vibes – Start Naming the Work

You cannot “focus” your way into a good year. You can only name your way into one.

And that starts with answering the question most founders avoid:

“What does a good year actually look like?”

Not thematically. Not philosophically. Not aspirationally.

Operationally. Measurably. Repeatably.

So when someone tells me: “This year, we’re going to be more focused.”

I don’t nod politely anymore.

I ask: “Focused on what, exactly?”

And then I wait.

Because if you can’t answer that question crisply – your team doesn’t stand a chance.

Clarity isn’t a vibe. It’s a standard. And until the standard is visible, your organization will always move slower than it should.

Take 30 seconds: What’s the one priority your team would name if you asked them right now? (And is it the one you’d want them to name?) Drop it in the comments – I read every one.

Your 2026 Resolution

Say it out loud: “I will name the work so clearly that my team could execute without me in the room.”

This resolution sticks because identity drives behavior. Not goals. Not intentions. Identity.

Once you see yourself as the leader who names the work, your decisions start aligning with that reality automatically.

You stop managing by vibe and hoping for the best. You start creating clarity that scales – the priorities, the definitions, the measurables, the boundaries.

Because when you shift identity, the work shifts with you.
