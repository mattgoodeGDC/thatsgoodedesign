The vacation article that proves my own point

Matt Goode8 September 20267 min read Get new pieces by email

# Everything runs through me

I’m catching up on my work reading from a vacation I just got back from, which might be the most on-the-nose way possible to run into this piece. HBR ran a piece by time management coach Elizabeth Grace Saunders a while back – “Make the Most of Your Vacation When You Can’t Fully Unplug,” from July 2024 – laying out five ways to protect your boundaries on a working vacation so you still come back refreshed. I want to go through all five, because I think they solve the wrong layer of the problem.

CLUTCH came out today. The book opens and closes with the same question, asked of the same client: what happens to your business if you take two weeks off and leave your phone at home? Watching him try to answer that question, both times, is the spine the entire book is built on. The whole argument is that leaders build systems that work without them. So when I read an article treating “I can’t unplug” as a fact about certain roles, I have opinions.

First, a caveat: the thesis isn’t universal, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. A surgeon mid-procedure cannot hand off to a backup because a meeting ran long. A trial lawyer mid-cross-examination can’t reschedule. Some roles carry irreducible, moment-specific expertise no system can distribute in real time. That’s real, and it’s a small minority of the people this article, or this book, is actually talking to. Most people who say “I can’t unplug” are not that lawyer or doctor. They’re a founder or a manager who built a business that only runs through them, and mistook that dependency for a fact of the job instead of a choice made, mostly by accident, a hundred small decisions ago.

Elizabeth Grace Saunders is a time management coach. Her HBR piece, “Make the Most of Your Vacation When You Can’t Fully Unplug” (sorry, paywall) argues that for people who genuinely cannot go fully offline on vacation, real rest is still possible through disciplined boundary-setting: communicate your absence in advance, designate a point person for true emergencies, keep any necessary contact asynchronous rather than scheduled, resist engaging with non-urgent items, and predefine your working hours so work doesn’t quietly expand to fill the whole trip.

Pitfall one: not telling people you’re away. The advice is to put up an out-of-office message before you go dark, so people aren’t left wondering. Fair, and worth doing regardless. But notice what the auto-responder actually fixes: your anxiety about people not knowing where you are. It doesn’t touch what happens after they read it. If your team still needs to know you’re unreachable because everything routes through you anyway, the message is managing your discomfort, not their capability. A team holding real authority doesn’t need advance notice of your absence, because your absence doesn’t change what they’re allowed to decide.

Pitfall two: scanning for emergencies. The fix here is a designated point person, briefed on exactly what counts as urgent. This is the one I already know well, because it’s the exact gap CLUTCH names. Naming a point person is a communication move. It only holds if that person already carries license, the standing authority to act without asking, and not just a title that sounds like they should. I’ve watched this fail more times than I can count. An ops lead is responsible for vendor renewals on paper. A contract change comes in while the founder’s away. She doesn’t approve it. She drafts an email: “Wanted to check before I sign off on this; let me know when you’re back online.” The title said she owned it. The habit said otherwise. Most backups have the title. Almost none have the license. So when the real call comes in, they forward it to the beach, the rental car, the middle of dinner. The out-of-office message did its job. The org chart didn’t do its job.

Pitfall three: committing to fixed meeting times. The advice is to keep things async and informal instead of locking a call to a specific hour, since travel schedules slip. Sensible, as far as travel logistics go. But it assumes the only reason a vacation meeting exists is scheduling friction. If a call has to happen live, on your voice, during your vacation, because a decision genuinely cannot move without you in real time, the problem was never the rigidity of the time slot. It’s that the decision was scheduled around your calendar instead of handed to someone else’s authority in the first place.

Pitfall four: engaging in non-urgent items. The advice is to resist the urge to answer “just one quick thing” while you’re supposed to be off. This is willpower management. It’s advice to build a better wall around yourself, not to build a team that stops sending things over the wall in the first place. If people keep routing non-urgent items to you on vacation, that’s a signal of habit, not a test of your discipline. Under the DELEGATE framework, part of a real handoff is guardrails clear enough that “should I check with Matt” doesn’t even occur to the person doing the asking, because they already know they’re allowed to decide it themselves.

Pitfall five: leaving your work hours undefined. The fix is to predefine your working blocks for the trip in advance, frontloading the heavier ones early. Good time management, for someone who’s already accepted that some portion of the trip has to be work. My argument runs the other way. The size of the work block a leader has to carve out of vacation measures how much authority they haven’t handed off yet. Zero delegation means every day needs a scheduled block. Real delegation means the block shrinks toward nothing, not because the leader got better at time blocking, but because there’s nothing left that only they can do.

Taken together, all five pitfalls are genuinely good advice for surviving a vacation you can’t fully leave. None of them ask why you can’t leave it in the first place. That’s the layer underneath the layer this article is written for, and it’s the one the book spends three hundred pages on.

None of that means the out-of-office message itself doesn’t matter, just that it’s doing a smaller job than people think it’s doing. Once the systems are in place, the message is decoration, not infrastructure, so it doesn’t have to be boring. As a teacher, I used to love coming up with something creative for school breaks and summer vacation instead of a normal out-of-office. My favorite:

“Mr. Goode has relocated to his secret island lair and is experiencing spotty internet coverage, owing to the fact that he buried his phone in the sand. Salt water, it turns out, is not great for phones. He will return to civilization, and to grading, on [date].”

Consulting is a different audience, so that exact one probably wouldn’t survive a client’s inbox anymore, but the instinct behind it is worth keeping. Have fun with the message. Be creative. Just make sure the systems, the point person, the license, and the guardrails are already in place before you write it, because the auto-reply is the easy part. Something closer to what actually holds up:

“I’m out until [date]. [Name] isn’t just covering for me; she has full authority to make this call herself. If she says yes, that’s a yes.”

Or, if you want to have it both ways:

“Currently out of office practicing what I preach, which is uncomfortable, because what I preach is that you shouldn’t need me for this. [Name] has full authority on anything urgent. Prove me right.”

The joke only lands if someone real is behind it and can actually say yes.

My client answered that question differently the second time I asked him. Not because his role changed. Because the business under him did.

Bonus material: if you’re in need of an out-of-office soon, consider one of these:

  1. “I’m away until [date]. For anything on [project], [name] has my full trust and full authority to make the call herself – I set that up before I left so you’re never stuck waiting on me. If something still feels like it needs me specifically, I’d genuinely love to hear about it when I’m back.”

  2. “I’ll be offline through [date]. [Name] knows the full picture and is fully equipped to act on it, so please don’t hesitate to bring things to her. Anything that truly needs me will still be here when I’m back, and I’ll be glad to dig in then.”

  3. “I’m away until [date]. I’ve handed real decision-making on [area] to [name] for the time being, not just the tasks, but the actual authority to decide. Please treat her answer as mine. I trust her completely, and I hope that trust comes through in how I’ve set this up.”

  4. “Out of office through [date], and intentionally hard to reach, by design, not by accident. [Name] isn’t relaying messages to me; she’s genuinely empowered to make the calls herself. Please give her the same confidence you’d give me. She’s earned it.”

And of course:

“Out of office, on a beach, reading CLUTCH, which feels like the right way to spend the week the thing finally came out. [Name] has full authority on anything urgent through [date]. Everything else can wait until I’m back, tan and slightly ahead of schedule for once.”
