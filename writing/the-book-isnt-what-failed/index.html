The Book Isn’t What Failed

Matt Goode21 July 20264 min read Get new pieces by email

# Winning feels like a near miss

I’m self-publishing a novel right now, working with a book coach, Donna Knutson, who’s kept me honest about the production and rights decisions I didn’t know I needed to make. A few weeks ago I sat in a virtual workshop run by Molly Seabrook of Credible.Ink. Her line has been sitting with me since: don’t let your book land with a thud.

When a book lands in silence, Molly’s point is, it’s almost never because the writing was bad. It’s because no one had context for it. Never seen it. Never heard of the author. No reason to care yet. The book didn’t fail. There was nowhere for it to land.

I’ve watched the exact same failure mode in founder pitches for two decades, and I never had the language for it until Molly handed it to me.

The pitch isn’t the problem. The absence is.

A founder walks into a room with a strong deck, a real product, a defensible model – and gets a pass. The instinct is to rewrite the deck. Sharpen the ask. Cut two slides. Sometimes that’s the right call. More often, the deck was never the variable that mattered. The investor had no context for this founder, this category, this bet, before the meeting started. The pitch was the first touch, not the fortieth. It was asking a stranger to catch something they hadn’t been warming up to catch.

Self-publishing removes the gatekeeper but not the problem – it just moves it later. There’s no agent to reject you and no one to blame the silence on. The book goes up, and either there’s an audience already leaning toward it or there isn’t. Skipping audience-building because you skipped querying is the same mistake in a different sequence.

The fix isn’t a bigger launch. It’s a longer runway – showing up consistently, in public, long before there’s anything to sell. That’s slow, unglamorous, and it’s the part almost everyone skips, in a book launch or a company launch.

Three skills get conflated into one, and only one of them is optional.

Every first-time author is carrying three separate skill sets and pretending it’s one job: the expertise itself (which they usually have), the craft of putting it on the page (maybe), and everything else – positioning, production, distribution, the slow work of being findable before anyone’s looking. Most people budget time and money for the first two and assume the third will sort itself out on launch day.

It won’t. Launch day arrives, and the audience that was supposed to materialize on its own simply doesn’t, because “on its own” was never how it was going to happen.

Ownership is the question nobody asks until it’s too late.

Self-publishing doesn’t remove this risk; it just relocates it. Whose name is the KDP account under? Who’s listed as publisher of record on the ISBN? If a designer or formatter touches the files, who owns the source when the project’s done? None of it feels urgent while you’re building. All of it becomes the whole problem the day you need to update, move, or prove you own what you made.

Founders skip the identical question constantly – with platforms, with early co-founder agreements, with vendors who quietly become infrastructure. The account question is a control question wearing a boring disguise.

The wrong metric will make you feel like you’re failing when you aren’t.

Authority nonfiction – the kind of book that exists to open doors, not top bestseller lists – gets judged by writers on the wrong scoreboard. Sales numbers, royalty checks, launch-week rank. Almost none of that is the point. The book is a container for the thinking and a credential for the rooms it gets you into afterward. Judge it by that, and a “quiet” launch looks completely different.

Founders do this to themselves too – measuring a new initiative by a metric that was never actually the goal, then concluding the work failed when the goal was simply mismeasured.

It’s the same reason companies keep promoting people into roles nobody built a runway for, then wonder why the readiness gap shows up later as a crisis. Organizations grow people into leadership roles faster than they grow leadership capability. Same absence, different room.

I sometimes see the publishing version and the founder version in the same week. A client gets handed a VP title with no decision rights to go with it, and six months later everyone’s confused why nothing moves without the founder in the room. Nobody built the context for that person to be believed as a leader before they needed to act like one. It’s not a confidence problem. It’s not a skills problem. It’s the exact gap Molly’s describing, dressed up as an org chart instead of a book jacket.

None of this is really about books. It’s about what happens to anything – a product, a pitch, a person – that shows up expecting to be caught by a market that was never warmed up to catch it.

Build the place before the thing arrives. The thing was probably fine.
