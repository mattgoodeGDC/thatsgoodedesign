“Restricted Interests” or Creative Genius? How AI Might Finally Serve the Kids We’ve Failed

Matt Goode22 January 202610 min read Get new pieces by email

# AI# Neuroinclusion

Two recent podcast conversations stopped me in my tracks this week. Not because they were explicitly about neurodiversity – only one was – but because both arrived at conclusions that validate everything I’ve been writing about neuroinclusive leadership, creative potential, and who our systems actually serve.

Mimi Ito, a professor at UC Irvine with PhDs in both anthropology and education, talking with Mitch Resnick (creator of Scratch) on the Lifelong Kindergarten Podcast.

  • Link here.

Rebecca Winthrop, director of the Center for Universal Education at Brookings, talking with Ezra Klein on The Ezra Klein Show.

  • Link here.

Different research traditions, different starting points. Same destination.

And both, surprisingly, offered genuine hope for how AI might finally create learning environments that work for the kids our current systems have failed.

The Label That Changes Everything

Here’s the core insight that ties these conversations together:

When a neurotypical kid obsessively pursues robotics, designs escape rooms about presidential assassinations, or spends hours building in Minecraft, we call them an “explorer.” We celebrate their creativity. We talk about how they’ve “found their spark.”

When an autistic kid does the same thing, we call it a “restricted interest.” We pathologize it. We design behavioral interventions to redirect them toward more “appropriate” engagement.

Same behavior. Different neurotype. Opposite interpretation.

Ito names this directly. She describes how autistic learners get pathologized for deep, specialized focus – what clinicians label “restricted interests” as part of how autism gets “medicalized.” But in her connected learning framework, that same capacity for intense focus is a foundation to build on, not a deficit to remediate.

“Some of the strengths that autistic learners have,” Ito explains, “which is really deep and specialized interests, a lot of focus, sociality that’s centered around interest versus the more neurotypical status, social hierarchy oriented socializing – how can we build on that as a strength rather than pathologize that as a deficit?”

Read that again: sociality centered around interests rather than neurotypical status, social hierarchy-oriented socializing.

This reframes everything. The autistic kid who won’t engage in small talk but will talk for hours about their special interest isn’t socially deficient. They’re operating on a different social logic – one organized around shared passion rather than social positioning. And in the right environment, that’s not a weakness.

It’s a superpower.

The Neurodivergent Kids Hiding in “Passenger Mode”

Winthrop’s research identifies four modes of student engagement: passenger (coasting), achiever (chasing perfect outcomes), resistor (avoiding and disrupting), and explorer (genuinely loving what they’re learning).

Here’s what caught my attention: she explicitly names neurodivergent kids as one population that ends up in passenger mode – not because they’re lazy or unmotivated, but because the system doesn’t work for them.

“You could have a neurodivergent kid,” Winthrop explains. “Kids don’t feel they belong and so they’re not tuning in. They’ve missed certain pieces of skill sets that they really need. Knowledge and education is cumulative in many ways and they get kind of overwhelmed.”

These kids aren’t disengaged because they lack capacity. They’re disengaged because the environment was never designed for how they learn. They’re in passenger mode because the vehicle was built for someone else.

And here’s the cruel irony: many neurodivergent kids are natural explorers – capable of extraordinary creative engagement when they find their domain. But our systems are so poorly designed for them that they never get the chance to activate. They spend their school years in survival mode, masking, compensating, falling further behind – their creative potential untapped.

“I Solve Problems Differently”

Mitch Resnick shared one of my favorite examples during his conversation with Ito. An 11-year-old on the autism spectrum created a Scratch project explaining what it’s like to be autistic. In it, he says:

“I solve problems differently. I sometimes do it in ways even my teacher couldn’t even have thought of.”

He ends with: “The most important lesson is you be you.”

And: “If everybody thought the same way, the world would be a boring place.”

This kid understands something that most organizational leaders still haven’t grasped: cognitive diversity isn’t a problem to be managed. It’s a creative advantage to be leveraged.

The Scratch community, Resnick notes, has a large subcommunity of young people on the spectrum who thrive there – precisely because it’s an environment where you can follow an interest deeply, show your work, and be encouraged rather than redirected.

Ito points out that this isn’t coincidental. Environments like Scratch, Minecraft communities, gaming spaces, and fan communities have always “overindexed for neurodivergent kids.” These are spaces where, as she puts it, people “have permission to really geek out and go deep on interests that other people might not be interested in, and to talk forever about them.”

Those are exactly the behaviors that get pathologized as “antisocial or inappropriate” in mainstream settings when exhibited by autistic individuals. But in these interest-driven communities? They’re the norm. They’re celebrated.

Ito connects this to her research on otaku culture and media fandom – spaces where intense, specialized interest isn’t just tolerated but expected. Where the social currency is knowledge and passion, not status and conformity.

What If AI Could Create More of These Spaces?

Here’s where both conversations surprised me with genuine optimism.

Winthrop is direct: “Another use case that I think is really great is neurodivergent kids. Super helpful. There are all sorts of kids who have different learning differences that struggle in school, don’t have access to the specialists that they need, that would benefit greatly from being in a classroom where they could have a little assistant to help them navigate.”

She speaks from personal experience: “My youngest son has dyslexia, and the sort of read and write text-to-speech, speech-to-text has been game-changing for him.”

This isn’t hypothetical. Assistive technology has already transformed possibilities for many neurodivergent learners. AI extends that dramatically.

Think about what a well-designed AI learning companion could offer a neurodivergent kid:

  • Infinite patience. No sighing when they ask the same question twelve different ways. No social penalty for needing more time or different explanations.

  • Interest-following capacity. An AI can follow a kid’s interest wherever it goes – across disciplines, into obscure corners, at whatever depth they want to explore. It doesn’t need to redirect them to the curriculum.

  • No social performance required. For kids who find neurotypical social demands exhausting, an AI interaction removes that cognitive load. They can focus on learning instead of masking.

  • Adaptation without judgment. Visual learner? Here’s a diagram. Need it explained through a metaphor about trains? Done. Want to approach the math problem backwards? Let’s try it.

  • Availability that matches their rhythm. Neurodivergent kids often don’t operate on school schedules. They might be ready to dive deep at 10 pm or need to process for three days before engaging. AI doesn’t have office hours.

Ito frames the larger possibility: AI could enable “a vision of personalized learning that is genuinely interest-driven, socially supportive in a way that is wrapped around that inherent diversity of kids’ interests and ways of socializing.”

But she’s also clear about the risk. The dominant vision of “personalized learning” in the field is really about “different pathways to a standardized outcome.” That’s not personalization. That’s more efficient conformity.

Real personalization for neurodivergent learners would mean building AI systems that support “the natural diversity of neurotypes and interests and strengths and ways of contributing” – not AI that finds more sophisticated ways to produce the same standardized outputs.

The “Best Available Human” Problem

Winthrop references Ethan Mollick’s concept: AI isn’t necessarily better than the best human for a given purpose – but it might be better than the best available human you have access to.

For neurodivergent kids, this is profound.

The ideal scenario is a trained specialist who understands their specific neurotype, can adapt instruction in real-time, follows their interests, and builds genuine relationship. But how many kids have access to that? Especially in under-resourced schools, rural districts, or families who can’t afford private specialists?

Winthrop describes one current application: AI supporting “wellness advisors who kind of fill the gap for school counselors in rural school districts where they don’t have school counselors.” The AI doesn’t replace the human – it augments a real person’s capacity to serve kids who otherwise would have no support at all.

For neurodivergent kids specifically, the gap between ideal support and available support is often enormous. AI won’t close that gap entirely. But a well-designed AI assistant that can help a kid with ADHD break down an overwhelming assignment? An AI that can explain concepts seventeen different ways until one clicks for a kid with a processing difference? An AI that lets a dyslexic kid engage with grade-level content through audio while they build reading skills separately?

That’s not replacing human connection. That’s creating access that didn’t exist.

The Design Problem

Both researchers are clear: the technology isn’t the answer. The design is.

“Any company that wants to work with kids in schools should be a benefit corporation,” Winthrop argues, “because legally you have a lot of companies who are creating perhaps really good stuff if used well that they have to maximize profits. They can’t maximize social benefit and well-being.”

Ito makes the parallel point about adult involvement: “We have to understand what that relationship really looks like, feels like, especially for children, and what is good about it and what is different about it, as well as the risks.”

This is where I get concerned. The AI tools racing to capture the education market aren’t being designed with neurodivergent kids and the specialists who understand them. They’re being designed by engineers optimizing for engagement metrics and market share.

The Dutch government, Winthrop notes, is doing something different – partnering with teacher unions, academics, and tech companies to experiment before rolling out. That’s the model. You can’t build AI that serves neurodivergent learners without neurodivergent people and neurodiversity experts in the development process.

The Creativity Crisis Is a Neurodiversity Crisis

Ezra Klein opens his conversation with Winthrop by noting a troubling trend: in 1976, about 40% of high school seniors had read six or more books for fun. Today, about 40% haven’t read a single book. Test scores are declining. Professors report that students can’t engage with the reading they’ve assigned for decades.

I hear that, and I think: are kids the problem, or is the curriculum?

I recently had a conversation with some friends about the books we read in middle school. The Lord of the Rings was a popular choice – books we discovered on our own, devoured on our own time, talked about endlessly with each other. Meanwhile, what books were offered in the actual middle school curriculum? Dead dogs. Old Yeller. Where the Red Fern Grows. Sounder. How sick is that? The first assigned book I remember actually enjoying was Lord of the Flies. After that, it was The Grapes of Wrath, and my only initial interest was because my grandmother was John Steinbeck’s neighbor in Sag Harbor. I ended up loving it, but it’s not a title I would have picked up on my own.

Maybe that’s an argument for exposing kids to a range of topics. But here’s the thing: if you want kids to read, forcing books they have zero interest in is a killer. For someone like me with dyslexia, it was already hard enough. Every assigned book that didn’t connect was another confirmation that reading wasn’t for me.

Except I was never a reluctant reader. Honestly, swear to god, my parents read me the dictionary as a bedtime story. By request. Each word was a discovery. When I was older, I inherited my parents’ 1960s encyclopedia set – Wikipedia before Wikipedia, for you kids – and I could explore any and all topics to my heart’s content. Hours disappeared.

In all fairness, it was the Peanuts Dictionary; I wasn’t exactly some uber prodigy. Just a kid interested in learning.

In any case, I wasn’t the problem. The curriculum was. I was a voracious learner. Just not for dead dogs and standardized reading lists.

What’s Actually at Stake

Ito frames it clearly: “That natural diversity is a societal and cultural strength, right? Rather than a weakness.”

When we design systems that reward compliance over exploration, that pathologize deep interest, that treat cognitive difference as a deficit – we don’t just fail neurodivergent kids. We fail everyone. We systematically select against the creative capacity we claim to value.

Winthrop emphasizes that we need people who can “come up with creative new solutions to things, which is not really what traditional public education has been about.” In an uncertain world where AI handles routine cognitive tasks, the premium is on exactly what neurodivergent minds often excel at: pattern recognition across domains, intense specialized expertise, novel approaches to problems, thinking that doesn’t follow conventional paths.

The researchers are converging on the same principles:

  • Deep interest is a feature, not a bug – whether we call it “explorer mode” or “restricted interest.”

  • Environments shape what’s possible – you can’t motivate people into creativity; you can only create conditions where it emerges (Remember those choose-your-own-adventure books? IYKYK)

  • Cognitive diversity is a collective asset – not a problem to be accommodated, but a strength to be architected for

  • AI could expand access dramatically – but only if designed for genuine diversity rather than efficient conformity

As that 11-year-old Scratch creator put it: “If everybody thought the same way, the world would be a boring place.”

He’s right. And it would also be a less innovative, less resilient, less capable place.

The question isn’t whether we can afford to design for neurodiversity. It’s whether we can afford not to.

And with AI, for the first time, we might have tools that could actually meet neurodivergent learners where they are – if we’re wise enough to build them that way.

This is the work I do with organizations every day: moving from individual accommodation to systematic architecture that leverages cognitive diversity as a strategic advantage. The same principles that could transform education are transforming workplaces right now.

If you’re thinking about how to build neuroinclusive systems – whether you’re leading a school, a startup, or a Fortune 500 team – the design challenge is the same: stop optimizing for conformity and start architecting for the full range of human cognitive diversity.
