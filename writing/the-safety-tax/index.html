3. The Safety Tax

Essay 3 of 5

Matt Goode19 November 20257 min read Get new pieces by email

# Psychological safety

What fear costs your team – and why the quietest people may be your most expensive

Several years ago, I worked with a team of subject matter experts. On paper, they looked like the kind of group that should have been sprinting. But they weren’t. They were stuck – constantly missing deadlines, continually reworking deliverables, and constantly surprised by issues they felt they “should have caught earlier.”

At first glance, it looked like a resourcing problem. They said what many teams say: “We just don’t have enough people to move faster.” But as we listened more closely, a different storyline emerged – one that research from Harvard, MIT Sloan, and Stanford has documented for years.

It wasn’t capacity.
It wasn’t competence.
It wasn’t commitment.

It was fear – subtle, quiet, and everywhere.

No one wanted to raise red flags until it was too late.
Feedback, when given at all, tended to float around the edges of the real issue.

Meetings were polite but shallow – what psychologists call “surface acting,” where people perform collaboration without engaging emotionally or intellectually.

Retrospectives felt like rituals, not learning environments.

This wasn’t dysfunction in the dramatic sense. It was something far more common and far more costly: a climate where people didn’t feel safe enough to tell the truth early. And when fear enters a system, it behaves like a tax.

We eventually named what was happening: the safety tax – the hidden cost of a culture where people hold back. And like most taxes, it doesn’t take one big bite. It accumulates quietly, quarter after quarter, in rework, in delayed concerns, in cautious decisions, in creativity dampened before it could even be offered.

You feel it in:

  • Rework that could’ve been avoided if someone had spoken up sooner.

  • Silence that masks early risk signals until the cost is higher.

  • Missed innovation, because the boldest ideas are often the most vulnerable to offer.

  • Decision latency, the delay between when someone sees a problem and when they feel safe enough to name it, is one of the strongest predictors of project slippage.

The quietest people on that team weren’t disengaged; they were calculating risk. And those calculations – invisible to leadership – were the most expensive part of the system.

As Edmondson has written repeatedly, “fear is the enemy of forward motion.” But McKinsey’s organizational research adds dimension: fear also erodes decision quality. When people filter their insights for safety rather than accuracy, leaders make decisions with incomplete data – and the cost multiplies downstream.

So the question isn’t: “Why won’t my people speak up?”

The real question is: “What have we designed – intentionally or unintentionally – that makes speaking up feel costly?”

Because once you can see the tax, you can finally begin to reduce it.

The most expensive thing on your team may be the thing no one is saying

Psychological safety isn’t just about how people feel. It’s about how much it costs to do the most basic, everyday acts of collaboration – asking for help, naming a mistake, questioning an assumption, offering a dissenting view. These behaviors form the backbone of learning organizations, and yet they’re the first to erode when fear creeps in.

Research across industries – from Edmondson’s early hospital studies to later work at Google, MIT, and INSEAD – shows a clear pattern: when these behaviors become risky, people don’t stop doing the work. They simply stop doing the learning that makes the work resilient. They adapt, yes – but not upward. They adapt around the system. And that detour? That’s the tax.

Let’s name it:

Hesitation before speaking up = Delayed insight

A Stanford study on “voice suppression” found that even a few seconds of hesitation can change the course of a meeting – the moment passes, the idea withers, and the team moves forward with incomplete data.

Avoiding difficult feedback = Compounded conflict

Unaddressed tension doesn’t fade; it calcifies. What could’ve been a five-minute correction becomes a five-week misalignment.

Withholding concerns = Rework and reputational risk

This is the pattern McKinsey documents in “decision bottleneck” cultures: the risk isn’t the mistake – it’s the delay in naming it.

Over-prepping for routine meetings = Burnout by bureaucracy

Organizational psychologists call this “defensive preparation.” It inflates emotional labor, increases cognitive load, and leads to the very burnout leaders often misdiagnose as “underperformance.”

Postponed decisions = Slower cycle times, unclear ownership

The research is clear: fear lengthens decision cycles more reliably than complexity does. These aren’t soft costs. They show up in project slippage, team churn, scope creep, and strategic confusion. They show up in the quiet resignation of smart people who stop offering their best thinking because it no longer feels worth the interpersonal risk.

And here’s the most important part: The longer these behaviors go unnamed, the faster they normalize. Silence becomes strategy. Over-preparation becomes expectation. Caution becomes culture.

Ask yourself:

Where in your system do these costs already exist – and what might your team accomplish if they didn’t have to pay this tax every day?

Measuring the tax: A trust diagnostic

Start with five questions, drawn from and adapted from Edmondson’s research, to help you locate fear-based behavior. Use a 1–5 scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree):

  1. It is difficult to ask other members of this team for help.

  2. When someone makes a mistake, it is often held against them.

  3. In this team, people are sometimes rejected for being different.

  4. Conflict is avoided, even when it needs to be addressed.

  5. Concerns are often escalated too late.

I’ve also included a simple Google Form – built on Edmondson’s five questions – that you can copy and use to track this dimension of psychological safety with your team. Click here for a copy of that form.

These questions may look simple, but their predictive power is anything but. Reverse-coded items like these are some of the strongest markers of psychological unsafety identified in Edmondson’s original work and later replicated in studies across aviation, healthcare, tech, and finance. When people struggle to ask for help, fear judgment for mistakes, or hide differences, it signals a workplace where emotional risk feels too high – and where collaboration becomes laborious rather than liberating.

If these resonate – especially the reverse-coded items – you are not simply managing a team. You’re managing the unintended consequences of fear.

And fear leaves fingerprints everywhere.

Research from the Academy of Management Journal shows that when employees believe mistakes will be penalized, they shift from learning behavior to self-protective behavior. Teams become more compliant, less candid. Conflict gets displaced rather than resolved. Problems are managed privately until they become too big to hide, which is why late escalation is one of the most evident signs that safety is eroding beneath the surface.

This is not a sign that your people lack courage. It’s a sign that your conditions are shaping their behavior in ways that inhibit truth, trust, and forward motion.

Ask yourself:

  • What are these questions revealing about the emotional math your team members are doing before they speak?

  • And how might your system be unintentionally teaching them that silence is safer than contribution?

Because when reverse-coded items score high, they don’t just describe the culture – they diagnose it.

What the research tells us – and what leaders forget

In Edmondson’s landmark study on clinical teams, she discovered something profoundly counterintuitive: the teams with the highest error rates weren’t worse – they were safer. Nurses and physicians on those teams felt secure enough to report problems, surface near misses, and document mistakes transparently. Their error rates reflected reality, not competence. As Edmondson noted, “the better the climate, the more people speak up.”

In contrast, teams with low reported error rates weren’t more effective – they were simply quieter. They hid mistakes, worked around them, or tried to fix issues privately rather than risk the interpersonal exposure of reporting them. What leaders often misinterpret as “clean performance” is, in practice, what organizational psychologists call “error silence.” The literature is clear: low error reporting is often a sign of high interpersonal risk, not high capability.

What’s striking is how consistently this pattern appears across sectors. Aviation safety studies show that near-miss reporting is highest on flight crews with strong psychological safety climates. Manufacturing research documents that teams with open reporting catch defects earlier and reduce downstream failures. Education and healthcare studies reveal that faculty and clinicians who feel psychologically safe share concerns earlier – leading to earlier interventions and measurably better outcomes. And yes, the same findings have been reproduced in tech and finance: the most innovative teams are often those most willing to surface small failures quickly.

The absence of reported problems does not indicate health. It often indicates fear.

And when fear becomes cultural – when it shapes the micro-behaviors, the unspoken rules, the informal channels people use to avoid exposure – the tax becomes exponential. The system pays not just once, but repeatedly: in silence, in rework, in missed opportunities to course-correct before the cost grows.

So ask yourself:

  • What “clean metrics” in your system might actually be quiet metrics?
    Where is the lack of noise masking the lack of safety?

  • And what would become visible – and fixable – if people believed the truth would not be punished?

Because the goal isn’t eliminating errors.
It’s surfacing them early enough that they no longer define the work.
