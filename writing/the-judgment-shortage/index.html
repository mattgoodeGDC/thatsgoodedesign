The Judgment Shortage

Matt Goode27 June 20268 min read Get new pieces by email

# 6C

AI made human judgment the most valuable thing a person brings to work. Three institutions spent twenty years making sure there’s almost none of it left.

N. Ángel Pinillos is an epistemologist. His subject is doubt itself: how it works, where it comes from, when we’re right to trust it, and when we aren’t. He ends his Chronicle of Higher Education case for the humanities (see yesterday’s essay) on a note of real optimism, reaching for David Hume to get there. We’re only entitled to project the past into the future, Hume said, if we have reason to believe the future will resemble the past. And the future, Pinillos argues, just stopped resembling it. Large language models aren’t a continuation of the trend that hollowed out the humanities for twenty years. They’re the wrench that jams the machinery and bends the line. The bottleneck on the whole revolution turns out to be human judgment, the careful kind, trained against hard texts and harder questions. This, he thinks, might be the century of the humanities, and of his own field, philosophy.

He’s right that the wrench landed. He’s right that demand for judgment just spiked, sharply, across nearly every kind of knowledge work, all at once. That part is real, and it’s new.

Here’s the question he closes the laptop without asking: If judgment is suddenly the most valuable thing a person can bring to work, how much of it is there?

I spent a whole essay on the answer, and it isn’t good news. We’ve spent the better part of a century running systems whose real function, not the stated one, the real one, is to manufacture the opposite of judgment.

School first. The factory model that public education was rebuilt around in the late 1800s was designed, deliberately and well, to produce what the industrial economy needed: punctual, compliant people who could reliably reproduce a standard. Rows, bells, batches, a correct answer in the back of the book, and a student whose whole job is to find it. Twelve years of that, and the lesson lands as reflex. Reproduce, don’t generate. Then the MBA, which takes that reproduction-trained mind and hands it a more refined version of the same instruction: optimize, choose well between the options on the table, pick the efficient path. All of it sits downstream of a prior act that the program never teaches, which is generating the options in the first place. Then the organization, which finishes the job by rewarding the person who arrives with the answer and quietly penalizing the one who questions the frame. Three institutions, one direction, twenty years per person spent practicing the suppression of the exact capacity the machines just made scarce.

An organization trained only to optimize is an organization stuck in one gear. I ended the “They Educated It Out” essay on the cleanest way to say it: the MBA teaches you what to do when the options are on the table, and the humanities teach you to question the table. Questioning the table is a different gear. In the work I do, it has a name, Visionary, the mode that authors the frame and asks whether you’re solving the right problem at all. The pipeline runs the other gear, Operator, the mode that ships the play, indispensable and only one of six, and it never teaches the shift. Most teams can’t find the clutch.

I gave you the designer Tom Wujec’s marshmallow in that essay. In his experiment, he hands teams some spaghetti and a marshmallow that has to sit on top. The kids usually win – except against the engineers. Kindergartners build taller towers than business school graduates, because the kindergartners prototype while the MBAs plan the perfect structure and build it once. Here’s the part that matters now. AI is the marshmallow. It’s the weight nobody’s plan accounted for, the thing that turns out heavier than the theory said, and it comes down on the structure at minute seventeen. The MBA team isn’t failing for lack of effort. It’s flooring the Operator gear inside a frame nobody thought to question.

If you think the supply problem can be fixed by deciding to fix it, by funding a program and declaring it rigorous, there’s a natural experiment running right now. A movement has spent the last few years and tens of millions of dollars trying to rebuild exactly this: classical, Great Books, formation-rich humanities education on public campuses. New centers, serious money, framed photographs of Oxford on the walls. The writer Ann Manov went to look at the flagship for Harper’s this summer, and what she found is the whole supply problem in one building. The programs built to restore rigor require less reading and writing than the ordinary departments they were created to shame: twenty-seven pages a week, where the regular course assigns a hundred and twenty, excerpts instead of books, a rap video for a final. Her conclusion is the line I’d put on the wall. To pull this off, “you would need a whole new ideal of the person.”

That’s the supply problem at its starkest. Pour money and conviction into it, brand it “Oxford,” and the customer-student economy reasserts itself anyway, because formation was never something you could conjure by relabeling the syllabus. It’s the educational version of sending three managers to a storytelling workshop and calling it narrative intelligence.

So put the three of them in a room. Pinillos says the demand for judgment just spiked; I agree. Manov shows that the supply can’t be summoned even by people pouring millions of conservative dollars into summoning it. I recall Allan Bloom’s work here, and also agree. And the conclusion that neither of them quite states is the one that matters to you. The gap doesn’t close on its own, and it doesn’t close by fiat. This is the scissors: demand climbing at the steepest rate of our working lives, supply driven down on purpose by institutions doing exactly what they were built to do, the two lines crossing at the same moment. That isn’t a shift in what the market wants. It’s a shortage. And a shortage is a far harder problem than a shift, because you can’t retrain people on the thing you spent twenty years training out of them.

Watch how most companies are meeting the moment, and you’ll see the shortage hiding in plain sight. The AI rollout becomes a procurement exercise. Buy the licenses, pick the vendor, set up the governance framework, the prompt library, and the center of excellence, build the perfect plan, and execute it once. Every dollar flows into the layer the machine already owns: the optimization, the speed, the fluent output at volume. Almost nothing flows into the only input that’s scarce, the judgment required to tell whether any of that output is worth shipping.

Here’s how it plays out, and you may have already watched it. The AI goes live. The dashboards light up, more drafts, more decks, more code, faster. And six months in, the work is quicker and quietly worse: more output, more confident errors, more polished documents that are wrong in ways nobody caught, because the people shipping them were trained for twenty years to reproduce a standard, not to read a confident paragraph and find the place it’s evading the question. The organization ran the MBA’s move at company-scale. It floored the optimization gear. And the marshmallow came in heavier than the deck accounted for.

Because that’s what AI is, underneath the demos. A multiplier. It takes whatever judgment is already in the gearbox and scales it, upward if the judgment is there, into slop at unprecedented volume if it isn’t. The machine doesn’t supply the judgment. It assumes it. The capacity to read a situation and shift modes under pressure, to feel when the frame is wrong and change it, has a name in the work I do, too. EQ, the clutch. It’s the thing that lets a leader move between gears without grinding them. AI has no clutch. It assumes that you and most organizations spent twenty years driving the thing it assumes toward zero.

There’s a second cost, quieter and more expensive. When the judgment that’s left lives in one person, the one everyone routes the hard calls to, the founder who still signs off on everything, or the senior lead the whole team quietly escalates to, AI doesn’t fix the bottleneck. It widens it. It scales everyone else’s drafts and leaves that one person reviewing more of them, faster. That person is the hero worker. I spend a lot of time writing about that phenomenon. And the whole company pays for the arrangement, a standing distributed readiness tax: the price of keeping the hard judgment in one set of hands while the work happens in everyone’s. AI raises that price. It doesn’t pay it down.

So the company that wins this transition will not be the one with the best tools. The tools are a commodity; everyone has the same ones by Thursday. It’ll be the one that still has people who can generate the frame, question the premise, and hear what the confident paragraph is evading, and that knew where those people were before it needed them. You can’t buy that capacity. It is formation, and repeat after me, formation can’t be acquired by the seat.

What you can do is two things. Stop running your own internal pipeline in the same suppress-the-generator direction the schools and the business programs already ran. And distribute the judgment you already have, deliberately, out of the hero worker and into the hands that do the work, because load-tested judgment in many hands is the one thing in this whole story that compounds.

That distribution is the harder of the two, and it’s where this goes next. The systems you’d use to find and spread judgment, your hiring screens, your promotion criteria, your performance reviews, were built by the same logic that produced the shortage. They measure the optimizer and miss the generator. They reward the person who arrives with the answer, which is the single move that tells you nothing about what someone does when there’s no answer and the weight is already on the structure.

The judgment is in the building. You just can’t see it yet, because every instrument you have was built to reward its opposite. That’s the last thing to fix.

Three questions to sit with this week:

  • Where in your organization does the judgment that matters sit in a single person?

  • What did your last technology rollout invest in, the tools, or the people who have to judge what the tools produce?

  • What becomes possible when readiness is distributed instead of hoarded in one set of hands?

CLUTCH is the operating system I built for distributing exactly this. EQ is its clutch, the capacity to shift gears that the machine will never have. We built our schools on the logic of the factory floor in 1890, and the machine just made that logic obsolete; the work now is to build people who can do what it can’t.
