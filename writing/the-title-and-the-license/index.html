The Title and the License

Your best person has the job. Find out whether they have the authority.

Matt Goode25 September 20264 min read Get new pieces by email

# My role is changing# DELEGATE

There is a moment I have watched happen in a dozen companies, and it always looks like a promotion.

A founder hands someone the thing. Vendor relationships, the hiring plan, the product line, the P&L for a region. It is said warmly and it is meant warmly. You own this now.

Six weeks later that person is in the founder’s office asking whether they can spend eleven thousand dollars.

They own it. They just cannot do anything with it.

Two different things wearing one word

When we say delegation, we usually mean one act. It is two, and most organizations only ever perform the first.

A Title is the name on the work. It is who gets asked about it in the standup, whose name is on the slide, who is understood to be responsible when it goes wrong.

A License is the authority to make decisions about it. The budget, the scope, the vendor choice, the calendar, the right to say no to a request from someone senior.

Title is cheap and it feels generous. License is expensive, because giving it away means you will occasionally watch someone make a call you would not have made, and live with it.

So we give the Title and keep the License, and then we are puzzled that the person is not stepping up.

Why this is the accountability problem

Lencioni argues, correctly, that the strongest accountability in a team runs sideways rather than downward. Peers holding peers to the standard beats a manager doing it, every time.

But peers cannot hold each other to work nobody actually owns.

Watch what happens in a team where Titles have been handed out and Licenses have not. Someone’s project slips. A peer notices. And the peer does the arithmetic in about half a second: is this person actually able to fix this, or are they waiting on the founder like the rest of us? If the answer is waiting, the peer says nothing, because calling someone out for a delay they have no power to prevent is just cruelty with a process attached.

So accountability becomes theater. The person with the Title can be blamed. They cannot act. Their peers sense this and hold back, and they are right to.

The team is not conflict-avoidant. The team is being accurate.

The eleven thousand dollar test

Here is a diagnostic you can run this week without telling anyone you are running it.

Pick something you believe you have delegated. Then answer these four, on paper, before you ask the person:

  1. What is the largest amount of money they can commit without asking you?

  2. Can they say no to a request from someone more senior than they are, and would that no hold?

  3. Can they change the deadline, or only report on it?

  4. If they hire the wrong person into their team, who do you believe made that mistake?

Then ask them the same four questions and compare.

The gap between your answers and theirs is the size of the problem, and it is almost always larger than the founder expects. I have never once seen a founder guess low.

Question four is the one that does the work. If you hear yourself say that you would have made the call differently, you never handed over the License. You handed over the blame.

What a real handoff contains

A License transfer is boring on purpose. It is four sentences, written down, in the place the work lives:

  • The decision rights. What they can decide alone, what they decide and tell you, what they bring to you first. Name the dollar figure.

  • The boundary. What is explicitly not theirs, so that a no from them is a real no.

  • The reporting line. What you want to know and when, which is not the same as what you want to approve.

  • The reversal rule. Under what conditions you will overrule them, agreed in advance and in daylight.

That last one is the one people skip, and it is the one that makes the rest credible. Everyone knows you can overrule them. Saying when converts an unpredictable risk into a known one, and people will take risks against a known rule that they will not take against a mood.

The part this costs you

I will not pretend this is free.

The first time someone spends the eleven thousand on something you would not have bought, you will feel it. You will have a private thought about whether they are ready. That thought is the actual test, and it is a test of you.

Because the alternative is the thing you already have. A team of capable adults with impressive Titles, all waiting outside your office, and a calendar that proves you are indispensable.

Indispensable is not a compliment. It is a diagnosis.
