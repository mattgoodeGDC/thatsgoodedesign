It's his money. It's their risk.

Fiduciary duty, public money, and the one person nobody can route around

Matt Goode17 October 202618 min read Get new pieces by email

# Everything runs through me# 6C# DELEGATE

Fair warning, saved this one for Saturday morning… About double the length of my usual essay. But I had some thoughts…

I saw one in a parking lot last week. You know the car. The one whose founder has spent the last two years becoming the story. This one still had its 30-day temporary tags.

Before I could stop myself, I said it out loud: You still thought that was a good idea?

I stand by it. A car is a purchase, and a purchase is a vote. Every new one adds value to a company whose largest shareholder is now the biggest known donor in these midterms. And it isn’t the only electric car anymore. Others exist now, many better, and to my eye this one has barely changed since it debuted. Buying it today means looking at the whole package, politics included, and deciding that’s fine.

A new Alex Gibney documentary about him includes a financial analyst who says the car company “is not automobile manufacturing or automobile retailing.” It’s closer to a religion, he argues. That explains the 30-day tags. People don’t buy into a religion on the specs.

The people I feel for bought theirs years ago, before he went all-in on politics. They bought a car. They didn’t sign up for a cause. Now some of them put stickers on the bumper explaining when they bought it. I can’t think of another car whose owners feel the need to publicly distance themselves from the company. The closest I can come is a certain fast-food chain with a very good chicken sandwich, and even its regulars don’t put disclaimers on their cars.

So there are two kinds of people in that parking lot: the ones who chose this, and the passengers who didn’t. This essay is about the passengers.

Leadership has an old word for this, and most people file it under compliance: fiduciary duty. A legal box for the general counsel. A clause nobody reads until something goes wrong.

I think of it as infrastructure. Fiduciary duty is the promise that your personal convictions won’t be paid for with other people’s exposure. It’s the load-bearing wall between what you own and what you’ve been trusted with. You don’t notice it while it holds. You notice it when someone starts knocking holes in it to make room for themselves.

The word comes from the Latin for trust, and that’s the whole idea. Anyone who leads something shared, a company, a budget, a team’s reputation, a public contract – is holding it in trust for people who aren’t in the room. The test isn’t whether a decision is legal. It’s simpler than that: who pays if you’re wrong? If the answer is anyone but you, it was never only your call.

A recent review of that documentary in The Atlantic put the danger in two sentences: “Delusions of grandeur are one thing. The real trouble comes when one person’s delusions become other people’s reality.” That’s fiduciary failure, defined by someone who wasn’t trying to define it.

So here’s a leadership teardown of a man I won’t name. You’ll recognize him anyway.

A disclosure before I start: I used to own stock in his car company. I sold every share and lived on it as severance when USAID cut my work during the reductions his efficiency office drove. So I’ve been a passenger too.

This is also the first time I’ve written about him at all. I’ve tried to stay objective by holding him to the same framework I use with every founder I coach, and by sticking to the record. Read what follows with all of that in mind.

The setup

Picture a founder whose largest single customer is the government. A Washington Post analysis counted at least $38 billion in public contracts, loans, subsidies, and tax credits across his companies. His space company, now publicly traded, gets about a fifth of its revenue from Washington. Super-voting shares give him 79% of the vote on a 42% economic stake.

This election cycle, he has put roughly $366 million of his own money into the midterms. There’s no real precedent for that. The $290 million he spent in 2024, when the White House was on the ballot, was already the record for any individual donor. The previous midterm record was $179 million, set by a Democratic megadonor in 2022. He has more than doubled it, with weeks still to go.

Put it in human terms. The most any one person can give a candidate this cycle is $3,500 per election, or $7,000 across a primary and a general. Matching his $366 million that way would require more than 52,000 Americans to each write the maximum check. He doesn’t have to, because he isn’t writing to candidates. He’s writing to super PACs, which can take unlimited sums. That’s how one person steps around a limit designed for everyone else.

To be clear, this essay isn’t about Citizens United. The ruling that opened the door to unlimited outside spending deserves its own argument, and that’s for another day. As far as I can tell, everything he has done here is legal.

But legal is the floor, not the standard. The law tells you what you can do with your own money. Fiduciary duty asks a harder question: who pays if you’re wrong? A leader can stay inside every rule and still move the risk of his bets onto people who never agreed to carry it. That isn’t a legal problem. It’s a leadership one.

There’s a quieter irony, too. Federal law bars government contractors from contributing to federal campaigns. His company can’t write that check. He can.

Follow the money

Most of it goes to the Senate. Here’s where his team says the money went JUST between July and September:

  • $158 million to the main super PAC for Senate Republicans. One of the largest known political donations in American history. On its own, it raised more than the Democrats’ Senate super PAC took in from all its donors that quarter.

  • $76 million to his own super PAC, which runs turnout in seven Senate battlegrounds and about three dozen House races. Its filings show it spent $16.6 million on Senate races by late September, led by Texas and Ohio.

  • $10 million to a super PAC backing one Senate candidate in Texas, where his space company is headquartered.

  • $32 million more to the House Republican super PAC, the Republican governors and the Republican attorneys general.

That’s only what’s disclosed. More has gone through groups that don’t name their donors.

Look closely at Texas, because that’s where his operation has been most focused. The candidate is the state’s attorney general. He took office in 2015 and was indicted that same year on felony securities fraud charges, accused of soliciting investors in a startup without disclosing his own financial stake in it. He pleaded not guilty, and the charges were eventually dismissed under a pretrial agreement that included $271,000 and 100 hours of community service. In 2023, his own party’s state House impeached him on articles alleging corruption and bribery. The state Senate acquitted him on all 16. This spring, he beat his party’s sitting senator in a runoff.

Senate Republicans’ main super PAC spent months reluctant to spend for him. Its own filing shows how that changed. On September 9, the founder gave it $75 million. The next day, it sent $75 million to a PAC backing the Texas candidate. On September 23, he gave $83 million. The next day, $83 million went to Texas. That Texas PAC spent more than $77 million on ads in September alone.

Pause on that. This essay is about fiduciary duty. The candidate getting the most attention from its subject was once charged with failing to tell investors about his own stake in their money.

Two senators his PAC has backed sit on the Appropriations Committee, which funds some of his company’s biggest contracts. One of them chairs it. Senators also confirm the people who run the agencies that buy his rockets, and they serve six-year terms, not two. If you want the most durable return on a political check, you write it to the Senate.

What does that do to a democracy? It doesn’t buy votes. It buys volume. Every race his money touches gets louder in one direction: more mailers, more door knocks, more ads, in what’s already projected to be the most expensive midterm ever, with a record $11.6 billion in political advertising. In a close Senate race, the side that can afford a bigger ground game in October can tip who shows up in November.

Big money in politics isn’t new, and it isn’t one-sided. The midterm record he just broke belonged to a left-wing donor. But there’s a difference between many wealthy donors pulling in different directions and one man, whose companies depend on the government, out-giving every donor to the other party’s Senate super PAC combined, in a single quarter. The first is a market. The second is a single point of failure.

What the last check bought

We’ve seen what this kind of spending buys. We saw it with less money.

After 2024, he got a desk inside the government and a mandate to cut it. His efficiency office went after budgets, staff, and contracts at all seven agencies where his own companies held contracts. The supplier suddenly audited his own customers from the inside.

The office closed this July. In August, the Government Accountability Office audited its claims. It couldn’t verify 96% of the reported savings from canceled grants. About $27 billion in “savings” was never executed. The office didn’t answer the auditors’ questions, and the White House says it won’t issue a final report. The website still claims $215 billion.

Who was minding the store?

Fiduciary duty runs in two directions. One is not spending other people’s risk. The other is simply showing up.

In early 2025, he headed six companies, including a public carmaker with millions of shareholders. He was also, by his own account to friends, sleeping in his government office a few minutes’ walk from the West Wing. He reportedly had a giant screen brought into that office so he could play video games. And the efficiency office he led was busy dismantling the country’s foreign aid agency, which he boasted he fed into a wood chipper.

That agency was never just a line item. A study published in The Lancet in July 2025 estimated that programs it funded prevented more than 91 million deaths over two decades. It projected that unless the cuts were reversed, they could lead to more than 14 million additional deaths by 2030, 4.5 million of them children under five. That’s a model’s projection, not a body count, and models can be wrong. But it is the fiduciary question at its starkest. Who pays if you’re wrong? Here, it isn’t shareholders. It’s people who will never know his name.

That spring, the carmaker reported a 71% drop in first-quarter profit and a 9% drop in revenue. Investors said out loud what the numbers implied: their CEO was distracted. Only then did he announce he’d step back from Washington.

He’s famous for his hours, and maybe he works more of them than anyone alive. But attention isn’t renewable. Even a genius gets the same 24 hours as everyone else, and every hour spent running a government office, or a video game, is an hour not spent on the companies whose shareholders are counting on him.

The games tell a small but revealing story. When gamers asked how he kept top-ranked characters while running six companies, he admitted others played his accounts for him. When you can’t do it all, you can hand the controller to someone else and keep your name on the leaderboard.

Shareholders can’t do that. They can’t hand off their exposure. In CLUTCH terms, he was a License holder who left one car idling in the driveway while he drove someone else’s, and the shareholders were still sitting in the passenger seat.

To be fair to him, many aerospace firms sell mostly to the government; the public market for rockets is small, and his company won many contracts by underbidding rivals.

None of that changes the problem’s shape. When one person ties a shared asset to a personal bet, the bet can be legal, sincere, even smart. It still moves the risk onto passengers. With 79% of the vote in one pair of hands, the other shareholders are in the back seat. The taxpayers aren’t even in the car.

The teardown: running him through CLUTCH

Most leadership advice treats leadership as a trait: you either have it or you don’t. CLUTCH treats it as a vehicle, a system of parts that have to work together. CREATE is the engine. VOICES is the gearbox. The 6C Compass is the navigation. DELEGATE is the handoff. And the part the book is named for holds it all together: “The connective tissue between all four is emotional intelligence, the clutch mechanism that enables smooth shifting between systems.”

The vehicle matters because of who’s riding in it. A solo driver can take whatever risks he likes. A driver with passengers owes them something, whether or not they get a say in the route.

Put this founder on the lift, and the diagnosis is the same at every station: a spectacular engine, one gear, a compass stuck on one heading, no handoff, and no clutch.

That isn’t a character study. The book is blunt that most leadership breakdowns “aren’t leadership problems. They’re design problems.” This one is a design problem: a trillion-dollar engine, one set of hands on the wheel, no brake on the passenger side, and a country full of passengers who never bought a ticket.

The engine: CREATE

The book calls CREATE “six cylinders of organizational capacity that must fire together”: Culture, Relationships, Execution, Agility, Teaching and Ethics. It’s blunt about what happens when they don’t: “When even one misfires, friction builds, capacity drops, and bottlenecks return.”

Two of his fire harder than almost anyone’s alive. Execution: rockets that land themselves and fly again. Agility: a satellite network switched on over a war zone within days of the invasion. Most leaders would trade a great deal for either one. Now listen to the other four.

  • Culture is designed. “Culture isn’t vibes. It’s designed.” When the person at the top treats shared assets as personal ones, he’s designing a culture where everyone below learns that’s how it works here.

  • Relationships are misfiring. Days after he left government, the president he’d spent $290 million to elect was publicly threatening to cancel his contracts. Now an ally is testing a workaround. And I’d bet plenty of early customers are tired of explaining their car.

  • Teaching is dead. The book’s line: “The point of leadership isn’t to create followers. It’s to CREATE more leaders.” The efficiency office closed with no final report and left nothing for the next team to learn from. An organization that won’t learn from its own record isn’t efficient. It’s just fast.

  • Ethics is knocking. Funding the senators who fund your contracts, and backing a candidate once charged with misleading investors, is a misfire you can hear from the street.

An engine running two cylinders at redline sounds powerful. It’s also exactly how engines blow, and when they do, the passengers feel it first.

The gearbox: VOICES

VOICES names six leadership presences: Visionary, Operator, Independent, Coach, Encourager, and Sculptor. The book’s diagnosis is simple: “Most leaders over-rely on one or two defaults.” Its prescription is harder: learn to “shift cleanly between all six gears.”

He has two defaults, and he redlines both. The Visionary says, Mars. The Operator sleeps on the factory floor, and later in a government office, because if he isn’t in the room, it isn’t happening. Those two gears built rockets that land. They also built organizations where nothing important moves without him.

The gears he skips are the ones that build other people. The Coach asks instead of tells. The Encourager builds belief. The Sculptor names the gap between where someone is and where they could be. Every one of those gears makes the leader a little less necessary. That’s the point, and it’s exactly the trade he won’t make.

The book is blunt about the cost: “leadership that stays locked in one gear creates dependence. Leadership that teaches others how to shift builds real capacity.” Look at who depends on him now: an ally’s army, his country’s space program, shareholders who can’t outvote him, and a party’s Senate campaign. That isn’t capacity. It’s a single point of failure with a gas pedal.

A leader stuck in one gear at highway speed isn’t bold. He’s stuck, and so is everyone riding with him.

The navigation: the 6C Compass

The book calls the 6C Compass “a decision-rights framework that clarifies who decides, how, and when.” It has six modes: Command, Consultative, Collaborative, Consensus, Core Expert, and Choice. “None of the six is better than the others. Each is correct for a specific kind of terrain.” The skill is reading the terrain, and a compass stuck on one heading can’t.

Command, used well. Command is for moments “where speed and safety demand you decide and move.” One early reader called it a fire extinguisher: essential, and rarely pulled. Switching the network on in the first days of the war was exactly the moment to pull the fire extinguisher. This year he shut down the invader’s unauthorized use of it, and Ukraine’s former defense minister credited the call with protecting the country’s air defenses and sparing thousands of civilian lives. Credit where it’s due.

Command, past its expiry date. Whether that network can be used over enemy territory touches an ally’s war plan and a government’s foreign policy. That’s Consultative at minimum: he can still make the call, but he asks the people who will live with it first. Instead, it sits on one desk. He reportedly sees it as escalation. Kyiv argues it would shorten the war. So Kyiv has started testing an alternative with a European partner. That’s what a team does when one person holds a switch it can’t live without. It quietly builds a workaround. A fire extinguisher that never gets put down isn’t safety. It’s a flood.

Command, with no name on it. This week Ukraine says its military terminals were throttled overnight, without warning, for anything moving faster than 100 miles an hour, the speed of the drones it uses to intercept Russia’s. Zelenskyy called it “a big mistake” that “will bring a lot of human losses.” Asked who made the call, the founder said such limits are “decided by the US military and ultimately” the president. The White House has neither confirmed nor denied it. A Republican senator wants the Pentagon’s inspector general to investigate. Maybe it really was Washington’s call. That’s the point. A decision this consequential, on a system this many people depend on, now has no visible owner. The Compass exists to answer one question before any other: who decides? When the answer is a shrug and a pointed finger, the passengers can’t even tell who’s driving.

Choice, mislabeled. His defenders will say the political money is Choice: his money, his call. The book frames Choice as handing over the keys, and keys come with guardrails, because a decision is only truly yours when its consequences stay with you. When they land on shareholders who can’t outvote him and taxpayers who fund his contracts, it’s no longer Choice. It’s Command over other people’s risk with a Choice label stuck on the front.

Core Expert, missing. The Core Expert mode asks one question: “Who’s best positioned to decide this?” Thousands of grant and contract cuts later, the auditors couldn’t verify most of the savings, and nearly 2,000 contracts the office said it had ended were never actually terminated. From the outside, it doesn’t look like anyone asked.

Map his biggest calls on the Compass and almost all of them land in one mode. A compass stuck on Command doesn’t point north. It points at him.

The handoff: DELEGATE

DELEGATE starts by separating the Title from the License. The Title is final accountability, and the leader keeps it. The License is operational ownership, and the leader hands it off. Then it breaks every handoff into eight parts: Define the Win, Establish the Way, List the Guardrails, Expect the Process, Give the Right Support, Agree on Checkpoints, Transfer Ownership, and Encourage Growth. Skip one, and the work comes back.

Run him through it, and he fails in both directions: as the person holding a License, and as the person handed one.

First, the License he holds. He has Title to his fortune. He holds only a License to his space company’s relationship with its largest customer, a relationship the shareholders own and the public paid to build. A License comes with guardrails. When the License holder starts acting like the Title holder, there’s no one left to enforce them. With 79% of the vote, nobody else has to agree. The same Atlantic review described power like his as “so complete that it becomes self-ratifying.” In CLUTCH terms, that’s a car with no brake on the passenger side.

Then the License he was handed. Put the efficiency office through the eight parts and count the gaps. Define the Win: its own savings tally kept growing, from $55 billion to $215 billion, and the auditors couldn’t verify most of it. List the Guardrails: it went after contracts at the very agencies that paid his companies. Agree on Checkpoints: it didn’t answer the auditors. Transfer Ownership: it closed with no final report and nothing handed on. From the outside, that’s half the handoff missing, in public.

Delegation that comes back wasn’t delegation. It was a loan. This one came back unaudited, and the taxpayers are still holding the note.

The clutch: emotional intelligence

“The clutch is what lets you pause between stimulus and response.” It lets a leader feel the engine and the road at the same time and shift without grinding metal.

Here’s the strange part: the self-awareness was there. In May he said he’d spend a lot less on politics. In July he said he’d gotten carried away and too involved. Then, starting that same month, $276 million came in.

That’s what a missing clutch looks like. The driver can hear the gears grinding. He just can’t stop pressing the accelerator.

The one in your building

Most leaders will never write a nine-figure check. But most organizations have a smaller version of this person. The founder who keeps the biggest client relationship in his own phone. The department head who spends the team’s reputation on a personal fight. The executive who cashes the board’s trust on a pet project. And behind each of them, a row of passengers.

Put your own leadership on the lift this week:

  1. CREATE. Which cylinder are you ignoring because the loud ones are firing so well?

  2. VOICES. Which gear haven’t you used in a month, and what has that cost the people around you?

  3. 6C Compass. Which decision are you calling Choice that is really Command over someone else’s risk?

  4. DELEGATE. Is the line between your Title and your License written down anywhere?

  5. The clutch. What have you already admitted you got wrong, and kept doing anyway?

The book’s last word on this is the hopeful one: “The bottleneck isn’t a character flaw. It’s a design problem.” Character is slow to change. Design isn’t.

So look at the one in your building. Look at the passengers. Then ask the question I blurted out in that parking lot: You still think that’s a good idea?

Every station on that lift is a chapter in the book.
