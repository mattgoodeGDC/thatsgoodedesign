What the auditors couldn't count

A watchdog can count awards and positions. It can't count what the people in those positions knew

Matt Goode7 October 20264 min read Get new pieces by email

# The culture is drifting# Psychological safety

On September 30, the Government Accountability Office, Congress’s nonpartisan watchdog, published its review of what happened to American foreign assistance after USAID closed.

Most of the report is counting. Between January 2025 and March 2026, about 6,780 of 7,510 USAID awards were terminated. USAID’s workforce went from about 13,600 people worldwide to 241.

Two findings stopped me, and neither of them is a number.

The State Department, which now runs what remains, could not produce data on its own staffing levels during the eleven months the auditors asked for it. It also could not show written guidance, including standards, for how foreign assistance decisions get made.

Read that as an operator. An organization responsible for about $52 billion in active awards could not say who works there or how it decides.

I worked at BHA, USAID’s Bureau for Humanitarian Assistance. I’m not going to argue here about whether the agency should have closed. That’s a policy question, and you have your own view. I want to look at something narrower, because I see it in every kind of organization I work with, from Series B startups to independent schools.

What an audit can’t see

An audit counts what it can count: awards, dollars, positions. It can’t count what the people in those positions knew.

The USAID Employee Support Fund tried. They surveyed their members, and 225 answered. Health officers, agriculture and environment officers, governance specialists, economists, engineers, and the contracting officers who oversaw how the money was spent.

A third of all respondents are unemployed. Of those working again, nearly half have left the field entirely, and about one in four are still in international development or foreign affairs.

The health officers make it concrete. Of the 26 who answered, two still work in international development. Eight have changed fields entirely.

It’s a self-selected sample, not a census. But the direction is hard to miss. The awards moved to State. The knowledge didn’t move with them.

The contracting officer is the control system

Take the contracting officers. On an org chart, a contracting officer looks like administration. In practice, that’s the person who knows why a clause exists, which partner has quietly missed three reports, where the money legally can’t go, and what happened the last time someone tried.

Those are the organization’s controls. They live in someone’s head.

That’s what connects the GAO’s two findings. When the people who made the judgment leave, nothing remains to explain how decisions were made. The GAO warns that, without documented standards, officials risk decisions that are “inconsistent, ad hoc, or inefficient.” Not because the people still there are careless, but because they never wrote the standards down. They didn’t need to be. The people were the standards.

Fast to drain, slow to fill

You probably haven’t lost 98 percent of your staff. But you’ve seen the smaller version. A founder’s first operations hire leaves and takes the vendor history with her. A school loses a division head of twenty years, and the new one discovers the schedule runs on rules nobody wrote down.

What drained wasn’t only oversight. It was application. The officer who knows which roads wash out in the rainy season, so the food has to move by June. The agriculture specialist who remembers which seed variety failed in that valley, and why. The health officer who knows which district clinic reports its numbers on time and which one needs a phone call and a visit. None of that sits in an award file. It’s the difference between money that was spent and money that worked.

Systems thinkers call this a stock. Knowledge accumulates slowly, through years of flow: projects, mistakes, relationships, the third time you see the same problem. A restructuring can drain the stock in an afternoon. Refilling it runs at the original speed, because the only way to learn which roads flood is to be there when they do. That asymmetry is the whole story, and it’s true at any scale.

Headcount is just where the stock happens to be stored. Cut the headcount, and you’ve drained the stock, whether the spreadsheet shows it or not.

Hiring costs more than growing

The Fund put it plainly: “A government cannot buy it back with a hiring announcement.” Neither can a company, and there’s data on why.

Wharton’s Matthew Bidwell studied people hired from outside against people promoted from within into similar jobs. External hires were paid about 18 to 20 percent more, scored lower on performance reviews in their first two years, and were more likely to leave. What they lacked wasn’t talent. It was the firm-specific knowledge and relationships that only time inside builds.

You pay a premium to hire judgment from outside, then wait two years for it to work. Or you grow it, and keep it.

Before you cut, map the load

If you’re about to restructure, ask four questions first.

  1. Which three decisions stall if this person leaves on Friday? If you can’t name them, you don’t know what you’re cutting.

  2. Who else could make each of those calls today, without asking? If the answer is nobody, you’ve found a load-bearing wall.

  3. Who is learning those calls right now? If nobody is, every resignation becomes a restructuring you didn’t plan.

  4. How long would it take you to list who works for you, where they work, and what they own? The State Department couldn’t do it in eleven months. Your answer should be a day.

None of this needs a consultant. It needs someone willing to look at the building before taking out walls.

The GAO made three recommendations to State. Two of them come down to this: know who works for you, and write down how you decide. That advice holds for any organization, whatever its size or mission.
