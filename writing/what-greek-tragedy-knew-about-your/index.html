What Greek Tragedy Knew About Your Organization

Matt Goode1 April 20267 min read Get new pieces by email

# The culture is drifting

Monday, I argued that the humanities aren’t soft – that what they produce isn’t a set of skills but a set of lessons, a formation that gets built into a person rather than installed. Tuesday, I was in Plato, watching him diagnose the philosopher-king problem 2,400 years before we started calling it a succession crisis: the people most likely to pursue authority are the least likely to have done the formation that makes authority worth holding.

The ancient world has more to say. We’re not done.

Sophocles understood something about power that most organizations are still refusing to look at directly.

Here’s what most people get wrong about hubris.

We use it as a synonym for arrogance – the leader who got too big for their boots, who flew too close to the sun (ha!), who believed their own press. But that’s not what the Greeks meant. Hubris in tragedy isn’t a personality flaw. It’s a structural condition. It’s the point at which a leader’s greatest strength becomes the thing that destroys them – not despite their excellence, but because of it. And crucially: because no one around them could say so.

The Greeks didn’t build an art form around bad leaders. They built it around good leaders inside systems that couldn’t save them from themselves.

Ajax – The Organization That Never Said What You Were Worth

Ajax is the greatest warrior in the Greek army at Troy. Not the cleverest – that’s Odysseus – but the strongest, the most reliable, the one who holds the line when everyone else breaks, who carries Achilles’ body off the battlefield while the rest retreat. When Achilles dies, and his armor is awarded to Odysseus instead of Ajax, the Greek leadership makes what may have been the right strategic call. Odysseus was the better choice for what came next – the cunning, the diplomacy, the long game of getting everyone home.

They just never told Ajax what else he was worth.

Ajax doesn’t accept the decision and move on. He loses his mind. Kills a flock of sheep believing they’re his enemies. Dies by his own hand. The modern read is breakdown. The organizational read is different: Ajax was never given a framework for his value beyond the single metric he’d been recognized for. His entire identity was his performance on the battlefield. When the performance wasn’t enough – when judgment and cleverness were suddenly valued over strength and loyalty – he had no other story to tell about himself.

This is the lesson from Monday that the organization failed to build around him: narrative intelligence – not just the leader’s capacity to author coherence for others, but the organization’s responsibility to author a coherent account of what its people are actually worth. The armor decision may have been defensible. The failure was everything that didn’t happen around it – the conversation that never occurred, the acknowledgment that never came, the investment in Ajax as a whole person rather than a function. High performers who are passed over don’t just need better communication about the decision. They need a relationship with their own value that can survive it.

Ajax had no such relationship. Neither do most of the highest performers currently sitting in your organization, quietly doing the work, waiting for a decision that will tell them whether they matter.

This is the Teaching cylinder in CREATE (Click here to learn more) failing – the organization’s responsibility to develop people beyond their current function, not just deploy them in it. Sophocles knew exactly what he was showing us. The Greeks lost their greatest warrior not on the battlefield but in the aftermath of a promotion decision that was never properly held.

Ajax is a development failure. The organization knew what he was worth. It just never said so.

Oedipus Rex – The System That Couldn’t Deliver the Truth

Oedipus is not a bad leader. That’s the whole point. He solved the riddle no one else could crack. He saved Thebes. He is genuinely, demonstrably competent – and that competence is exactly what makes him catastrophic. Because when the truth starts surfacing, Tiresias the prophet already knows it. Jocasta his wife already knows it. The shepherd who was there at the beginning knows it. The entire system has the information.

It simply cannot get to the person at the center.

That is not an ancient Greek problem.

That is a Tuesday morning in your organization.

Tiresias tries. He tells Oedipus the truth directly, in the very first confrontation of the play. Oedipus dismisses him, accuses him of conspiracy, and sends him away. And after that, nobody tries again. Not because they don’t know. Everyone knows. Even the audience knows. Because in a single public exchange, Oedipus trained the entire court. Every person watching ran the same calculation and reached the same conclusion. The information stayed where it was. The leader remained in the dark. The gap between what the system knew and what the center could hear kept widening – quietly, rationally, invisibly – until it couldn’t.

This is the Culture cylinder of CREATE (Click here) misfiring. The environment produces anxiety rather than trust, silence rather than signal. It doesn’t announce itself. It accumulates one dismissed concern at a time, until the organization is full of people who know exactly what’s wrong and have learned, very rationally, to say nothing.

The leader experiences this as things being fine.

Right up until they aren’t.

Oedipus is a system failure. The truth existed. The system couldn’t deliver it.

Antigone – The Leader Who Got the Truth and Refused It

Creon is also not a bad leader. He’s a competent new executive trying to establish order after chaos, making what he genuinely believes is a defensible decision. Polynices was a traitor. Traitors don’t get burial rites. The rule must hold. Every step of his logic is locally reasonable.

And every step takes him further from a position he can safely reverse.

The scene that breaks me is Haemon. Creon’s own son – measured, loyal, not a radical – comes to his father and makes the case carefully. He doesn’t challenge the principle. He challenges the application. He tells Creon what the city is actually saying, what people are whispering, that the room is not with him on this one. He is doing everything right. He is the Challenger gear of VOICES (Click here) functioning exactly as it should – naming what isn’t being said, surfacing the uncomfortable data, giving the leader the information he needs before the cost of ignoring it becomes irreversible.

Creon sends him away.

Not because the argument is wrong. Because by that point, changing his mind has become, in his own accounting, more costly than being right. Every piece of contradictory evidence gets reframed as a challenge to defend against rather than information to integrate. Haemon tries. The chorus tries. Tiresias – the same prophet who tried with Oedipus – tries a third time. Creon hears all of it. He is not, like Oedipus, operating in an information vacuum. The truth reached him.

He simply wouldn’t receive it.

Antigone is what happens when the only person willing to challenge the leader is willing to die to do it.

That’s not courage. That’s a systems failure dressed as heroism.

Creon loses his son. His wife. His city’s faith. Every bit of it was reversible right up until it wasn’t. He had the information Oedipus never got – and it made no difference. Where Oedipus is a system failure, Creon is a character failure that becomes a system failure. The outcome is identical. The root is different. And the difference matters enormously for what you build.

Three plays. Three failure modes – and they escalate.

Ajax’s organization deployed its best people without developing them and lost them to a decision that was never properly held. Oedipus’s system couldn’t get the truth to the center, and collapsed under the weight of what it couldn’t say. Creon’s organization got the truth to the leader, and watched him refuse it, which is the most expensive failure of the three, because it means the system worked and the leader didn’t.

The Greeks understood that organizational collapse is almost never the result of bad intentions or insufficient effort. It’s the result of good leaders inside systems that couldn’t do three things: develop people beyond the role they were filling, deliver truth upward, and create leaders capable of receiving it when it arrived.

Those are not character problems. They are design problems.

The Teaching cylinder, the Culture cylinder, the Challenger gear – CREATE and VOICES are two of the three portions of the CLUTCH framework (Click here), is at its root, an attempt to build what Sophocles kept showing us was missing. Not better leaders in isolation. Better systems around the leaders that exist.

Sophocles staged these as tragedies because in his world, the system couldn’t be redesigned. The gods had already decided. The prophecy was already in motion.

You don’t have that excuse.

Tomorrow I go further back – to the poem that preceded the tragedies and in some ways contains all of them. The Iliad isn’t just three failure modes. It’s the complete causal chain, told at human cost. And the person who pays that cost isn’t Agamemnon or Achilles.

It’s Patroclus.
