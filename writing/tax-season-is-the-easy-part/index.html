Tax Season Is the Easy Part

Matt Goode4 April 20262 min read Get new pieces by email

# Winning feels like a near miss

This is my first year filing as a business. Which means it’s my first year discovering just how many costs I’d been experiencing without tracking – until someone handed me a form and made me account for them.

That’s what April does. It forces a reckoning.

What it doesn’t do is send a bill for everything else.

Most organizations are paying taxes that will never appear on a return, never trigger an estimate, never generate a notice from anyone. They accumulate in the margin between what your people are capable of and what your systems are configured to let them do. They compound, quietly, until they show up as a retention problem, a missed quarter, or a decision that took three weeks longer than it should have.

The IRS wants to know what you spent. These taxes want to know what you lost.

And because nobody’s tracking them, most leaders have no idea how much they owe.

Starting on Monday, I’m going to name each one specifically.

The Trust Tax. Micromanagement isn’t a personality flaw – it’s what happens when the infrastructure for safe delegation doesn’t exist yet. The leader who can’t let go may be the only person in the room being honest about that.

The Clarity Tax. Five people deferring one decision per day is a number you can calculate. Most organizations never do. Ambiguous decision rights aren’t a culture problem. They’re a math problem.

The Meeting Tax. Your team has a meeting after the meeting. That’s the real meeting – and it’s running whether you’re in the room or not. When formal channels don’t process what they need to, informal ones adapt. That adaptation has a cost.

The Succession Tax – the one that’s already running, before anyone has left, before any crisis. Your best people can see the ceiling. They leave for organizations with somewhere to go.

The Transition Tax. Someone gets a new title, new direct reports, a new box on the org chart. Same support structure – which is to say, none. Organizations grow people into leadership roles faster than they grow leadership capability. The promotion is visible. The gap is not.

Each essay opens with a rough estimate of what that particular tax costs your organization this week. Not forensic accounting. Useful accounting. Enough to make the invisible feel real.

April ends. These don’t.

They keep running – in the hesitation before anyone tries something new, in the decisions that wait for you, in the people who left for somewhere with more headroom.

The IRS will always find a way to send the bill.

These costs won’t. That’s what makes them expensive.

Before the first essay: if someone handed you a bill for what your organization lost this week to costs that never appeared on any report, would you recognize the line items?
