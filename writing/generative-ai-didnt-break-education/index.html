Generative AI Didn’t Break Education – It’s a Leadership Test for Independent Schools

Matt Goode2 September 20257 min read Get new pieces by email

# AI# Schools

When ChatGPT hit classrooms, it didn’t sneak in quietly. It detonated.

Assignments unraveled. Grading frameworks collapsed. Students adapted overnight while many faculty and administrators scrambled to catch up. Parents worried about “cheating.” Boards demanded policy.

But here’s the truth: generative AI didn’t break education. It revealed the cracks we’d been ignoring.

For independent schools, those cracks are even sharper. Families don’t pay tuition for rote memorization, compliance-heavy assessments, or rigid content delivery. They invest in the distinct value proposition of an independent school: clarity of mission, intimacy of culture, strong faculty relationships, and the promise of whole-child development.

And this is where leadership comes in. AI isn’t just a classroom issue. It’s a leadership test – one that will determine whether schools cling to fear and rigidity or lean into adaptability, trust, and vision.

The Agency Question

This afternoon, I had a fascinating conversation with a tech entrepreneur who is also a neuroscientist. He’s as dissatisfied with today’s education system as many school leaders quietly are, but he framed it differently: in his view, AI can either strip students of agency or dramatically expand it.

In the wrong hands, AI risks making students dependent, outsourcing their curiosity and critical thinking. But in the right hands, it can act as an expert assistant – helping a young person dive deeply into engineering, history, or medicine far faster than traditional systems allow. It can accelerate learning, personalize pathways, and unlock creativity.

That tension – reduced agency versus expanded agency – cuts to the heart of independent school leadership. Will AI be allowed to erode curiosity? Or will schools model their use as a stimulant for curiosity, a tool that empowers deeper learning and self-direction?

A Chance to Get Ahead of the Wave

This entrepreneur also reminded me of the tidal wave of social media. Every educator I know can point to the damage it’s done: fractured attention spans, fragile self-esteem, rising rates of anxiety and depression. We weren’t ready, and the consequences were profound.

With AI, we have a second chance. The wave is here, but it's still early. Independent schools have the opportunity to get ahead of it – to make thoughtful, purposeful decisions now, before the technology reshapes adolescence by default.

The leaders who act in this moment won’t just protect their schools; they’ll strengthen their missions.

Rooting AI in Values

One mistake would be to look for a one-size-fits-all model. That’s not how independent schools work. Each has a unique ethos. Montessori programs, for instance, are rooted in curiosity, independence, and self-directed learning. Quaker schools emphasize reflection, simplicity, and community. Faith-based schools focus on moral and spiritual formation.

The leadership challenge isn’t: What’s the universal AI policy?

The question is: How do we integrate AI in a way that amplifies our values, rather than diluting them? For Montessori, AI might be used as a spark – a way to fuel exploration without replacing hands-on discovery. For a classical school, it could enrich Socratic dialogue by offering counterpoints or alternative perspectives. For a global school, it might provide multilingual access to knowledge and new avenues for cultural fluency.

Independent schools already have clarity of culture. Their task is to align AI with that culture so its presence strengthens identity instead of undermining it.

Faculty Culture Is the Fulcrum

If culture is the heart of independent schools, faculty are its lifeblood. Parents don’t just buy a curriculum; they invest in the teachers who deliver it. Faculty embody the ethos of the school.

That makes faculty buy-in non-negotiable.

And yet, many teachers feel anxious about AI. If their professional identity has been defined as “knowledge deliverer,” then AI looks like a competitor. If schools respond with rigid, top-down directives, resistance will only harden.

Leadership here requires humility. Heads and division directors must model curiosity themselves, creating sandbox spaces where faculty can test tools, experiment with lessons, and reflect together. After-school design sprints, cross-disciplinary pilots, or collaborative workshops can turn fear into agency.

Execution matters, too. Independent schools can move faster than public systems – but agility unused is agility wasted. Advisors experimenting with GPT-powered support tools, teachers reframing writing prompts, or department chairs testing grading workflows: these are not distractions; they are signs of intrapreneurship. Leadership’s role is to give these innovators the conditions to succeed and scale.

Students Are Already Leading

The most overlooked reality is that students aren’t waiting.

Many independent school students are already using AI not just to cut corners, but to create. They’re building prototypes, writing business plans, and testing ideas at a pace that often exceeds that of most schools.

Adults sometimes dismiss this as corner-cutting. But in truth, students are stress-testing the relevance of their education. They’re asking: Does what I’m learning here truly prepare me for the future I’m inheriting?

That’s not cheating. That’s leadership.

The question is whether schools will respond to that initiative with punishment or channel it. Innovation labs, entrepreneurship programs, or ethics electives could turn unsanctioned use into co-creation. Schools that position themselves as safe spaces for responsible AI experimentation will gain both reputational strength and enrollment advantage.

The Ethical Imperative

Parents and trustees want clarity. Teachers want guidance. Students want boundaries they can respect. Ethics is where trust will be either built or broken.

This cannot be a one-page addendum to the student handbook. It must be a lived conversation:

  • What does integrity look like when AI is everywhere?

  • Where is the line between assistance and substitution?

  • How do we balance efficiency with originality?

Independent schools are uniquely positioned to lead this dialogue. Their smaller scale allows for nuance. Their missions allow them to emphasize values as much as skills.

Leadership as Posture, Not Position

The World Economic Forum predicts that 40% of core job skills will shift in the next five years. That’s not a data point to sprinkle into a PowerPoint. It’s a mandate.

Independent schools that thrive in this environment won’t be the ones with the flashiest tech rollout or the slickest AI strategy document. They’ll be the ones where leadership shows up as posture – modeled daily by heads, division directors, department chairs, and faculty alike. That’s where the CREATE framework becomes more than theory; it’s a roadmap.

  • Culture: Leaders must articulate why their school matters in an AI world. A head who tells parents, “We’re not afraid of AI because our mission is to grow thinkers, not just test-takers,” anchors the community in clarity. Culture becomes the compass, not the casualty, of disruption.

  • Relationships: Trust is the currency of change. A division director who runs faculty roundtables on AI, listening before mandating, builds the connective tissue that makes innovation possible. Students who see adults modeling trust are more likely to approach AI with integrity themselves.

  • Execution: Posture means acting, not waiting. A department chair piloting AI-infused writing assignments with a small group of juniors is modeling leadership by doing. It doesn’t require perfection – it requires motion. Schools that can iterate quickly will learn faster than those waiting for the “perfect” policy.

  • Agility: When a math teacher experiments with AI to generate multiple solution pathways, she signals to her students: flexibility is a strength, not a weakness. Leaders who normalize experimentation cultivate resilience at every level of the institution.

  • Teaching: AI makes it clear that the role of teachers is no longer to deliver content but to coach discernment. A history teacher who uses AI to generate competing historical narratives, then coaches students on how to evaluate them, models what it means to teach in the age of abundance.

  • Ethical Decision-Making: Finally, ethics is where leadership posture is tested most visibly. A board chair who insists AI policies be rooted in the school’s values – not just risk management models responsibility. A teacher who pauses a class discussion to ask, “Should we use AI here?” is coaching ethics in real time.

Students are watching. If their leaders freeze, they’ll learn to freeze. If their leaders adapt, they’ll learn to adapt. Leadership in the AI era isn’t about titles or tech plans – it’s about posture. And posture is contagious.

A Leadership Opportunity, Not a Threat

That conversation with the neuroscientist entrepreneur has stayed with me. He sees AI as both risk and opportunity: a technology that could diminish student agency if we’re careless, or massively expand it if we’re thoughtful. He believes schools must find ways to make AI serve their mission, not the other way around.

He’s right.

COVID was our last once-in-a-generation disruption, and too many schools responded by doubling down on old practices. AI offers another chance. This time, we can’t afford to miss it.

Independent schools have the agility, intimacy, and cultural depth to lead. But doing so will require courage: the courage to listen, to experiment, to admit uncertainty, and to model adaptability in real time.

Generative AI didn’t end education. It reset the terms. For independent schools, this is not the time to guard what was. It is the time to guide what’s next.

Question for heads, boards, and faculty leaders: How are you ensuring AI expands agency in your community rather than diminishes it?
