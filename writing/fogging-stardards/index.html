Fogging Standards

Arteta Removed His Two Best Players. That Was the Rebuild.

Matt Goode7 June 20267 min read Get new pieces by email

# CREATE# The culture is drifting

A non-negotiable is only worth what you're willing to lose to keep it. Mikel Arteta lost his highest earner and his own captain to prove Arsenal's.

A captain booed off his own pitch by his own fans. Two eighth-place finishes. A dressing room full of expensive, comfortable men who had stopped believing in the thing they were paid to do. That was Arsenal in December 2019, the week Mikel Arteta took the job.

The talent hadn't gone anywhere, ok ok ok, yeah it kinda did. But so did the standards. So the first thing he did wasn't tactical. Before they practiced a single corner, he named three non-negotiables – respect, commitment, passion – and repeated them from his first press conference onward. The word did real work. A negotiable is something you trade when the price is right. He was telling a room full of millionaires that on these three, there was no price. The question every one of them was quietly asking is the only one that matters when a leader announces a standard: what happens to the person too valuable to hold to it?

Anyone who has watched All or Nothing, Amazon's documentary of the rebuild, knows he meant it at volume. The team talks run close to violent. "I don't want one fucking player complaining, one fucking player walking," he tells the changing room at half-time. It plays as theatre. It wasn't. The proof came in who he was willing to lose.

And the non-negotiables were never only about attitude. They became a system. The model he brought to Arsenal asked the whole team to defend as one – a press that starts at the very front, every forward hunting the ball, every player tracking back the instant it's lost. There is no place in that system for a passenger, however gifted. And the two most gifted attackers at the club were exactly that. Neither Özil nor Aubameyang pressed with conviction. Neither of them tracked back. Özil practically never ran at all in fact, but his passes could break defensives, and make grown men cry. He even “invented” his own shot. Such was the prerogative of genius so it was said. In the era they came up in, the gifted attacker was excused from defending – that was for other, lesser players to do. Arteta's non-negotiable was that no one is excused.

Start with the man you could least afford to bench. Mesut Özil was the highest-paid player at the club, on a reported £350,000 a week, a fan icon, the closest thing Arsenal had to a marquee name. In the spring of 2020, when the squad was asked to take a pay cut to protect jobs through the pandemic, most of them agreed. Özil wanted assurances first, he wanted his money, nevermind the cooks, trainers, or the crew that cuts the grass. 

Arteta froze him out. He didn't play a competitive minute after March 2020, was left out of the Premier League and Europa League squads entirely, and by January 2021 his contract was torn up and he was gone to Fenerbahçe. The official line was "football reasons." It was also the literal one: there was no room in a pressing side for a player who would not press. That is what a standard with teeth looks like from the wrong side of it.

Then Arteta did the harder thing. He did it to his own man.

Pierre-Emerick Aubameyang was the mercurial footed captain Arteta inherited and chose to keep. More than that, he was Arteta's bet: the manager backed a new three-year contract in September 2020 that made him one of the highest earners at the club, and said openly that he wanted to build the team around him.

What ended it was lateness. In March 2021, Arteta left his own captain out of the squad for a North London derby – a derby – for arriving late to a team meeting on the morning of the game. Arsenal won 2-1 without him. Aubameyang kept the armband that time. Nine months later, after he returned late from a trip, Arteta took the armband for good and dropped him from the squad. He never played for Arteta again. Weeks later he left for Barcelona, his contract terminated, the sunk cost eaten in full.

The size of that is easy to miss. A manager benched his best forward for the biggest fixture of the season, then took the armband off him for good, over minutes on a clock. Lateness to a meeting doesn't look like a thing worth losing a player over – which is exactly why it's the truest test of a standard. It was never about the minutes. Turning up late says the quiet part out loud: my time is worth more than yours, the rule that binds the room binds me a little less, what we agreed was closer to a suggestion. Arteta treated a late arrival as a fact about the club, not a fact about the clock.

And the breach is cheapest to forgive in exactly the people you can least afford to lose. The replaceable player who rolls in late gets corrected without a thought. Your best one gets a reason it's fine – he's brilliant, he was probably working, the meeting could wait. The team clocks which you do, every time. So picture the last time someone wandered into your 9:00 five minutes late with a giant Starbucks latte in hand and you said nothing. That's the same behavior that cost a captain his armband. The only difference is whether you treat it as load-bearing or as a courtesy you keep quietly extending.

The easy version of "high standards" is a lie. It punishes the people you were glad to be rid of anyway – the marginal squad player, the attitude you'd already written off – and it costs you nothing. Every leader alive will tell you they hold a high bar while quietly only ever enforcing it downward. A real standard runs the other way. It's worth exactly what it costs you to apply it to the person you can least afford to lose. Arteta's became real the moment it cost him his most gifted player, then his own captain, then a season, then a pile of money paid to make both men disappear. The bill is the proof. If your standard has never cost you a star, you don't have a standard. You have a preference you enforce on the weak.

True story, as his swim coach, I once left my own son behind when he was late for departure to an away meet. Gasps ensued. No one was ever late again, and it became part of team lore handed down for years. Yo, coach left his own son behind: don't be late.

The most common exemption inside any company is talent. Picture the engineer who closes the hardest tickets and has left a junior shaken after code review for two years running – because the one time someone raised it, the answer was a shrug and "that's just how he is, and have you seen his output?" Everyone on the team can name him. The speed with which they can name him is the culture. The values page says one thing; the team runs on the version they have watched you permit. And he stays exempt for the same reason Arteta refused to accept: he's too good to lose.

His wager was that the standard was a more valuable asset than the player – that a dressing room watching their manager hold the line against the biggest name in the room would become something no individual talent could buy. It took years, and it was not clean. Three straight seasons ended in second place, close enough to feel like failure.

It's tempting to read this backward, now that there's a trophy at the end, as though the standard was always going to pay off. It wasn't. For three years this looked like a manager who had traded away match-winners for a principle and finished second with it, and for stretches it looked like it would cost him his job. The bet could have failed. Had it, these same decisions would read today as stubbornness. That is the actual nature of a standard – you hold the line before you know whether it works, because the moment you only enforce it when the payoff is guaranteed, it stops being a standard and goes back to being a preference.

Then, this spring, it paid. Arsenal won the Premier League – their first title in 22 years, built on the best defence in the division: the fewest goals conceded, a clean sheet in half their matches. The trophy was won by eleven players doing the exact work Özil and Aubameyang would not. The talent that won it came later. The standard that made the talent worth buying came first.

Culture is not the values you frame on the wall. It's the worst behavior you're willing to walk past – and the real test of it is never the person you were glad to lose. It's the one you weren't.
