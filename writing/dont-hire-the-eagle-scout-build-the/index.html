Don’t Hire the Eagle Scout. Build the Patrol.

The badge isn’t what makes Scouts lead. The system underneath it is.

Matt Goode13 October 20265 min read Get new pieces by email

# My role is changing# DELEGATE

A couple of summers ago at Philmont, my son was our crew’s navigator for the day. It was first thing in the morning, everyone keen to get moving, and he blew past a couple of obvious markers and missed a trail turn. I had a choice: Scouter uniform or dad hat. I kept the uniform on and said nothing.

Two miles later, the crew hit something they couldn’t ignore: a dirt road. They weren’t technically lost. They weren’t where they were supposed to be. Then the looks came, all pointed at me. Why didn’t you say something?

The detour cost us four extra miles, most of it in ankle-deep mud. My son took the ribbing. When we debriefed as a crew, we found out someone had spotted the trailhead and kept quiet, and that morning eagerness had outrun everyone’s judgment. For the rest of the trek, they checked every landmark. We didn’t get lost again.

My son forgave me. Kinda.

Earlier this month, Scouting America field director Blake Nettleton argued that employers should pay attention to Eagle Scouts. He cites a 2026 Harris Poll study of 3,178 U.S. adults. Seventy-four percent of Eagle Scouts have held a leadership position at work. Among adults who never joined Scouting, that number is 31 percent.

I believe those numbers. I just don’t think they say what we want them to say.

The badge is the wrong signal

Blake’s piece compares Eagle Scouts with people who never joined. But the study had a third group, Scouts who never made Eagle, and that’s where it gets interesting. If you broaden the question to leadership roles at work, in the community, or in the military, 80 percent of Eagle Scouts say yes. So do 71 percent of Scouts who stopped short. Among people who never joined, it’s 38 percent.

I’m one of the 71 percent. I never earned Eagle. At the age I should have been finishing it, I chased swimming cuts to become a more competitive Division I prospect. I swam in college, met my wife, and made friends for life. Zero regrets.

With one giant asterisk. I never finished the paperwork for my Eagle because, at the time, I didn’t value it. Once you turn 18, that door closes for good.

But the badge is the only thing I left behind. What I learned as a Scout, and as a leader of other Scouts, came with me anyway.

There’s a catch. Scouting America paid for the survey, and it’s a snapshot, not a trial. Families who sign up may differ from families who don’t.

If the badge adds so little on top of the program, it’s the wrong thing to hire for. What’s worth stealing is the machine underneath it.

What a patrol actually is

Strip out the uniforms, and a troop is a four-part leadership system.

A small team with a real job. Six to eight Scouts who have to eat, pitch tents, and get the gear home. Failure is visible by dinner.

Authority sits with the learner. The patrol leader is a teenager, chosen by peers who don’t have to follow. Nobody is paid to comply.

Adults aren’t allowed to rescue. When I was a Scout, adults mostly stayed in the background. I can remember them stepping in exactly twice.

The first time, a younger Scout picked up a copperhead. Yes, picked it up. The second was a winter campout on the Appalachian Trail, when one of us slid into hypothermia. Both times, the adults moved fast, and both ended in some kind of evacuation.

Everything else was ours. The plans, the meals, the routes, the arguments about all three. We made plenty of mistakes, and nobody stepped in to stop them, because none of them could really hurt us. That wasn’t neglect. It was room, and someone had decided exactly how much of it we could safely have. I didn’t see the design then. I see it now. Short of a copperhead or hypothermia, my job as Scoutmaster is to advise and keep my hands in my pockets, even when I could fix the plan in thirty seconds.

Reps on a cadence. A weekly meeting, a monthly outing, for years. A Tufts study of Philadelphia Cub Scouts found that those who showed up regularly scored higher on character measures than those who didn’t. The dose matters.

None of that is a curriculum. It’s structure, and it runs whether anyone feels inspired that week or not.

What most companies do instead

Now look at how most organizations make a first-time manager. Promote the best individual contributor, send them to a two-day course, and hand them a team and a boss who steps in the moment anything wobbles.

Then watch what happens when something goes sideways. At Philmont, someone on the crew saw the trailhead and kept quiet, because the map belonged to the navigator. Teams do the same thing every week. The person who spots the problem says nothing because it isn’t their call, and the boss fixes it before anyone has to learn from it.

That’s training. It’s an event. The patrol is infrastructure.

Scouting keeps adults from leading. Most companies quietly pull them back in, then wonder why their new managers stall.

Build the patrol

You don’t need a merit badge program. You need four honest answers about every person you’re developing.

  1. Do they own an outcome that fails visibly if they drop it?

  2. Do they hold real authority over the work, even if it’s borrowed?

  3. Has their manager agreed, out loud, not to rescue them?

  4. Will they get another rep within a month?

A no on any of these means you aren’t developing a leader. You’re hosting one.

The leaders you already employ

Blake’s best point is his last one. The den leaders, Scoutmasters, and committee chairs on your payroll run this system on their weekends. Every month, they practice advising instead of doing, the exact skill most senior people never build.

You may not need to hire the Eagle Scout. You may already employ the adult who helped make one. Ask them how they do it, then ask why your org makes it so hard.

Stop recruiting for the badge. Build the patrol. Then let them miss the turn.
