Return to Office Is a Confession

Matt Goode23 June 20264 min read Get new pieces by email

# The culture is drifting

I’m writing this on the Acela. A few World Cup tourists drifting toward the café car, but mostly business travelers – laptops open, spreadsheets and decks, somewhere over Connecticut. Half this car is running a company from a tray table, calmly, between coffees. Nobody is being watched. Everyone is working.

Some of them, back home, require their own employees to be in an office five days a week.

Here’s a question I put to the owners among them: what would happen to your company if you went on vacation for two weeks and turned your phone off? Not silenced. Off. Most people go quiet when they picture it. A few admit the place would be on fire by day four.

Now flip it. What would happen to your company if your employees didn’t see an office for two weeks?

Most owners can’t come up with much. They reach for culture, or collaboration, or the new hire who needs supervision. But press for the concrete disaster – the thing that actually breaks – and it rarely arrives.

Sit with which of those two answers should worry you more?

We spend enormous energy on the second question, whether people can be trusted out of sight, and almost none on the first. Which is strange, because the first is where the real fragility lives. The danger in most companies isn’t the unwatched employee. It’s the one person who can’t be unwatched without the whole thing wobbling – and that person usually has a corner office and signs the mandate.

Adam Grant and his Wharton colleagues published an op-ed this Monday (6/22) in the New York Times (click here), which argued that the trait most reliably predicting opposition to remote work isn’t a concern for productivity. Once you control for trust and sociability, it’s narcissism. The appetite for an audience, imagine that?! Grant has a whole argument and the data to carry it: six years of surveys across thousands of managers and the Fortune 500 chief executives. His research used proxies for narcissism – the size of the pay package, the signature, the photo in the annual report. The bigger those ran, the more hostile the leader was to hybrid work. They even pinned the causation: prompt an executive to admire the role a bold ego played for Steve Jobs or Larry Ellison, and their taste for return-to-office mandates climbed. All probably true. But the sharper tell isn’t on the couch, it’s structural: the people who write these mandates are, more often than not, exactly the ones who’ve quietly exempted themselves from them.

None of which means presence never matters. Some work plainly needs the room, and a few jobs are nothing but the room. I taught for twenty years, and teaching over a screen was miserable – not inconvenient, miserable – because teaching runs on the attention moving between people in a space: the small corrections you make off a face that’s drifting, the question you only think to ask because you saw someone hesitate. None of that survives a grid of muted squares. The apprentice who learns the job by overhearing it, absorbing what nobody thought to write down, loses the same way. Pretending that work transfers cleanly through a screen costs you something real.

But notice what it has in common: it’s episodic and intense, not nine-to-five. Mentoring, the hard conversation you need a face for, the messy week when a team actually forms – these are moments, and they reward being deliberate about presence, gathering on purpose. A blanket five-day mandate does the reverse. It makes presence routine and ambient, and therefore cheap, and a thing that’s always happening stops being a thing anyone shows up for. The owner’s mistake isn’t believing presence can matter. It’s losing the ability to tell when it does – and reaching, instead, for the version that mostly soothes him.

In CLUTCH, I keep coming back to the difference between a title and a license. A title is the role and the accountability that rides with it. A license is the authority to act without asking. Both two-week questions are the same diagnostic pointed in opposite directions – they measure whether the license was ever granted. Give it, and the business runs while you’re on a beach and your people are at their kitchen tables. Withhold it, and the company is just you, wearing an org chart, and the mandate is how you keep everyone close enough to disguise that.

Which leaves the owner somewhere awkward. The thing he won’t tolerate in his employees – being trusted out of sight – is the exact thing he can’t do himself. He’s the one who can’t take the phone off. The only difference is that no one writes a policy for him. He’s the unwatched node, and he’s the one that would actually break.

So before the next mandate, run the test on yourself first. Two weeks, phone off. If the answer frightens you, the problem was never that your people might vanish from a building. It’s that you built a company that can’t survive anyone vanishing – and the first person it can’t survive is you.

A few questions worth sitting with:

  • What would keep working if you were unreachable for two weeks?

  • Who on your team is ready for more license than they currently hold?

  • What does presence give you that a system hasn’t yet?
