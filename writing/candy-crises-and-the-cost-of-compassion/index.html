Candy, Crises, and the Cost of Compassion

Matt Goode30 October 20254 min read Get new pieces by email

# EQ

Americans will spend $3.9 billion on Halloween candy this week.

That’s nearly half of what the U.S. government spent in an entire year – through USAID’s Bureau for Humanitarian Assistance – to deliver food, water, shelter, and medical care to people who’d lost everything to war, famine, and disaster.

Think about that ratio for a moment. One night of candy. Six months of global lifesaving work.

This morning, I read a piece on NPR that quoted my former boss, Sarah Charles, discussing what U.S. assistance to Jamaica might have looked like after Hurricane Melissa – a Category 5 storm that devastated the Caribbean.

Click here, for that story...

She described what BHA used to do before it was dismantled: pre-positioning relief supplies in Miami and Haiti weeks before hurricane season. Coordinating with local governments so aid could flow as soon as airports reopened. Embedding water and sanitation specialists ahead of landfall to prevent cholera outbreaks in the chaos that follows.

This wasn’t “charity theater.” It was precision disaster response, built on decades of hard lessons and dozens of other crises. Teams who knew that the difference between 100 deaths and 10,000 often came down to having the right supplies in the right place 48 hours before anyone else thought to move.

That machinery operated on roughly $8.4 billion annually during the Biden Administration – less than 1% of the federal budget.

It’s largely gone now. The expertise is scattered. The systems were dismantled. The institutional knowledge – the kind you can’t rebuild with an executive order – is gone, forever.

Consider this:

Have you ever lost everything to a wall of water? Most of us haven’t. Most of us never will.

But last year, 108 million people were displaced by conflict or natural disasters – not over decades, but in 2024. That’s more than the combined populations of California, Texas, and Florida, suddenly homeless.

When walls of water swallowed coastlines after the Indian Ocean tsunami, or when monsoon floods engulfed Bangladesh, USAID helped in the immediate aftermath, delivering food and clean water. But the agency stuck around, engineers helped restore clean water systems, rebuilt bridges and shelters, and supported farmers with flood-resistant seeds and livelihood grants.

The goal wasn’t charity. It was stability. Rebuilding dignity one well, one pump, one field at a time.

Forty-five million children suffer from acute malnutrition – the kind where their bodies start consuming themselves, where a simple respiratory infection becomes a death sentence because there’s nothing left to fight with.

That’s not hyperbole; it’s the latest estimate from the UNICEF/WHO/World Bank Joint Malnutrition Report (2024), based on 2022 data – the most recent global count.

In 2023–2024, those spoonfuls became measurable lives saved. In Yemen, USAID’s Bureau for Humanitarian Assistance provided over $40 million in funding and supplies – including ready-to-use therapeutic food and fortified milk – to treat more than 140,000 children suffering from severe acute malnutrition, saving an estimated 17,000 lives. In South Sudan, where floodwaters and conflict cut off entire regions, USAID’s partners reached over 2 million children, admitting 338,000 for lifesaving treatment – an estimated 42,000 of them survived because that help arrived in time. And in Somalia,

BHA-supported clinics continued to deliver high-energy food pastes and train mothers to recognize the earliest signs of wasting – likely saving another 10,000 to 15,000 children.

It wasn’t glamorous, and it rarely made headlines.

But line by line, crisis by crisis, USAID turned American values into survival. It saved lives, just not necessarily American lives, except when it did:

For nearly two decades, America’s top defense leaders have said the same quiet truth: diplomacy and development are national security. Former Trump Secretary of Defense Jim Mattis warned Congress, “If you don’t fund the State Department and USAID fully, then I need to buy more ammunition.” Robert Gates, SecDef under *BOTH* George W. Bush and Barack Obama, put it more bluntly: “Development is a lot cheaper than sending soldiers.”

Each of them understood what USAID has always represented – a frontline defense against instability, extremism, and the kind of desperation that draws troops into conflict zones. But what’s often missed is that USAID doesn’t just save lives over there – it saves lives here.

USAID’s Bureau for Humanitarian Assistance was built to fight these crises at scale.

When earthquakes flattened cities, BHA deployed search-and-rescue teams and emergency medical units within hours. When droughts pushed families to the brink of starvation, they delivered high-nutrition food that could save a child’s life in weeks. When disease outbreaks threatened to spiral into epidemics, they funded rapid-response teams to contain the spread before CNN arrived.

They helped families find shelter when everything they owned was buried under rubble. They saved babies from preventable deaths – not with experimental treatments, but with oral rehydration salts that cost pennies. They delivered clean water to refugee camps, stopping cholera before it could kill thousands. They vaccinated children against measles in war zones.

The work wasn’t glamorous. It rarely made headlines. But it was the difference between a crisis and a catastrophe.

And it costs each American about $117 per year, roughly what many of us spend on Netflix, Spotify, and a couple of lattes each month.

As we hand out candy tonight, I’m holding both truths:

  • We can mobilize $3.9 billion overnight for sugar and costumes. We once mobilized $8.4 billion annually to keep the most vulnerable people on Earth alive.

  • We can move mountains when we want to. We can build supply chains that deliver candy corn to every zip code in America. We can coordinate logistics that would make military planners weep with envy – all for a holiday that exists because we decided it should.

Both reveal what we value. Both show what we’re capable of when we decide something matters.

One is sweet. The other is sacred.

Only one reveals our character.

The question now isn’t whether we can afford compassion. We’re spending half the annual humanitarian budget on a single night of candy. The money exists. The logistics capability exists. The expertise existed until we decided to dismantle it.

The question is: which story do we want the world to tell about us?

Both futures are possible.

We’re choosing one right now.
