They Didn’t Send the Machines to Business School

Matt Goode30 June 202610 min read Get new pieces by email

# AI

The essay series was supposed to be AI against the humanities. But the AI labs quietly put them on the same side of the table. The credential that loses is the one in the middle.

Here is a fact that should reorganize how you think about what a mind is worth.

The companies building the most advanced artificial intelligence on earth have been quietly paying philosophers to teach their models how to reason. Not to write the marketing copy. To think. Daniel Greco, a professor of philosophy at Yale, described the work in the Chronicle of Higher Education this month: he sits with a frontier model, hands it questions hard enough to find the edge of its reasoning, and grades what comes back, so the next version can be trained on the difference. He is one of many. Anthropic, Google DeepMind, and OpenAI have all brought philosophers inside, some on staff, some on the side. OpenAI’s Sam Altman has said the company consulted hundreds of moral philosophers while deciding how its model should behave.

One AI adviser told Wired it may be the best time to be a philosopher since Aristotle tutored Alexander. He was half joking. He was not wrong about the demand.

The tell is in who they didn’t call.

These are the most sophisticated buyers of cognition who have ever existed. Near-unlimited capital, one defining problem: build something that can think. When they reached the edge of what the machine could do on its own, they did not phone the business school. They went to the philosophy department.

That should tell you which capacity is actually scarce.

Watch what the machine was already good at before the philosophers arrived. It could summarize anything. It could optimize inside a defined problem, run the analysis, produce fluent output at volume, and pick the efficient path among the options on the table. That is not a small thing. It is also, almost exactly, the skills the MBA spends two years and six figures learning. Optimize the given frame. Choose well among the options. Execute.

The machine does that now. Faster, cheaper, no signing bonus.

What the machine could not do, the thing the AI labs are paying Greco and people like him to build into it, is reason its way to an answer that was never in the material. His questions are built to find the limit, hard enough that the model cannot retrieve a stored answer and has to generate one. That is the whole line. The difference between looking something up and thinking. Between the answer in the back of the book and the question no one has asked yet.

I have spent this series arguing that this line is the entire game.

That the capacities good leadership runs on are not skills you install, but a formation you build into how a person sees. I made the case through Plato on who gets promoted, Sophocles on the truth a court cannot hear, Homer on the day a title and the judgment under it stopped being the same thing. Then, through two philosophers fighting in public over how to save the humanities, Jennifer Frey and N. Ángel Pinillos, in the same Chronicle that just ran Greco.

The whole way through, I wrote it as a rivalry. The humanities against the machine. The generator against the optimizer. Human judgment as the thing AI was coming for, and the thing we had to defend.

I had the contest wrong. Watch what AI the labs actually did and the sides redraw. They did not set the machine against the humanities. They sat them down together. Greco does not compete with the model. He sits beside it and teaches it to think. The most advanced technology we have ever built hit its ceiling, and the breakthrough came from fusing it with the oldest training in judgment we have. The machine and the philosopher are on the same side of the table.

The rivalry was never AI against the humanities. OK, maybe it was a little. But, in another frame, it was the two of them against the thing in the middle. And the thing in the middle is the move the MBA sells.

I was building an argument. The AI labs were running the experiment. It came back with more than my answer. It came back with the alliance; most of us have been too busy defending one side to see.

The Wall Street Journal reported this month that demand for the MBA is drifting down along with the pay. A survey of more than 600 corporate recruiters by the Graduate Management Admission Council puts the projected median starting salary at $120,000, down from $125,000 a year ago, with fewer openings and a harder landing even for graduates of the top programs.

A slipping salary is not the story. The reason behind it is.

Part of it is cyclical and real: the post-pandemic hiring frenzy cooled, consulting pulled back, and the targets came down off record highs. McKinsey cut its intake from Chicago’s Booth School from 71 in one year to 33 the next; Amazon, Google, and Microsoft trimmed too. But the structural reason is the one that should hold your attention. MBA graduates funnel into a narrow band of destinations: finance, consulting, technology, and professional services. Gad Levanon, chief economist at the Burning Glass Institute, named the trap precisely: those are the sectors shedding jobs fastest right now, and the entry roles inside them go first. As he put it, “the bottom rungs young grads depend on are being pulled away.”

Sit with what is pulling them. The junior MBA job was, in plain terms, a ticket to entry-level optimization. Build the model, run the analysis, format the deck, and work on the defined problem under a partner. Repeat every year with new associates. That is the work the machine now does at volume. Andrew Yang saw a world where AI came for truck drivers; he may be proven correct, too. But AI is currently eating the lunch of many white-collar professions. The rung is not just contracting in a soft economy. It is being automated out from underneath, in the same sectors, by the same force. The degree was a credential into the exact tier of cognition the machine just absorbed.

And the market is already pricing the alternative. A February 2026 report from the Federal Reserve Bank of New York found that philosophy majors now have lower unemployment than computer science majors. Read it twice. The degree your uncle called useless is outperforming the one everyone stampeded to get.

The MBA sold a single promise: come here, and we will make you the person who optimizes well and judges well, the complete operating mind that a company pays a premium for. It only ever delivered the first half. It taught the optimization and assumed the judgment, which is the same assumption the machine makes when no one has built the judgment in. Now the machine does the optimization for free. The judgment the MBA never taught is being built by hand on both sides at once, into the AI model and into the student, the humanities form. That is the alliance, seen from the side that loses to it. AI took the floor the MBA stood on. The humanities hold the ceiling it could never reach.

I am not arguing that MBAs lack intelligence, or that the degree teaches nothing. Some of the sharpest operators I work with came up through it. I am arguing that it monetized half of cognition the machine just turned into a commodity, and never had the half that was the humanities’ to give.

The fair objection is that some of this is theater.

Edward Harcourt, a philosopher at Oxford who directs its Institute for Ethics in AI, has warned about ethics-washing: hire a few philosophers, and the public reads your product as wise and safe, whether or not anything changed. There is a real version of that worry. A philosopher on the payroll can be a credential a company wears rather than a conscience it consults.

Grant all of it. The point survives. You do not perform respectability with a degree nobody respects. If hiring philosophers signals seriousness to the market, that is the market telling you what it now takes seriously. And the work Greco describes is not a press release. It is hours of paid expert labor, writing questions and grading reasoning, because the model got measurably better when a trained mind sat across from it. Marketing does not move a benchmark. Judgment does.

Make it concrete. A self-driving car is bearing down on a choice no driver ever wants to make: protect the passenger, the old woman in the crosswalk, or the kid chasing a ball into the street. It cannot save all three, and someone has to decide whose life the software values before the car is ever built. Would you pay extra for the model that picks you? Plenty of people would. That is not a marketing question, and it is not one the optimizer can answer. There is no efficient path through it, no answer in the material to retrieve. It has to be reasoned by someone trained to reason about exactly that. Classical streetcar dilemma for the 21st century.

The AI labs did, at the level of the machine, exactly what most organizations refuse to do at the level of their people. They found the scarce input, named it plainly, and paired the tool with it. No workshop. No tool bought on hope. They put the optimizer and the judgment on the same team.

Your company has the same problem one layer down, and most leaders miss why. AI does not supply judgment. It multiplies whatever judgment is already in the building, upward if it is there, into confident error at volume if it is not.

That is the whole reason I named the book CLUTCH. An engine with no clutch can only do one thing: send all its power straight to the wheels, all the time, whether or not the road can take it. A car becomes drivable the moment you add the part that decides how much force reaches the ground, and when. Judgment is that part. In the book, that clutch is emotional intelligence, the capacity to read the moment and meter the power to it. The machine has no clutch. It assumes the judgment and scales whatever it finds, full power, every time.

Whether the labs actually built that clutch into the model is a separate question, and I am not convinced they have. The reasoning got sharper and the benchmark moved. But ethics, and judgment in the situation no one has seen before, the things I have spent this series arguing the humanities form, may not be the kind of thing you can install in a system trained only on what already exists. The labs are paying a fortune to find out, which tells you how rare the thing is.

Either way, your move is the same, and it runs through people, not licenses. Formation does not transfer by the seat. What you can do is build the infrastructure that forms it and finds it: systems that reward the person who questions the frame instead of screening them out, that move real weight onto someone in stages and watch what holds. Then put your tools in those hands.

That is the work. Not better tools. A clutch for the judgment you already have, and the judgment you still need to grow.

Step back far enough, and the whole arc collapses into one strange sentence.

We built schools on the logic of the factory and trained generations to reproduce the right answer. We built business schools on top of that and trained the survivors to optimize the given frame. Then we built machines that ran that optimization better than any of us, and to give them the judgment the business school skips, we sat them down with philosophers.

The machine is going to grad school for the formation we spent a century training out of the humans.

The labs already made the correction. They paired the most powerful tool ever built with the oldest discipline of judgment we have, and let the credential in the middle fall away.

Yours is sitting in your building right now, in the people your system was built to overlook. The labs found theirs and put it to work beside the machine.

Go find yours.

Three questions to sit with this week:

  1. Where in your company are you still paying a premium for the optimization the machine now does for free?

  2. If you had to find the one person who can reason to an answer that isn’t in the playbook, would your hiring and promotion systems surface them, or screen them out?

  3. What changes if you treat judgment as something you grow under load, the way the labs treat it as something worth pairing with every tool they build?

CLUTCH is the operating system I built for this: the infrastructure that forms judgment the way judgment is really formed, under weight, over time, with the handoff made on purpose. The labs just spent a fortune proving where it comes from. Plato has been making the same point, unpaid, for 2,400 years.
