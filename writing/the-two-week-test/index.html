The Two Week Test

Matt Goode10 June 20263 min read Get new pieces by email

# Everything runs through me

Every framework in this book grew out of one question. Not a question about strategy, or vision, or the org chart. Something smaller, and far harder to sit with:

Could you leave for two weeks – phone off, laptop shut, unreachable – and trust the work to keep moving without you?

I’ve asked it for years, in boardrooms and on factory floors and across enough dinner tables to know the tell. The words rarely come first; the body answers before the mouth does – a flicker, a held breath, the quiet math of what falls over by Thursday. That flinch is the most honest thing a leader ever shows me. The day I understood what it was telling me is the day this book started.

The sentence that gave it away

A few months back, I sat across from the CEO of an engineering firm I admire. Sharp, well-liked, the kind of founder people follow into a fire. He told me, almost sheepishly, that he was finally taking his family to Italy. Two weeks. First real break in years.

“That’s overdue,” I said.

He nodded. Then, after a beat: “I’ll still be working most of it, though.” His laptop was already packed.

That sentence is the whole problem, and he didn’t hear himself say it. He wasn’t packing the laptop because he distrusts his team. He was packing it because the company had been built – quietly, over the years – to run through him. Italy wasn’t going to change that. It was just going to move his desk to a hotel balcony.

The test underneath the sigh

So picture your own two weeks. Phone off. Laptop shut. Now run the tape to day fourteen. When you walk back in, did the work move, or did it wait for you?

Most leaders can’t answer cleanly, and the flinch is the diagnosis. Noam Wasserman, who tracked nearly ten thousand founders at Harvard, named the trap. He called it the founder’s dilemma: the instincts that build a company – ownership, decisiveness, high standards – are the same ones that cap it. You move fast, so you do it yourself. The system grows around your judgment. One day, you look up, and you’re the tollbooth on your own highway, every car idling for you.

You can’t out-work this. No run of late nights fixes a problem baked into the design. Effort is finite; a well-built system scales. Until the design changes, the bottleneck stays – and so do you, laptop in the carry-on.

Why it’s worth fixing, in plain numbers

Here’s the part founders feel in their gut but rarely price out. The business that can’t run without you is worth less the day you try to sell it, because a buyer isn’t purchasing a company – they’re purchasing your calendar, and your calendar isn’t for sale. Acquirers discount owner-dependent firms hard, sometimes by a third or more, for exactly this reason. The same trap that ruins your vacation also caps your exit.

And the cost isn’t only financial. It’s the slow erosion of why you started. The engineer who headed to Italy didn’t build a company so he could answer Slack from a balcony while his kids swam without him. He built it for freedom, and somewhere along the way, the thing he built started owning him back. The always-on, last-to-leave story wears like a badge, right up until the day a body or a marriage sends the invoice the business never did.

The leaders who can disappear for two weeks are no less committed. They built something that holds. That’s not a personality difference; it’s a design difference, and design can be changed. That’s the whole bet of this book: the two-week test isn’t a verdict on you – it’s a readout on what you’ve built. And what’s built can be rebuilt.

So this is where we start, because it’s where I started: not with a framework, but with one uncomfortable question and the honest flinch that follows it. Everything after this is the answer.

Take these into your next quiet hour:

  • If you disappeared tomorrow for two weeks, what moves without you, and what stops?

  • For the things that stop, what about the design routes them through you?

  • What would taking that trip for real be worth to you?
