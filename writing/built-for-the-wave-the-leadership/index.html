Built for the Wave: The Leadership Architecture That Turns AI Into Advantage

Part 2 of 2

Matt Goode3 February 20268 min read Get new pieces by email

# AI

Yesterday, I wrote about the transformation gap – the chasm between what leaders are demanding and what their organizations can deliver. ([Read Part 1: The Wave Won’t Wait])

The numbers are stark. Fifty-six percent of organizations have poured resources into AI and have nothing to show for it. Seventy percent of the workforce is disengaged. And the leaders pushing hardest for transformation are trapped, spending half their time on short-term fires.

But buried in that data is a different story. Twelve percent of companies are seeing real results – both revenue gains and cost savings from AI. They’re not the ones with the biggest budgets.

The vanguard isn’t winning because they’re larger. They’re winning because they built better.

The Great Leveler

AI changes the math on scale.

For decades, enterprise size meant enterprise advantage. More resources. More reach. More capacity to absorb the cost of transformation. Small and mid-sized companies competed at the margins, moving faster in narrow lanes while the giants owned the broad ones.

AI inverts that.

The technology doesn’t care about your headcount. It cares about your architecture. A 50-person technical company with the right systems will adopt AI faster – and extract more value from it – than a 5,000-person enterprise that still routes every decision through 12 stakeholders.

The 56% seeing no results from AI? That includes Fortune 500 companies with eight-figure transformation budgets and armies of consultants. Size didn’t save them. Their own systems – built for a different era – are what’s holding them back.

This is the window. But windows close.

The question isn’t whether you can afford to build the right architecture. It’s whether you can afford not to – while your competitors figure it out first.

What the Vanguard Built

PwC identified what separates the 12% from the rest. It’s not better technology. It’s not more funding. It’s four things:

  • Technology environments that enable AI integration

  • Clearly defined roadmaps for AI initiatives

  • Formalized Responsible AI and risk processes

  • Organizational cultures that enable adoption

The first three are operational. Important, but straightforward. You can hire for them. You can buy them.

The fourth one – culture that enables adoption – is where most organizations break.

Because “culture” isn’t the same as atmosphere. It’s not off-sites or value posters. Culture is the accumulation of what gets rewarded and what gets punished. It’s the answer to the question every employee learns in their first ninety days: What’s actually safe to do here?

When 75% of CEOs say their companies don’t tolerate high-risk innovation projects, they’re describing a culture that has taught people that the safe play is the smart play. Don’t experiment visibly. Don’t fail publicly. Don’t be the one holding the bag when the pilot doesn’t scale.

That’s not a motivation problem. It’s a design problem.

And design problems have design solutions.

The Architecture

I work with founders and leadership teams on what I call leadership architecture – the underlying systems that determine whether transformation is possible or just aspirational.

It has three components. Think of them as the engine, gearbox, and navigation. Miss any one, and you’re not going anywhere.

1. The Engine: CREATE

The symptom you’ll recognize:

Your AI pilot worked beautifully. Small team, clear use case, real results. Then came the rollout – and adoption stalled at 15%. The tool exists. People aren’t using it.

Or: Your top talent keeps leaving. Exit interviews say “culture” and “growth opportunities,” but you can’t pin down what’s actually broken.

Or: Meetings end in agreement. Then nothing changes. Initiatives launch with fanfare and die in silence.

These aren’t random failures. They’re engine misfires.

The framework:

CREATE represents six cylinders that must fire together for an organization to generate power:

  • Culture – the fuel that drives belonging and psychological safety

  • Relationships – the spark that builds trust and alignment

  • Execution – the engine design that keeps energy flowing

  • Agility – the suspension that enables speed without breaking apart

  • Teaching – the transmission that moves knowledge through the system

  • Ethics – the integrity that keeps the whole thing trustworthy

When transformation stalls, it’s rarely a technology problem. It’s a cylinder problem.

Culture won’t tolerate visible failure? Innovation dies before it starts. No one runs experiments that might not work.

Relationships are transactional? No one shares early-stage ideas. Why would they? There’s no trust that vulnerability gets rewarded.

Execution systems are unclear? AI tools get adopted in silos. Everyone’s doing their own thing because no one sees how the pieces connect.

Agility is missing? You can’t pivot when the first approach fails. And in transformation, the first approach always fails.

Teaching is hoarded? The pilot team learns everything. The rest of the organization learns nothing. Knowledge remains trapped in heads rather than flowing through the system.

Ethics are ambiguous? People hedge. They cover themselves. They avoid exactly the risks that transformation requires.

The 12% didn’t just buy better AI. They tuned all six cylinders first.

Your diagnostic: Ask three people on your team: What makes it hard to try new things here? Don’t defend. Don’t explain. Just listen. The gap between what you think is working and what they experience is where your engine is misfiring.

2. The Gearbox: VOICES

The symptom you’ll recognize:

You’re exhausted. Your calendar is full of the same meetings about the same problems. Every decision seems to need you. You keep pushing for execution, but the system-level issues persist.

You know you should be thinking about the long term. You feel the weight of what’s coming. But every time you try to lift your head, another fire pulls you back down.

The framework:

VOICES represents six leadership gears – different stances for different terrain:

  • Visionary – sets direction, paints the horizon

  • Operator – drives execution with discipline

  • Independent – empowers ownership by stepping back

  • Coach – develops people through feedback and reflection

  • Encourager – fuels belief and morale through hard stretches

  • Sculptor – redesigns the systems that make everything else possible

Most leaders have two or three gears they default to. They use those gears regardless of terrain – and then wonder why they’re burning out going uphill or spinning out on straightaways.

Here’s the gear most leaders are missing: Sculptor.

When PwC’s vanguard built “cultures that enable adoption,” they weren’t giving better speeches. They were in Sculptor gear – redesigning structures, incentives, and processes until adoption became the easier path.

The 88% still struggling? They’re stuck in Operator – pushing harder on execution within systems that weren’t designed for their requests.

You cannot Operator your way to transformation. You have to Sculpt your way there.

Your diagnostic: Track your calendar for one week. Categorize each block: which gear were you in? Most leaders report spending 70%+ in Operator mode. If you’re demanding transformation while operating in Operator, you’re asking people to perform differently within systems designed to produce the same results.

3. The Navigation: 6C Decision Compass

The symptom you’ll recognize:

You’re the bottleneck. Decisions wait for you – even ones that shouldn’t. Your direct reports are capable, but they escalate when they could resolve. You’ve told them to “take ownership,” but they keep coming back for permission.

Meanwhile, the decisions you actually need to make – the strategic and architectural ones – are getting crowded out by operational noise.

The framework:

The 6C Decision Compass distributes decision-making intentionally:

  • Command – I decide. Speed or crisis demands it.

  • Consultative – I decide, but I gather input first.

  • Collaborative – We decide together. Buy-in matters as much as the outcome.

  • Consensus – We all agree. Slow, but creates deep commitment.

  • Core Expertise – The expert decides. Someone knows more than me.

  • Choice – I set guardrails. You decide within them.

Most leaders default to Consultative. They gather input, then decide. Every decision. Every time. It feels collaborative. It feels thorough.

It also makes you the bottleneck for everything.

The vanguard didn’t become faster decision-makers. They built systems in which decisions occur at the right level and in the right mode, without routing through a single point of failure.

That 47% of CEO time spent on short-term issues? That’s not a discipline problem. It’s a decision architecture problem. When you haven’t explicitly pushed decisions to Choice or Core Expertise, they default upward. To you. Always.

Your diagnostic: Map your last ten significant decisions. For each one: What mode did you use? Was it the right mode – or just your default mode? How many of those decisions could have been distributed? –

For most leaders, the honest answer is “more than half.” That’s where your time is going.

How the Three Connect

CREATE diagnoses which cylinders are misfiring in your organizational engine.

VOICES tells you which leadership gear is required by the current terrain.

6C distributes decisions so the organization can move faster than any single leader.

The vanguard’s secret – “cultures that enable adoption” – is what happens when all three are working:

  • The Culture cylinder is tuned for experimentation, not just compliance

  • Leaders operate in Sculptor gear, redesigning systems instead of just driving execution

  • Decisions flow through Choice and Core Expertise instead of bottlenecking at the top

That’s not inspiration. It’s architecture.

And architecture is something you can build – regardless of your headcount.

The Window

Here’s what I know after two decades of watching transformation attempts succeed and fail:

Pressure doesn’t work. Demanding urgency from a disengaged workforce only accelerates the vicious cycle.

What works is design. Systems where the transformed behavior is also the easiest behavior. Where psychological safety is load-bearing infrastructure. Where decisions happen at the speed of trust.

The wave is here. AI is rewriting the rules of what’s possible – and who gets to win.

For small and mid-sized technical companies, this is the moment. You don’t have the bureaucratic drag that’s killing the enterprises. You don’t have twelve layers of sign-off between idea and implementation. You have the ability to build right now, while the giants are still trying to turn around.

But that advantage has an expiration date. The window where architecture beats budget won’t stay open forever.

Build for the wave. Or get built over by it.
