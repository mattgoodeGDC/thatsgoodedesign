Andor Is a Leadership Masterclass

On compliance, commitment, and why authority is more brittle than it looks

Matt Goode26 January 202615 min read Get new pieces by email

# The culture is drifting# VOICES

I don’t know what could have possibly caused it, but this weekend, I spent some time reflecting on leadership, authority, compliance, and commitment. For those who know me personally, you know I'm a big fan of Star Wars, and if you’re a long-time subscriber, you know I love using TV or movies to help carry the weight on a particular topic. This essay uses the (mostly) second season of Star Wars: Andor – but even if you haven’t seen it, I think you’ll still get the point.

===

There’s a moment in Andor Season 2 that should haunt anyone who thinks about leadership.

Major Partagaz – the calculating, unflappable head of the Imperial Security Bureau – sits alone in his office. He’s listening to a recording. Not intelligence briefings. Not rebel communications intercepted for analysis.

He’s listening to Nemik’s Manifesto (you can too, by clicking).

“Tyranny requires constant effort,” the voice says. “It breaks, it leaks. Authority is brittle. Oppression is the mask of fear.”

Partagaz turns it off. His subordinate enters.

“Just keeps spreading, doesn’t it?” Partagaz says.

“It’s been hard to contain.”

And in that moment, you realize: the most powerful man in Imperial intelligence knows he’s already lost. Not the battle – the argument. The idea got out. The truth is loose. And no amount of surveillance, no expansion of the security apparatus, no crackdown will put it back in the box.

This is what Andor understands about leadership that most business books never touch: the difference between control and influence, between compliance and commitment, between power that requires constant force to maintain and power that sustains itself because people actually believe.

The Empire commands. The Rebellion inspires. And over the course of two seasons, Andor builds the most sophisticated case study in leadership philosophy ever put on television – using a galaxy far, far away to show us exactly how authority crumbles when it forgets that people aren’t resources to be managed but agents who choose.

The Manifesto as Mirror

Karis Nemik died in Season 1. The young idealist was crushed by the literal weight of stolen Imperial credits during an escape. He never saw the rebellion he dreamed of. Never witnessed the Death Star’s destruction. Never learned whether any of it mattered.

But before he died, he wrote something down. He gave it to Cassian Andor – a man who, at that point, didn’t want it. “He insisted,” Vel told Cassian. And so Cassian took it. Carried it. Eventually, in a quiet moment before his mother’s funeral, he listened.

What Nemik wrote wasn’t a battle plan. It was a diagnosis.

“There will be times when the struggle seems impossible. I know this already. Alone, unsure, dwarfed by the scale of the enemy. Remember this: Freedom is a pure idea. It occurs spontaneously and without instruction. Random acts of insurrection are occurring constantly throughout the galaxy. There are whole armies, battalions that have no idea that they’ve already enlisted in the cause.

Remember that the frontier of the Rebellion is everywhere. And even the smallest act of insurrection pushes our lines forward.”

He wasn’t trying to inspire heroism. He was describing physics. The natural tendency of people toward agency, toward dignity, toward resistance when that dignity is threatened. The manifesto argues that oppression is the aberration, not freedom, and therefore requires unsustainable energy to maintain.

Then comes the line that lands differently now than it did in 2022:

“The Imperial need for control is so desperate because it is so unnatural. Tyranny requires constant effort. It breaks, it leaks. Authority is brittle. Oppression is the mask of fear.”

Oppression is the mask of fear. Read that again.

The systems that crush people don’t do so from strength. They do so from terror – terror that if they stop crushing, even for a moment, the truth will surface. That people will remember what they’re capable of. That the compliance holding everything together will evaporate like morning fog.

The Leadership Insight Beneath the Philosophy

Here’s what makes Nemik’s manifesto more than political theory: it’s a diagnosis of what sustainable leadership actually requires.

The Empire’s model is extraction. Take compliance. Demand output. Punish deviation. It works – until it doesn’t. Because extraction depletes. Every act of control costs energy. Every enforcement action requires resources. Every crackdown creates a story that spreads beyond the crackdown itself.

The Rebellion’s model is cultivation. Create conditions for agency. Trust people with the truth. Let commitment emerge from genuine choice. It’s slower. Messier. Harder to control.

But cultivation compounds. Extraction exhausts.

This is the leadership lesson hiding in Nemik’s philosophy: you cannot force people into sustainable high performance any more than you can force them into freedom.

Both have to be chosen.

And leadership that forgets this will spend increasing resources maintaining decreasing returns – until, one day, it looks around and realizes the manifesto is spreading and there’s nothing left to do but listen to it alone in an office, wondering where it all went wrong.

We’ve Been Sleeping

If Nemik provides the philosophical framework, Maarva Andor delivers its emotional truth.

Her funeral speech – delivered via hologram to the people of Ferrix, with Imperial troops watching from every corner – is the moral center of Season 1. An elderly woman, speaking from beyond death, telling her neighbors what she should have said while she was alive:

“I always wanted to be lifted. I was always eager, always waiting to be inspired. I remember every time it happened, every time the dead lifted me – with their truth.

And now I’m dead. And I yearn to lift you. Not because I want to shine or even be remembered. It’s because I want you to go on. I want Ferrix to continue. In my waning hours, that’s what comforts me most. But I fear for you.

We’ve been sleeping.”

She doesn’t blame the Empire first. She blames herself. She blames the quiet accommodations, the small surrenders, the daily decision to look away because looking was too painful, and besides, what could one person do?

“We had each other, and they left us alone. We kept the trade lanes open, and they left us alone. We took their money and ignored them, we kept their engines churning, and the moment they pulled away, we forgot them. Because we had each other. We had Ferrix.

But we were sleeping. I’ve been sleeping. And I’ve been turning away from the truth I wanted not to face.”

This is also a confession about the failure of leadership to emerge. Maarva isn’t just admitting she didn’t resist – she’s admitting she didn’t lead. She waited for inspiration instead of providing it. She wanted to be lifted rather than lift others.

The hardest truth in her speech isn’t about the Empire. It’s about Ferrix. About all the people who had the capacity to lead and chose, day after day, to wait for someone else to go first.

This is the hardest part of Andor to watch – not the torture scenes, not the prison brutality, not Luthen’s impossible choices. It’s this: the acknowledgment that tyranny doesn’t succeed through force alone. It succeeds through exhaustion. Through the slow erosion of attention. Through good people deciding that today isn’t the day, that someone else will handle it, that surely things aren’t as bad as they seem.

“There is a wound that won’t heal at the center of the galaxy. There is a darkness reaching like rust into everything around us. We let it grow, and now it’s here. It’s here, and it’s not visiting anymore. It wants to stay.

The Empire is a disease that thrives in darkness. It is never more alive than when we sleep.”

And then, the line that sparked a riot:

“If I could do it again, I’d wake up early and be fighting these bastards from the start. Fight the Empire!”

The Architecture of Complicity

Andor isn’t subtle about what makes authoritarianism possible. It shows us – in granular, procedural detail – how ordinary people become instruments of oppression.

There’s a scene early in Season 1 where Major Partagaz addresses his ISB officers. He asks a simple question: “What do we do in this building? Why are we here?”

Dedra Meero – ambitious, brilliant, utterly committed to her career – responds with the textbook answer: “We’re here to further security objectives by collecting intelligence, providing useful analysis, and conducting effective covert action, sir.”

Partagaz’s response is chilling precisely because it’s so reasonable:

“Very good, Dedra. That is verbatim from the ISB mission statement – and wrong. Security is an illusion. You want security? Call the Navy. Launch a regiment of troopers. We are healthcare providers. We treat sickness. We identify symptoms. We locate germs, whether they arise from within or have come from the outside. The longer we wait to identify a disorder, the harder it is to treat the disease.”

Healthcare providers. Treating sickness. The rebellion reframed as infection, resistance as pathology, freedom as disease.

This is how it works. Not through mustache-twirling villainy but through bureaucratic euphemism. Through career advancement tied to efficient cruelty. Through intelligent people convincing themselves they’re solving problems rather than creating them.

Dedra doesn’t think she’s evil. She thinks she’s good at her job. She thinks she’s protecting civilization from chaos. And that self-perception – that narrative of competent professionalism in service of order – allows her to authorize torture without flinching.

The system doesn’t require monsters. It requires people who won’t ask what the system requires of them.

One Way Out

Then there’s Kino Loy.

Andy Serkis plays him as a man who has come to terms with captivity. He’s a floor manager in an Imperial prison – a prisoner himself, but one who’s figured out the rules. Keep your head down. Meet the quotas. Don’t make trouble. Your sentence will end, and you’ll go home.

Except it won’t. And when Kino discovers that “release” means transfer to another facility where you’ll work until you die, something breaks in him. Or rather, something finally gets fixed.

What happens next is a masterclass in emergent leadership – the kind that doesn’t come from titles or training but from a moment of moral clarity that refuses to stay silent.

Kino doesn’t ask permission. He doesn’t form a committee. He doesn’t wait for the right moment or the perfect plan. He simply decides that the lie he’s been living inside is no longer acceptable, and he acts from that decision.

His speech over the prison intercom isn’t polished. It’s not philosophy. It’s a man who just stopped believing the lie:

“My name is Kino Loy. I’m the day shift manager on Level Five. I’m speaking to you from the command center on Level Eight. We are, at this moment, in control of the facility.

How long we hang on, how far we get, how many of us make it out – all of that is now up to us. We have deactivated every floor in the facility. All floors are cold. Wherever you are right now, get up, stop the work. Get out of your cells, take charge and start climbing.

They don’t have enough guards and they know it. If we wait until they figure that out, it’ll be too late. We will never have a better chance than this, and I would rather die trying to take them down than giving them what they want.”

Then he tells them the truth:

“We know they fried a hundred men on Level Two. We know that they are making up our sentences as we go along. We know that no one outside here knows what’s happening. And now we know, that when they say we are being released, we are being transferred to some other prison to go and die – and that ends today!”

“One way out.”

The prisoners repeat it. One way out. One way out. One way out.

What makes the scene devastating isn’t the uprising itself – it’s what happens after. Kino leads his people to freedom. They reach the edge of the platform, looking down at churning water fifty meters below. The way out is to jump.

Cassian asks him if he’s coming.

“I can’t swim,” Kino says.

He helped five thousand men escape. He knew, the whole time, he wasn’t one of them. He did it anyway. That’s leadership.

The Cost of Lucidity

Luthen Rael operates in a different register entirely.

He’s not an idealist. He’s the architect – the one who builds the networks, funds the operations, and makes the impossible choices. When his spy, Lonni Jung, begs to be released from his double life, Luthen refuses. The mission is too important. The asset, too valuable.

Lonni, desperate, throws Luthen’s detachment back at him: “And what do you sacrifice?”

What follows is the greatest monologue in Star Wars history. And I don’t say that lightly.

“Calm. Kindness. Kinship. Love. I’ve given up all chance at inner peace. I’ve made my mind a sunless space. I share my dreams with ghosts.

I wake up every day to an equation I wrote fifteen years ago from which there’s only one conclusion: I’m damned for what I do. My anger, my ego, my unwillingness to yield, my eagerness to fight – they’ve set me on a path from which there is no escape.

I yearned to be a savior against injustice without contemplating the cost, and by the time I looked down, there was no longer any ground beneath my feet.”

Then the turn:

“What is my sacrifice? I’m condemned to use the tools of my enemy to defeat them. I burn my decency for someone else’s future. I burn my life to make a sunrise that I know I’ll never see.

And the ego that started this fight will never have a mirror or an audience or the light of gratitude.

So what do I sacrifice? – -- Everything.“

Luthen isn’t asking for sympathy. He’s explaining the arithmetic of resistance. Someone has to do the work that can’t be discussed in polite company. Someone has to make calls that would horrify the people who benefit from them. Someone has to hold the darkness so others can walk toward light.

He’ll never be thanked. He’ll be remembered, if at all, as a dangerous man who went too far. And he made that choice with open eyes, years ago, and makes it again every morning.

Most leadership literature focuses on what you gain from leading: influence, impact, legacy, and the satisfaction of making a difference. Luthen inverts this entirely. He’s cataloging what leadership costs when it’s real – when the stakes are existential, and the work can’t be sanitized for public consumption.

This isn’t inspirational. It’s not supposed to be. It’s honest. And that honesty is itself a form of leadership: treating the people around him as adults who deserve to understand what they’re signing up for.

What Andor Teaches About Real Leadership

Here’s what most leadership discourse gets wrong: it assumes the goal is influence, impact, legacy. It assumes leadership is about the leader.

Andor argues the opposite.

  • Real leadership creates conditions for others to act. Mon Mothma doesn’t fire a shot. She speaks the truth in a chamber where truth has become contraband – knowing it will cost her everything. Her speech about the Ghorman massacre, calling out genocide by name while the Empire tries to cut her feed, isn’t a strategy. It’s a witness. And that witness gives others permission to see what they’ve been pretending not to notice. Believe your eyes, not what the government is telling you.

  • Real leadership transfers ownership. Luthen doesn’t just recruit operatives – he builds infrastructure that survives him. Networks that keep moving when nodes go dark. A rebellion that doesn’t depend on any single person, especially not him.

  • Real leadership makes itself unnecessary. Kino’s final gift to the prisoners isn’t his leadership. It’s their own agency. By the time they’re jumping into the water, they don’t need him anymore. That’s the point.

  • Real leadership tells the truth about cost. Nemik’s Manifesto doesn’t promise victory. It promises that trying is worth it anyway. That’s harder than inspiration. That’s respect for the people you’re asking to sacrifice.

  • And perhaps most importantly: real leadership distinguishes between compliance and commitment. The Empire gets compliance through surveillance, punishment, and fear. The Rebellion gets commitment through shared purpose, transparent stakes, and genuine choice. One requires constant effort to maintain. The other sustains itself.

The Closing of the Fist

There’s a speech early in Season 1 that gets less attention than it deserves. After the Aldhani heist, the Empire’s response is swift and comprehensive. Colonel Yularen addresses the ISB:

“The criminals responsible for last night’s atrocity on Aldhani think they’ve taken the Empire by surprise. We know better. We know the real shock will be when they discover how ready and eager we are to respond.

This is why we plan. This is why we work so hard when we’re at peace. This is why we recruit so carefully and demand so much.

The following measures will be adopted Empire-wide as of today. A tribute tax equal to five times the amount stolen from Aldhani will be levied on any sector harboring partisan activity. We will make it clear that no one steals from the Empire.”

Luthen, watching from the shadows, explains to Mon Mothma why this is exactly what they needed:

“We need them angry. We need them coming down hard. Oppression breeds rebellion. The Empire has been choking us so slowly, we’re starting not to notice. The time has come to force their hand.”

This is the dark genius of Andor’s political analysis. Oppression doesn’t just create suffering – it creates recruits. Every overreach, every crackdown, every demonstration of the fist closing tighter wakes up another Maarva. Another Kino. Another person who was trying to stay asleep but can no longer do so.

The Empire thinks control is the solution. It’s actually the problem.

This is the fundamental design flaw in any leadership model built on control: it produces the very resistance it’s designed to suppress.

Tighten the grip, and you radicalize the moderates. Punish dissent, and you prove the dissenters right. Make examples, and you create martyrs. Every act intended to restore order becomes evidence that order was never the point – power was.

Leaders who understand this know that sustainable authority comes from legitimacy, not force. The Empire never learns this lesson. By the time Partagaz is listening to Nemik’s manifesto alone in his office, it’s far too late.

The Frontier Is Everywhere

So what does any of this mean for us – for people watching a television show about a galaxy far, far away while our own politics curdle and our own institutions strain?

I’ll leave you with Nemik’s closing instruction. Not a battle cry. Not a promise of victory. Just a simple, almost quiet imperative:

“And know this: the day will come when all these skirmishes and battles, these moments of defiance, will have flooded the banks of the Empire’s authority – and then there will be one too many. One single thing will break the siege.

Remember this.

Try.“

Not succeed. Not win. Not a guarantee of anything.

Try.

Because the frontier of the rebellion is everywhere. Because even the smallest act of insurrection pushes the lines forward. Because tyranny requires constant effort, and we don’t have to make it easy.

Because the people who come after us deserve to inherit something worth fighting for.

Leadership, Andor argues, isn’t a position. It’s a choice.

Made repeatedly, often at personal cost, with no guarantee of recognition or success. It’s Nemik writing a manifesto he’ll never see spread. It’s Maarva recording a speech she’ll never deliver in person. It’s Kino leading an escape he can’t join. It’s Luthen burning his life for a sunrise he’ll never see.

And it’s everyone who hears their words and decides: I won’t sleep anymore.

And because when Partagaz listens to that recording and says “Just keeps spreading, doesn’t it?” – the answer, the only answer that matters, is yes.

It does.

What truth have you been turning away from? What would it mean to wake up early and start fighting?

Brittle authority is never more alive than when we sleep. Wake up.
