Re-Engaging Engagement: What Gallup’s Data Actually Reveals

How the decline is actually an invitation to do more.

Matt Goode29 January 202613 min read Get new pieces by email

# The culture is drifting# EQ

The decade-long gains in employee engagement didn’t just stall. They exposed what was fragile all along.

Gallup released its 2025 employee engagement data this week, and the numbers are sobering. Active engagement sits at 31%, unchanged from 2024, but down five points from the 2020 peak of 36%. That’s roughly 8 million fewer engaged workers over five years.

But here’s the thing: I don’t want to write another hand-wringing piece about declining engagement. We’ve had enough of those. Instead, I propose something counterintuitive – this decline is an invitation. Not to do more of what wasn’t working, but to fundamentally rethink what engagement was supposed to be in the first place.

The Signal in the Noise

Look at where engagement is collapsing most dramatically: younger workers. Gen Z and younger millennials saw an eight-point drop. Older millennials dropped nine points. Baby boomers? Zero change.

The conventional reading is that younger workers are “quiet quitting” or have some generational deficiency in work ethic. That reading is lazy and incorrect.

Here’s what the data actually shows: the workers who are most dependent on organizational systems – on clear expectations, on development, on being told what excellence looks like – are the ones falling through the cracks. The workers who’ve been around long enough to figure things out on their own and build their own mental models of success are doing fine.

This isn’t a generational problem. It’s a design problem.

The Older Millennial Paradox

Here’s what makes this data even more telling: older millennials (ages 37–44) actually declined more than their younger counterparts – nine points versus eight. That’s counterintuitive. If younger workers are more system-dependent, shouldn’t they fall furthest when systems fail?

The answer reveals something important about how organizational decay actually works.

Older millennials are at the career stage where early promises should have materialized. They’ve been waiting 15+ years for the development paths, the promotions, the leadership opportunities they were implicitly promised. When those systems degrade, individuals experience this as betrayal, not merely as absence. Gen Z never had those promises to begin with – they entered a workplace that was already eroded.

There’s also the burden of institutional memory. Older millennials remember when onboarding was thorough, when managers had time for developmental conversations, when career paths were legible. They’re watching systems they once relied on collapse in real time. Younger workers don’t know what they’re missing.

And then there’s the squeezed middle. Workers in their late 30s and early 40s are often in middle management or senior individual contributor roles. They’re expected to compensate for absent systems (heroic management), absorb pressure from above (do more with less), and shield their teams from chaos below. They’re the buffer layer. When systems fail, they absorb the damage personally.

This actually strengthens the system’s argument. It’s not just that younger workers need more support – it’s that everyone needs systems. When the people who should be most resilient – experienced, established, organizationally fluent – are declining fastest, that’s not a generational signal. That’s a structural one.

The canary isn’t just Gen Z. It’s the whole mine.

What’s Actually Broken

Gallup’s qualitative analysis cuts to the point. Employees identified two things that would help most:

  1. Better communication about both company direction and their own development (35%)

  2. Supportive relationships, communication, and respect (34%)

Note what’s not on this list: ping-pong tables, unlimited PTO, or even compensation (though 23% did mention pay and benefits). The fundamentals people are asking for aren’t expensive perks. They’re the fundamentals of organizational functioning.

One employee response captured it perfectly: “If we have a complaint, the response is always, ‘remember why you’re here’ or ‘have more grace’ or ‘practice gratitude,’ which is not solving the problem and making us feel bad for even bringing something up.”

That’s not a communication problem. That’s a psychological safety problem disguised as wellness language.

The Clarity Crisis

Here’s the data point that should keep every leader up at night: less than one-third of leaders themselves strongly agree they have a clear definition of what exceptional performance looks like in their role.

Let that sink in. The people responsible for setting expectations, developing talent, and defining success can’t clearly articulate what success looks like.

And we wonder why younger workers – the ones still learning the unwritten rules of professional life – feel lost?

When leaders lack clarity about exceptional performance, that ambiguity cascades. Managers can’t coach to standards they can’t articulate. Individual contributors can’t aim at targets they can’t see. The whole system runs on vibes and implicit knowledge that some people absorb and others never access.

This isn’t a training problem. It’s an architecture problem.

The Real Opportunity

Here’s where I get optimistic – but not in a rah-rah, let’s-do-engagement-surveys-harder kind of way.

The decline in engagement reveals what has always been true: many organizations have never built the infrastructure for engagement. They built the appearance of it. They implemented engagement initiatives without addressing the underlying systems that create clarity, development, and psychological safety.

When times were good and the labor market was tight, heroic managers could paper over these gaps. Individual leaders with high emotional intelligence could create pockets of engagement through the sheer force of their relational skills. The system appeared to be working because talented people were compensating for its absence.

The pandemic disrupted that compensation mechanism. Remote work, then hybrid work, then return-to-office mandates, then economic uncertainty – each wave stripped away another layer of heroic compensation. What we’re seeing now isn’t engagement declining. We’re seeing the actual baseline that was always there once the heroics became unsustainable.

And that’s the opportunity: stop relying on heroic managers and start building systems that create engagement structurally.

From Heroic to Architectural

I think about organizational health through three integrated frameworks: CREATE (the engine that powers sustainable leadership), VOICES (the gearbox that enables leaders to shift approaches), and the 6C Decision Compass (the navigation system that clarifies who decides what and how). Together, they form a complete system for building organizations that don’t depend on heroic individuals to function.

CREATE is a six-cylinder engine: Culture, Relationships, Execution, Agility, Teaching, and Ethics. Each cylinder performs distinct work, but they fire sequentially and depend on one another. When all six are running in sync, decisions are made with clarity, teams adapt without chaos, and people grow without burnout. When cylinders misfire, you get the symptoms Gallup is measuring – disengagement, confusion, and attrition.

VOICES is a leadership gearbox with six modes: Visionary, Operator, Independent, Collaborator, Empath, and Sculptor. Different situations and different people require different gears. The problem is that most leaders get stuck in one or two gears and never shift – usually Visionary (inspiration) and Operator (task management). That’s like driving a manual transmission using only first and fourth gear.

The 6C Decision Compass provides six decision modes: Command, Consultative, Collaborative, Consensus, Core Expertise, and Choice. Each mode answers the question “who decides, and how?” When decision authority is ambiguous, everything slows down – especially for people who haven’t yet learned to navigate organizational politics by instinct.

When I map Gallup’s findings onto these frameworks, the diagnosis becomes actionable.

The three things employees say are failing map directly to CREATE:

  • “Feeling cared about” = Culture + Relationships cylinders misfiring

  • “Opportunities to learn and grow” = Teaching + Agility cylinders stalled

  • “Clarity about expectations” = Execution + Ethics cylinders running rough

The heroic manager problem maps to VOICES: Most leaders get stuck in two gears – Visionary (here’s where we’re going!) and Operator (here’s what I need you to do today). That’s like driving with only forward and reverse. You can technically get somewhere, but you’re grinding the transmission.

Young workers don’t need more vision or more task management. They need leaders who can shift into Coach (individualized development), Sculptor (system redesign), and Collaborator (shared problem-solving). Those gears require more skill – and most managers were never taught to use them.

The clarity crisis maps to the 6C Decision Compass: Ambiguity about “what exceptional performance looks like” is, at its core, ambiguity about decisions: who decides, how, and when. When decision authority is opaque, young workers – who haven’t yet accumulated the political capital to push through ambiguity – freeze. Making decision modes explicit (Command, Consultative, Collaborative, Consensus, Core Expertise, Choice) eliminates the “is this my call?” anxiety that kills both clarity and initiative.

Re-Engaging Engagement: By Level

The fix isn’t one thing. It’s the right things at each level of the organization. Here’s what leaders can do based on where they sit:

Team Leaders and Direct Managers

This is where the damage is happening – and where recovery is fastest. The relationship between a direct manager and their reports accounts for up to 70% of the variance in engagement scores. If you’re a team leader, you have more leverage than anyone else in the organization.

  • On clarity: Document what “good” looks like for each deliverable type. Not vibes. Not “you’ll know it when you see it.” Actual examples, with commentary on why they’re good. Young workers are operating without guidance because no one documented the standards that experienced workers absorbed through years of osmosis. Create a “What Good Looks Like” document for your team – with before/after examples, annotated deliverables, and explicit quality criteria. This isn’t micromanagement. It’s infrastructure.

  • On development: Shift your 1:1s from status updates to developmental conversations. Replace “What’s your status on X?” with “What are you learning? Where are you stuck? What would help you grow?” This is the Teaching cylinder – and for most managers, it’s barely running. Gallup’s data show that young workers are 11 points less likely to report having opportunities to learn and grow than in 2020. That’s not because learning opportunities disappeared. It’s because managers stopped creating space for developmental conversation.

  • On psychological safety: When concerns are raised, take visible action. The “have more grace” response that Gallup captured is a failure in the Relationships cylinder. It tells employees that raising problems makes them the problem. Even if you can’t solve the issue, acknowledge it, explain what you can and can’t do, and follow up. The visibility of response matters more than the perfection of the solution.

  • On decision clarity: Make decision authority explicit on every assignment. Say the words: “This is yours to decide,” or “Gather input and recommend to me,” or “I’ll decide, but I want your perspective first.” The anxiety of “Is this my call?” is crushing for new workers. They haven’t yet built the political capital to push through ambiguity, and they haven’t learned to read the implicit signals that experienced workers rely on instinctively. Name the mode. Every time. It takes five seconds and prevents days of paralysis.

Middle Managers and Directors

You establish the systems within which team leaders operate. If your systems require heroics, you’ll burn through managers.

  • On clarity: Are your norms explicit or do they require “reading the room”? Build a norms document. Make implicit expectations visible. The best predictor of whether young workers thrive isn’t their talent – it’s whether anyone bothered to write down the rules of the game.

  • On development: Build feedback loops that are fast and specific. Annual reviews are useless for people who need to know now if they’re on track. Establish structures for monthly or biweekly developmental check-ins, and hold your team leaders accountable for conducting them.

  • On psychological safety: Create legitimate channels for concerns – not suggestion boxes, but channels where something happens, and people can see that it happened. If concerns go unaddressed, people stop raising them. Then you lose visibility into problems until they explode.

  • On leadership modeling: Stop rewarding heroic managers and start building systems that work even when the manager is having a bad month. If team engagement collapses whenever the manager is stressed, you don’t have a system. You have a dependency.

VPs and Senior Leaders

You control resource allocation and strategic priorities. If manager development isn’t funded, it won’t happen.

  • On clarity: Define exceptional performance at the department level. In writing. With examples. If you can’t articulate it, your managers definitely can’t, and their direct reports are flying blind.

  • On development: Invest in manager capability. The managers are failing at development because no one taught them how – they were promoted for being good individual contributors, not for their ability to grow others. This is a Training cylinder failure at the organizational level.

  • On accountability: Hold leaders accountable for development outcomes, not just business outcomes. What gets measured gets managed. If you only measure revenue and delivery, you’ll get managers who sacrifice their people to hit numbers. Then you’ll wonder why engagement is declining.

  • On modeling: The default senior leader mode is Visionary + Operator – strategy cascades and execution pressure. Young workers never see senior leaders in developmental or system-redesign mode. When you model Coach and Sculptor at scale, you signal that these gears matter.

CEOs and Executives

You set the conditions that make everything else possible – or impossible. Your choices about what gets resourced, measured, and celebrated cascade through every level below you.

  • On clarity: Treat clarity as infrastructure, not communication. Saying the strategy louder doesn’t create clarity. Building systems that make the strategy visible in daily decisions does. If people can’t connect their work to organizational priorities without a heroic manager translating, you have an architecture problem. Consider: does your strategic plan exist only in slide decks presented quarterly, or is it embedded in how decisions are actually made at every level?

  • On culture: Stop treating engagement as an HR metric and start treating it as organizational architecture. Engagement isn’t something you measure. It’s something you build. The survey tells you whether you’ve built it – but the survey isn’t the intervention. When engagement declines, the answer isn’t more surveys or more town halls. It’s examining the systems that create (or fail to create) clarity, development, and belonging.

  • On development investment: If manager development isn’t funded at the executive level, it will not occur at scale. The managers are failing at development because no one taught them how – they were promoted for being good individual contributors. You can’t expect managers to build capabilities they were never given. Budget for it. Protect the time for it. Make it a strategic priority, not an HR nice-to-have.

  • On decision transparency: Be explicit about your own decision-making process. When executives make decisions without naming the mode, the whole organization learns that power is opaque. When you say “I’m making this call, but here’s what informed it” or “We’re going to decide this collaboratively – here’s the process,” you teach the organization that clarity is a value. Model the behavior you want to cascade.

Diagnostic Questions

Before you launch another engagement initiative, answer these honestly:

CREATE Diagnostic:

  • Can a new employee figure out how to succeed without finding a sponsor to decode the unwritten rules? (Execution + Culture)

  • When someone raises a concern, does something visible happen? Or does it disappear into “gratitude”? (Relationships + Ethics)

  • Are your managers having developmental conversations, or just status updates? (Teaching + Agility)

VOICES Diagnostic:

  • What gears do your managers actually use? If it’s just Visionary and Operator, they’re grinding the transmission.

  • When’s the last time a senior leader visibly operated in Coach or Sculptor mode?

  • Are you rewarding heroic managers or building systems that don’t require heroics?

6C Diagnostic:

  • On any given project, can team members name who decides what, and how?

  • Do young workers know when something is their call versus when they need to escalate?

  • Is decision authority explicit, or does it require reading political tea leaves?

The Path Forward

The Gallup data isn’t a crisis. It’s a diagnosis. The organizational immune systems we thought we had were largely fictional – dependent on individual heroics that couldn’t scale and couldn’t sustain.

The good news: we now know what’s actually broken. Not generational work ethic. Not remote work. Not TikTok attention spans. What’s broken is the basic architecture of clarity, development, and psychological safety that organizations never fully built.

Younger workers aren’t failing to engage. They’re revealing what engagement always required – and what most organizations never provided. The boomers aren’t more engaged because they have better values. They’re more engaged because they’ve had decades to build their own workarounds for broken systems. Younger workers haven’t had that time yet, and they’re telling us – through their disengagement – that the systems need to operate independently.

This is actually good news, if you’re willing to hear it. Because systems can be built. Heroism can’t be scaled, but architecture can. And the organizations that figure this out first will have a significant competitive advantage in attracting and retaining the talent others are losing.

Now we get to build the real thing. Not with another survey. Not with another town hall. Not with another “manager training” that teaches theory without changing systems. With actual infrastructure – documented standards, explicit decision rights, developmental conversations that happen reliably, and feedback channels that visibly respond.

That’s the work. And it starts wherever you sit.

What systems in your organization actually create clarity, development, and belonging – independent of heroic managers? I’d love to hear what’s working. And if you’re building these systems and want to think through the architecture together, that’s the work I do.
