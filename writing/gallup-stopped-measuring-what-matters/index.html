Gallup Stopped Measuring What Matters Most

Matt Goode13 February 20264 min read Get new pieces by email

# EQ# The culture is drifting

I use Gallup’s CliftonStrengths assessment with nearly every client I serve – founders, school leaders, and technical teams. I’ve built a significant part of my consulting practice on Gallup’s tools and philosophy.

But it’s not just the strengths work. I rely on Gallup’s data – and specifically its reputation for nonpartisan rigor – to make sense of the world beyond my practice. Just last week, I published an essay drawing on Gallup’s “Most Important Problem” research, the kind of long-running, methodologically consistent data that lets you cut through partisan noise and see what Americans actually care about.

I believe in the science. I point clients and readers to Gallup’s name as a mark of independence and credibility.

Which is exactly why I’m not going to be quiet about this.

On February 11, Gallup announced it will no longer publish presidential approval ratings or favorability ratings of political figures – ending an 88-year tradition that began during the Great Depression, when George Gallup first sent pollsters across the country to ask ordinary Americans what they thought of their president.

Their explanation? The ratings are now “widely produced, aggregated and interpreted” and “no longer represent an area where Gallup can make its most distinctive contribution.”

That’s not a strategy. That’s an exit.

The Measurement Company That Stopped Measuring

One of my personal mantras – one I bring into every coaching engagement and every leadership system I design – is simple: what gets measured gets done. Gallup taught me that. Their entire brand is built on the premise that what gets measured gets understood. They sell that idea to every organization that buys their engagement platform, every coach who gets certified in their methodology, every leader who sits down to debrief their Top 5.

And the thing that made Gallup Gallup – before the StrengthsFinder, before Q12, before the engagement surveys – was the radical, democratic act of asking Americans what they thought about power.

That was the original product. That was the mission.

Now they’ve decided it’s no longer where they “make their most distinctive contribution.” In 2026. During one of the most consequential political periods in modern American history. With independent institutions under pressure everywhere you look.

The timing doesn’t just raise questions. It answers them.

Who Actually Loses

Yes, other pollsters measure approval ratings. FiveThirtyEight aggregates them. The data will still exist in some form.

But the loss runs deeper than headlines.

Political science loses its longest continuous dataset. The American Presidency Project at UC Santa Barbara has called Gallup the only source that consistently asked the same approval question since the dawn of scientific opinion polling. Researchers studying presidential power, electoral behavior, and democratic accountability don’t just need a poll – they need the same poll, asked the same way, across decades. That continuity is irreplaceable. Every new pollster who picks up the question starts a new clock.

Methodological rigor loses its most visible champion. Gallup was one of the last major firms still conducting presidential approval polls using live telephone interviews – an expensive, rigorous methodology that most pollsters have abandoned for cheaper online surveys. When Gallup’s number disagreed with the aggregator’s, it meant something. That independent signal is gone.

And the American public loses something harder to name. When a major nonpartisan institution believes that tracking the public’s assessment of its leaders is worth doing – even when the results are inconvenient – that sends a signal about what a democracy owes itself. When that institution walks away, it sends a different signal entirely.

When institutions voluntarily retreat from accountability work, they don’t create a vacuum that gets filled. They create permission for the next institution to retreat, too.

You Don’t Get to Sell Courage and Practice Retreat

I sit across from leaders every week and tell them that self-awareness requires honest data. That you can’t build a healthy culture without rigorous measurement. That the hard numbers – even the uncomfortable ones – are the foundation on which everything else is built.

So when the company that built the temple of measurement quietly closes the door on the most publicly visible measurement it ever created – not because the methodology failed, but because the political environment got uncomfortable – I notice the gap between the sermon and the practice.

The Institutional Retreat Diagnostic

Gallup isn’t the only organization navigating this tension. If you lead one – or advise one – these are worth sitting with:

  1. What are we still measuring because it’s easy, and what have we stopped measuring because it’s uncomfortable?

  2. If we dropped our most publicly visible commitment tomorrow, would our explanation survive a single honest follow-up question?

  3. Are we evolving our mission – or editing it to avoid friction?

The Bottom Line

I’ll keep using CliftonStrengths – the science is sound, and it changes lives. But I’m not going to pretend this decision is just a business pivot.

Gallup made its name by believing that the voice of the people was worth measuring, especially when it was inconvenient for the powerful. Walking away from that work doesn’t reflect an “evolution.” It reflects a choice. And choices have consequences – for credibility, for public trust, and for every coach and consultant who points to Gallup’s name and says, “These are the people who believe in rigorous, independent measurement.”

I still believe in the measurement. I need Gallup to believe in it, too.
