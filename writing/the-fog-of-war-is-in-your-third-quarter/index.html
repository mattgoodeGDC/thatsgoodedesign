The Fog of War Is in Your Third Quarter

Fifteen minutes inside a simulated attack, and the two gears most leaders never shift out of.

Matt Goode9 June 20264 min read Get new pieces by email

# VOICES# Winning feels like a near miss

The fake blood was the detail that got me. Not the smoke, not the blast that knocked the plan out of my head – the fake blood. Hadn't expected that.

It was a simulation. A hostile-environment course built for a posting that never came. Smoke. A vehicle still rolling behind the noise. Extras trained to scream and bleed until they owned the scene. A call that there might be a second device. Men playing militia working the edges with rifles. And a ring of staff with clipboards, grading every move. Inside ninety seconds, I stopped seeing the clipboards. The people scoring me went invisible.

Leadership runs on a gearbox. Six gears, and almost every leader lives in two: Visionary, which paints the goal, and Operator, which drives the work. Both run on control, so under pressure we grip them harder and call it range. It isn't. It's two gears and four blind spots.

A good driver doesn't shift on a schedule. They read the tachometer, feel the engine strain, watch the road, and move the instant the car asks for it. The instrument that tells you when to shift is emotional intelligence – your read on your own state, your people's, and the conditions in front of all of you. Most leaders drive with that gauge covered.

Operator got me in. Off the X, you call it in, you two with me – then hands in the work, reading bodies because the words weren't in a language I had. When two of my people clustered on one casualty and left others bleeding, ten seconds of Visionary – here's where everyone goes – then back down. Those two came easily. They always do.

Then the scene stopped being a task.

A crowd had turned while my head was down. A woman reached me first – help my baby, in a language I didn't share, holding something I had to make myself look at. You can't command panic into calm or inspire it away. So I shifted into the gear with no task in it: dropped my voice, slowed my hands, gave her a face that wasn't panicking. Ninety seconds. It was the difference.

Then the medevac came in hot and every call was routing through me. The instinct is to grip tighter. I gave the calls away instead. You own the LZ. You own the count. You own her.

Then the whistle, and the two gears the scene had no room for, waiting in the debrief.

Operator and Visionary got me started. They couldn't have finished it. A leader with only those two is right for the first ninety seconds and wrong for everything after.

And here's what the gearbox metaphor almost hides: each shift wasn't only a different move, it was a different voice, each one pitched to the person in front of me. Same leader, six registers. That's why it isn't called gears. It's called VOICES.

Where each one came out, and the voice it took:

Visionary – The lifted, horizon-pointing voice. Ten seconds mid-scene when my people clustered on one casualty: here's where everyone goes.

Operator – The clipped, directive voice. The opening minutes and the hands-in triage, running the sequence out loud.

Independent – The releasing voice: you own this, decide. When the medevac came in and every call routed through me, I handed the decisions off instead of becoming the bottleneck.

Encourager – Almost no words at all. A lowered voice, slowed hands, a steady face. At the crowd, with the mother I couldn't speak to, because steadiness is the only thing that interrupts panic.

Coach – The questioning voice. In the debrief: what would each of us do differently?

Sculptor – The structural voice. The debrief again, when several of us hit the same wall – a system problem, not a people problem.

So what does any of that have to do with your third quarter?

Yours had no smoke. But it had a blast – the hire who quit, the deal that died, the number that took the plan with it. A crowd of stakeholders wanting answers while you worked the real problem. A second device under the visible one. And clipboards – your board, your team, the market – grading every move after you'd stopped noticing them. If the quarter went sideways, I'd bet it wasn't the wrong move. You stayed in two gears and couldn't hear the moment change.

You can't control the road. That's VUCA. What you control is how you respond – which gear, which voice, and whether you're reading the car and the road closely enough to know when to shift. The instrument that does the reading is EQ. The only real skill is hearing which gear the moment is already asking for, before it forces your hand.

That's the through-line of the book I'm writing, CLUTCH. You don't need the book to start, though. You need to notice, this week, which gear you keep reaching for – and which one the room has been quietly asking you to find.
