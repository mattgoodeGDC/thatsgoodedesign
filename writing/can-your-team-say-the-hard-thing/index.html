Can your team say the hard thing?

Resolution #6: High Standards Need High Safety – or They Break People

Matt Goode16 December 20256 min read Get new pieces by email

# Psychological safety

“High expectations don’t break teams. Lack of safety does.”

This fall, I worked with a team that looked perfect on paper.

Brilliant people. Clear mission. Solid budget. Leadership that genuinely cared.

But they had one quiet pattern: They avoided saying the hard thing.

No one wanted to embarrass a teammate. No one wanted to be the difficult one. No one wanted to risk conflict.

As a result: Decisions dragged. Rework increased. Frustration grew underground.

And the founder couldn’t figure out why such talented people were executing so slowly.

This is Resolution #6 in my series on building a business that holds its shape. [Previous: Your Bottleneck Isn’t Incompetence – It’s Invisible Heroics]

When I asked them privately what was happening, one person said something that stopped me cold:

“We don’t talk about problems until they’re big enough to be safe to name.”

That sentence is the exact opposite of psychological safety. And yet – it’s incredibly common.

The Experiment That Changed Everything

So we ran an experiment.

In their next team meeting, I prompted the team lead to ask: “I want everyone to name one small thing that’s been bugging them – something too minor to bring up before.”

Silence.

Then someone said: “The project timeline in the deck doesn’t match what engineering thinks is realistic.”

Then: “I’ve been unclear on who owns the customer feedback loop.”

Then: “I think we’re solving the wrong problem for this client.”

Fourteen minutes. Seven small problems. All of them fixable – if they’d been named two weeks earlier.

The founder looked around the room and said: “Why didn’t anyone say this before?”

Another silence.

Then someone said quietly: “We didn’t think it was safe to.”

The Team That Fell Apart

During the first year of the pandemic, I watched one of the most talented teams I’d ever worked with quietly unravel.

Not because they lacked skill. Not because the workload was unreasonable. Not because people were unwilling to stretch.

They fell apart because they had no psychological safety left in the tank.

Fear replaced candor. Caution replaced initiative. Silence replaced disagreement.

People stopped asking questions because questions felt dangerous.

By the end of that year, three people had quit – not for better offers, but because they couldn’t sustain the emotional cost of working in a low-safety, high-pressure environment.

The founder was devastated.

“I thought I was pushing them to excellence,” he said. “I was just pushing them away.”

That year changed how I think about leadership.

It taught me this: The moment of crisis is the wrong time to start building safety. You build it early, deliberately, and long before you need it.

What High-Performing Teams Actually Know

Here’s what I see constantly:

Founders who hire brilliant people, set ambitious goals, and then wonder why execution feels fragile.

The answer? They built high challenge without high safety.

They assumed talent would compensate. It doesn’t.

Google’s Project Aristotle studied 180 teams and found that psychological safety was the single strongest predictor of team effectiveness – more than talent, more than resources, more than structure.

Amy Edmondson’s research shows the same pattern:

High challenge + high safety = the learning zone High challenge + low safety = anxiety zone Low challenge + low safety = apathy zone Low challenge + high safety = comfort zone

Teams don’t need less challenge. They need more safety to handle the challenge that already exists.

High talent without safety is a car without traction – the engine spins harder, but the vehicle doesn’t move.

What Safety Actually Looks Like

Psychological safety is not about comfort. It’s about capacity.

It’s not:

  • Endless affirmation

  • Softening every message

  • Avoiding accountability

  • Protecting feelings at the cost of clarity

It’s:

  • Speaking the truth without punitive consequences

  • Asking questions without fear of judgment

  • Naming problems early

  • Disagreeing respectfully

  • Giving and receiving feedback cleanly

  • Owning mistakes without shame

  • Leaders modeling vulnerability, not perfection

Safety is built in micro-moments, not grand speeches.

Like when a PM raises her hand in a leadership meeting and says: “I think we’re about to make a mistake.”

And the founder says: “Tell me more” – not defensively, just curiously.

That’s 30 seconds. But it signals to the entire room: Dissent is valued, not punished.

Three Daily Practices That Build Safety

1. Start every 1:1 with: “What’s one thing I should know that you haven’t told me yet?”

This signals you’re looking for the unsaid, not just the status update.

2. Publicly thank someone who disagreed with you in a meeting

“Sarah pushed back on my timeline estimate, and she was right. Thank you for saying it.”

3. Admit when you changed your mind based on feedback

“I was going to do X, but after hearing from the team, we’re doing Y instead.”

These aren’t dramatic gestures. They’re deliberate signals that truth-telling is expected, not exceptional.

The Team That Rebuilt

Three months after we rebuilt safety on that team, the founder told me something had shifted.

“People started flagging problems in Teams before they became fires. Engineering pushed back on unrealistic timelines – and the product thanked them for it. Someone admitted they’d made a costly error and, instead of hiding it, asked for help fixing it.”

The work didn’t get easier. But the climate changed.

And in a high-challenge environment, climate is everything.

The Psychology: Teams Retain What They Feel

Here’s what matters:

Teams retain what they feel, not what they’re told.

If a team feels safe when you give feedback, the feedback lands. If they don’t, even the clearest message gets filtered through fear.

Your job isn’t to manage the work. It’s to manage the climate in which the work becomes possible.

This is why strengthening psychological safety early works: It preloads the climate your team will need when the stress inevitably hits.

Your Identity Shift for 2026

This resolution isn’t about becoming a “nicer” leader.

It’s about becoming someone who builds climate as deliberately as you build strategy.

It’s the difference between walking into a tense meeting thinking “We need to push harder” versus “What’s making it unsafe to speak up?”

Same tension. Different operating system.

That identity creates structure. Structure creates climate. Climate creates capacity.

Because once you see yourself as someone who manages climate, not just output, everything downstream becomes cleaner, lighter, and more sustainable.

Your team stops hiding problems until they’re fires. Your meetings stop feeling performative. Your culture stops burning through good people.

And your organization stops mistaking silence for alignment.

The Question That Changes Everything

Here’s what I want you to ask yourself:

Are you building a high-challenge team, or a high-challenge, high-safety team?

Because one creates performance. The other creates attrition.

Take 30 seconds: When was the last time someone on your team disagreed with you openly? If you can’t remember, that’s your answer. Drop it in the comments – I read every one.

Your 2026 Resolution

Say it out loud: “I will build psychological safety as a daily discipline – not a crisis response.”

This resolution sticks because identity drives behavior. Not goals. Not intentions. Identity.

Once you see yourself as someone who builds climate deliberately, your decisions start aligning with that reality automatically.

Not after the burnout. Not after the conflict. Not after the silence. Before.

Because safety isn’t a reaction. It’s a discipline. And discipline is what separates teams that scale cleanly from teams that splinter under pressure.
