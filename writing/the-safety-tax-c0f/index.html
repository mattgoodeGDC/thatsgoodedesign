The Safety Tax

Matt Goode27 July 20262 min read Get new pieces by email

# Psychological safety# The culture is drifting

Every team that doesn’t feel safe is paying a tax. You won’t find it on any report, because the costs never show up as a line you can point to. They show up as absences – the thing that didn’t get said, the bet no one made, the problem that stayed buried until it was too big to bury. Fear has a price, and your team pays it before you ever see the invoice.

That’s what makes it so easy to ignore. A budget overrun gets your attention. A great idea that someone swallowed in a meeting because the room felt unsafe costs you just as much, and you never even knew it existed.

What the tax actually charges

It comes due in four places, and none of them announces itself.

It charges your ideas. The sharpest insight in the room belongs to the person who’s least sure it’s welcome, so they keep it, and you ship the second-best version without ever knowing the first one existed. It charges your risks. Smart bets require someone willing to be wrong out loud; on an unsafe team, the safe move is always to not move, so you get caution dressed up as consensus. It charges your problems. The flaw, the slip, the bad number gets hidden until it’s too late, because flagging it early feels more dangerous than letting it ride. And it charges your talent. Your best people have options, and the first thing they spend those options on is leaving a place where it isn’t safe to speak.

Caption: The Safety Tax – what an unsafe team pays, in ideas, risks, problems, and people.

Safety is the cheapest investment you’re not making

Here’s the part that should land for anyone watching a budget. Amy Edmondson’s decades of research on psychological safety keep pointing to the same thing: it isn’t a soft nicety; it’s the precondition for every behavior you actually want – candor, initiative, fast error-correction, real innovation. Teams that have it surface more problems, not fewer, and they surface them early, while they’re still cheap. Teams that don’t have it run quiet and look fine, right up until the buried thing detonates.

So the work isn’t to exhort people to “be more open.” Exhortation is free and worthless. Safety is built structurally, in what your responses teach the room: whether the person who brings bad news gets thanked or punished, whether the dissent you didn’t want to hear changes anything, whether the one who flagged the real risk is remembered for it or quietly resented. Every one of those moments either lowers or raises the tax. You are setting the rate all the time, whether you mean to or not.

Worth sitting with this week:

  • What’s the most expensive thing your team isn’t telling you right now?

  • The last time someone brought you an unwelcome truth, did the bill go up or down?

  • If safety is a rate you set, what rate did you set in your last hard meeting?

Reply and tell me: what’s the tax on your team right now?

And if this named something you’ve been circling for a while, forward it to the person who needs to see it before the bill comes due.
