A Captain Doesn’t Swim the Fastest

Matt Goode1 July 20264 min read Get new pieces by email

# Everything runs through me# DELEGATE

My friend Jennifer wrote about what swimming taught her about culture. It sent me back to a line I buried in my own acknowledgments – and to the average swimmer I used to be.

My friend Jennifer Smith wrote an essay last week about what swimming taught her about building culture. She swam for fifteen years and competed in college, and one passage stopped me. Click here to read her work.

You cannot win a swim meet on a single great swimmer, she points out. Put Michael Phelps on your roster, and you still lose if the rest of the team doesn’t show up. Winning a meet takes a collection of winners – sometimes a swimmer touching the wall in fourth place is the margin. Which is why, at the best meets, the loudest cheer in the building is often for the slowest swimmer in the pool.

That sent me looking for a line I’d buried in the acknowledgments at the end of CLUTCH.

Here it is:

Before any of the other roles, I was a college team captain. I learned that a captain is not the best player on the team. A captain is the player whose presence makes the rest of the team better. Everything since has been a variation on that lesson – and on an adage a mentor of mine repeated until it stuck: hire people smarter than you, enable them, then get out of the way.

It’s a cryptic thing to leave sitting in the front of a book about building leadership systems, and I never explained it. Jennifer’s essay is a good excuse to.

I was, by any fair measure, an average swimmer. I walked on to the team and eventually earned a scholarship. In my whole college career, I won only a handful of individual events. However, what I could be counted on for was a steady second or third – I scored points, just never the kind anyone remembers. What I loved were the relays. And whatever I brought to that team beyond a dependable third place, it wasn’t speed. It was the fledgling leadership of a teenager still working out what the word meant.

The conviction underneath that line didn’t come from my own captaincy. It came from a book that never appears in mine: Sam Walker’s The Captain Class. Walker spent a decade studying the most dominant teams in sports history, expecting the pattern to live in the stars, the coaches, or the budgets. He found it in the captains – and almost none of them were the best player on the roster. The captain of the dominant U.S. women’s soccer team wasn’t Mia Hamm or Brandi Chastain. It was Carla Overbeck, a defender one coach called average at best, who carried her teammates’ bags to their hotel rooms and passed the ball to better players the instant she got it. That humility gave her the standing to get in anyone’s face when the team needed it. Walker’s research shaped how I think about leadership more than almost anything else I read while writing, and it earned exactly zero footnotes in the final manuscript. So consider this the footnote.

Somebody builds that structure – the one where a fourth-place finish counts and the slowest swimmer is worth screaming for. On Walker’s teams, it was the captain. In Jennifer’s pools it was the culture the swimmers reinforced every grinding afternoon.

That somebody is rarely the star. And that is the part most leaders get backwards. We assume the person out front should be the most talented one in the room – the best engineer running engineering, the best closer running sales. But the captain’s job was never to swim the fastest. It was to make the team faster than the sum of its swimmers. Those are different jobs, and they call on completely different skills.

This is the whole argument of CLUTCH. Leadership is infrastructure, not performance. The work is building the conditions under which other people do their best work – and then, the hardest part, stepping back so the structure holds without you standing in the middle of it.

Get out of the way is the line leaders can’t execute. It feels like the team will sink the moment you stop holding it up. Walker’s dynasties say the opposite. The captains who built something lasting made themselves essential to the structure and invisible in the spotlight. They built teams that kept winning whether or not anyone was watching them lead.

So the line was never about being a captain. It was about building the kind of team that no longer needs one out front – the kind where the loudest cheer in the building goes to the slowest swimmer, and no one can quite tell you who taught them to cheer like that.

That’s the whole project of CLUTCH – building the structure so it holds without you standing in the middle of it. The captains who lasted made themselves essential to the system and invisible in the spotlight. The book shows you how to you do the same.
