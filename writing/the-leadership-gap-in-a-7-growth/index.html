The Leadership Gap in a 7% Growth World

Matt Goode13 November 20257 min read Get new pieces by email

# Growing faster than it's getting clearer

The jobs are growing. The leaders aren’t.

By 2030, the global economy is projected to add nearly 78 million new jobs – a 7% net increase in employment, according to the World Economic Forum’s 2025 Future of Jobs Report. On the surface, it sounds like progress: growth, opportunity, expansion. But buried beneath the optimism is a quieter signal with profound implications for every organization.

The roles are multiplying faster than the readiness to lead them.

The report’s most urgent finding isn’t technological – it’s human. The fastest-growing skill shortages are not in AI, data, or engineering, but in leadership and social influence – the very capacities that turn intelligence into alignment and innovation into sustained impact. It suggests a paradox of our age: while demand for talent is accelerating, the supply of capable, emotionally intelligent leadership is lagging.

See my recently completed series on the importance of EQ in the Age of AI…

We are, quite literally, creating jobs faster than we are creating leaders – and that widening gap is becoming the quiet constraint on global growth.

The Hollowing of the Middle

Over the past decade, organizations have flattened, automated, and optimized their way into a structural void – a quiet thinning of the very layer that once turned vision into action and culture into continuity. The so-called “hollowing out of the middle” was meant to make companies leaner and more agile. In practice, it often made them lonelier.

Harvard’s Andrew McAfee once described middle managers as “dinosaurs,” predicting that digital dashboards and enterprise systems would soon make their roles obsolete. Why rely on a regional manager when a CEO can access real-time data from anywhere in the world? Why gather the team for a huddle when a broadcast email can cascade priorities to thousands at once?

But McAfee later changed his mind – because the data, and the human experience, told a different story. MIT’s Paul Osterman, who has spent decades studying management systems, found that middle managers remain indispensable precisely because they serve as “the transmission belt between the top and the bottom” of organizations. They don’t just relay information; they interpret it. They don’t merely communicate goals; they translate them into meaning.

Remove that layer, and organizations lose their sense-making muscle – the people who convert abstraction into action, complexity into clarity, and change into something humans can actually follow.

What the Middle Actually Does

Middle managers are not relics of bureaucracy; they are the connective tissue of culture. In Paul Osterman’s research, and in Harvard’s Multiunit Enterprise study by David Garvin and Lynne Levesque, the pattern is unmistakable: middle managers perform the work that makes an organization feel human and coordinated. They form teams and help them work well together. They translate priorities across departments, bridging silos that would otherwise calcify. They make trade-offs senior leaders never see, but that ultimately determine performance. And, most importantly, they connect human meaning to organizational direction – turning abstract strategy into something people can see themselves in.

Gloria St. Martin-Lowry captures it perfectly in her Guide for New Middle Managers: these leaders are translators of purpose, architects of clarity, and mirrors of culture. They don’t just keep the lights on – they keep the work meaningful.

In the language we use at Goode Designs, they are the gearbox in the leadership engine. Vision alone is just torque; it spins in place. Execution alone is effort without direction. The middle is where those forces meet. Middle managers convert aspiration into alignment through relationships, reflection, and teaching. They make leadership transferable, not just directional.

The Human Cost of the Hollow Core

When organizations flatten too far, decisions start to bottleneck at the top, and trust quietly erodes at the bottom. The Gallup Global Leadership Report (2024) offers a sobering reality check: only 21% of employees strongly agree that they trust their organization’s leadership. It’s a reminder that the real gap isn’t just strategic – it’s relational. What’s missing isn’t alignment of objectives, but the infrastructure of connection that makes people feel seen, supported, and safe.

Gallup identifies four universal needs of followers:

  1. Trust – Keep your word.

  2. Compassion – See me.

  3. Stability – Ground me.

  4. Hope – Develop me.

Together, they form the emotional architecture of effective leadership. People want leaders who keep their word, who see them as individuals, who create predictability amid uncertainty, and who invest in their growth. None of that can be delegated to dashboards or designed into slide decks.

These needs are met in moments – the small, human exchanges that happen between tasks and across thresholds: a hallway check-in, a quick feedback loop, a moment of coaching before a meeting. Those were the micro-interactions middle managers once carried instinctively, quietly holding the culture together through presence and care.

Without that middle layer, those moments vanish – and with them, the trust, stability, and meaning that turn strategy into commitment. What’s left is a leadership gap measured not in performance metrics, but in psychological distance – a widening space between PowerPoint and performance.

When the Middle Vanishes, Innovation Slows

Another cost of the hollow middle is harder to see, but even more consequential: innovation.

In a 2023 MIT Sloan Management Review study, Ingvild De Paoli, Silvia Profili, and Alessia Sammarra found that nearly 60% of bottom-up innovation originates with middle managers – those positioned close enough to the front lines to detect weak signals, yet experienced enough to translate them into strategic relevance. They are the interpreters of insight: bridging what’s emerging on the ground with what’s envisioned at the top.

When that layer disappears, so does the organization’s adaptive intelligence. The system may still move, but it stops learning. Ideas die quietly in silos. Signals get lost in translation. The rhythm of reflection – that loop between what we observe, what we decide, and what we refine – begins to fade.

What remains is an organization that can still execute, but no longer evolve.

From Pipeline to Ecosystem

The traditional leadership pipeline model assumes a steady, linear flow of emerging managers rising to replace those ahead of them – a predictable conveyor belt of development that moves people upward as others move on. But that logic belongs to a slower era. In today’s flatter, faster world, that pipeline has collapsed. Roles change faster than readiness can form. Teams reorganize before experience compounds. The line between “leader” and “led” is increasingly fluid – and yet the systems for developing leaders remain stubbornly static.

You can’t fill a hollow middle with titles.
You have to fill it with teaching, trust, and connection.

That’s the fundamental shift underway: from hierarchy to ecosystem.

Leadership is no longer a rung on a ladder; it’s a rhythm within a system – something distributed, dynamic, and context-dependent. It isn’t just held by individuals; it’s hosted by environments.

In traditional organizations, authority sat at the top and accountability flowed downward. In modern ones, leadership capacity must circulate laterally – moving like a current, not a chain of command. Everyone carries some piece of the leadership load. The work of leadership is not merely to decide but to design – to create the conditions where clarity, alignment, and adaptability can coexist under pressure.

This is where frameworks like CREATE become essential – not as acronyms, but as architectures for how leadership functions systemically:

  • Culture anchors purpose. Leaders define the “why” and ensure everyone can see themselves in it.

  • Relationships build trust. Leadership without relationship is compliance, not commitment.

  • Execution sustains accountability – not through control, but through shared ownership and feedback loops.

  • Agility keeps systems responsive to change – sensing, adjusting, and learning in real time.

  • Teaching multiplies leadership by turning experience into transferable knowledge.

  • Ethical Decision-Making protects integrity – aligning short-term choices with long-term values.

The middle once carried these intuitively. A strong manager could hold ambiguity, soften tension, and humanize direction. They were the live connection between aspiration and action. But as that layer has thinned, the burden of meaning-making has shifted outward.

Now, every leader must intentionally design these dynamics.

The relational fluency once modeled by middle managers must be built into the system itself – through rituals, reflection, and shared language. Culture can no longer depend on proximity. It must be engineered for connection.

The organizations that thrive will be those that treat leadership not as a scarce resource, but as a renewable one – something replenished through teaching, trust, and continual reflection. Leadership, in this new era, is less about being in charge and more about being in flow – building the systems that allow others to lead with clarity and conviction.

The Leadership Flywheel

When leadership capacity lags behind organizational growth, velocity inevitably turns into volatility. The data bears it out – but so does the daily experience of teams stretched thin, trying to do more with fewer humans connecting the dots. Execution outpaces alignment. Change accelerates faster than comprehension. The system keeps moving, but its ability to make meaning lags.

The answer isn’t nostalgia for middle management – it’s not about rebuilding layers of hierarchy or returning to an older design. The solution is more radical and more human: the redistribution of leadership across systems that teach, coach, and clarify in real time.

Leadership today must circulate like oxygen – moving through conversations, decisions, and feedback loops that keep the organization alive and adaptive. The leaders who thrive in this new architecture don’t just direct work; they design clarity. They don’t just hold authority; they cultivate capability.

That’s where the next essay goes – exploring how Upskilling as a Leadership Act can rebuild the connective tissue that flattening stripped away, turning learning itself into the modern engine of trust, growth, and resilience.

Reflection Prompt

“What part of your system still depends on one overstretched middle manager – and what would it take to build that capacity into everyone?”
