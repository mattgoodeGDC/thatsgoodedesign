The Transition Tax

Matt Goode9 April 20265 min read Get new pieces by email

# My role is changing

Your organization paid approximately 22 hours this week to a cost that doesn’t appear on any balance sheet. That’s the newly promoted leader spending two hours a day navigating decisions they were never told they owned (10 hours), four direct reports each losing thirty minutes a day to unclear direction and recalibration (10 hours), and the original leader absorbing two hours of decisions that should never have reached their level. The promotion happened last month. The tax started the day you made it.

Nobody tracked those hours. Nobody will.

Here’s how it happened.

Maya spent six years becoming your best ops lead. She ran projects clean, kept her team intact during the hard quarters, and knew the difference between a process problem and a people problem. Last month you gave her a title, four direct reports, a new box on the org chart, and your genuine confidence that she was ready.

What you didn’t give her: a definition of what decisions she now owns and which ones still need your sign-off. A structured handoff of the relationships she inherited. A plan for the first ninety days that was about her development, not her team’s deliverables. Any scaffold at all.

So she’s doing what promoted leaders always do. She’s figuring it out.

Organizations grow people into leadership roles faster than they grow leadership capability.

Not because leaders are unwilling. Not because managers are failing. Because promotion is visible, fast, and cheap, and leadership infrastructure is invisible, slow, and expensive. So organizations do the visible thing. They hand someone a new title and call it an investment.

It isn’t. It’s a liability with a delayed payment schedule.

The research is specific about what that liability costs. According to Josh Bersin’s work on internal mobility, replacing a leader externally costs three to five times more than growing one from within. McKinsey’s data adds that organizations promoting internally are 2.2 times more likely to outperform peers, and their leaders reach full productivity 50% faster. The implication runs in both directions: internal development done well is a performance multiplier, and internal development done without infrastructure is the most expensive version of both outcomes at once. You promoted from within, which was right. You handed over a title without a handoff system, which is where the tax starts running.

The cost lands in three places simultaneously, which is part of why it’s so easy to miss.

The promoted person is operating without decision rights they can name, relationships they’ve had time to earn, or a framework for the ten situations they’ve never encountered before. They’re burning goodwill they don’t know they have by making calls that weren’t theirs to make, and deferring on calls they should own. They’re learning leadership by practicing it on the people they manage. That’s not professional development. It’s extraction.

The team under them is being managed by someone in the middle of figuring out what managing means. The cost is real and diffuse – slower decisions, unclear ownership, the particular exhaustion that comes from not knowing what your manager is trying to accomplish. High performers start looking for clarity, and some of them schedule the call with the recruiter who’s been in their inbox for three months.

The original leader – the founder, the VP above the new role – is now absorbing the gap between what the promoted person can do and what the role actually requires. You’re adding feedback loops that shouldn’t exist at your level, catching things that should have been caught earlier, making yourself available in ways that weren’t in the plan. The bottleneck you were trying to remove just moved one level down. And now it’s hiding.

This isn’t a management failure. It’s not a hiring mistake or a performance problem. It’s a design choice – one that most organizations make by default because they haven’t built an alternative. The alternative is expensive and invisible, and the default is cheap and fast, and most organizations are busy. So they choose the default.

The problem is that defaults compound.

Every underprepared leader produces more underprepared leaders. Every promotion without infrastructure teaches the next person what promotion looks like.

What actually promoting someone requires isn’t mentorship. It isn’t check-ins. It’s three specific things, and they all have to happen before day one in the new role. Together, they form the core of DELEGATE, the handoff framework at the center of CLUTCH.

Role clarity with decision rights. Not a job description – a map of what this person now owns, where the edges are, and what “escalate” means in practice. Decision rights that aren’t explicit get filled in by assumption, and assumption is where teams break.

An explicit relationship handoff. Who did this role interface with, what does each of those people need from this position, and what’s the current status of each relationship? This doesn’t happen automatically. It requires a conversation and a transfer of context.

A ninety-day capability plan. Not an onboarding checklist. A development arc: what does this person need to be able to do by day thirty, day sixty, day ninety that they can’t fully do today, and what’s the mechanism for building it?

Before you hand anyone a new title, three questions:

  1. Can this person name three decisions that will now be theirs to own that weren’t before, and three they’ll need to give up?

  2. Can you describe, specifically, what support they’ll receive in the first ninety days – and is that support structural or interpersonal? Lunch doesn’t count.

  3. If they struggled visibly in month two, what’s the plan that isn’t “have a conversation”?

These aren’t interview questions. They’re a conversation you have with yourself – or with your leadership team – before the title changes.

Think about the last promotion you made. Not a title change: a genuine transfer of leadership responsibility.
