The Most Expensive Silence in Business

Matt Goode24 July 20263 min read Get new pieces by email

# Psychological safety# The culture is drifting

You’ve been in the meeting. A decision is forming, the room is nodding, and something in your gut says this is wrong – but you look around, no one else is objecting, the boss seems set, and the moment to speak passes. Multiply that one swallowed objection across a whole organization, and you have the most expensive silence in business.

The leaders living inside it usually misread it. They see a quiet, agreeable team and call it alignment. It isn’t. It’s a culture that has learned, correctly, that speaking up costs more than staying quiet.

Silence isn’t apathy. It’s fear.

Here’s what leaders get wrong about a team that’s gone quiet: they assume people stopped caring. They didn’t. People don’t stop speaking up because they don’t care. They stop because it isn’t safe. Somewhere – maybe not even with this leader – they learned that the person who raised the hard question got talked over, or labeled “not a team player,” or quietly remembered at review time. So they did the rational thing. They kept the objection to themselves.

The tragedy is that the objection was often the most valuable thing in the room, and the culture priced it out.

Go fever, and what silence costs

The starkest example is also the most studied. In January 1986, engineers at NASA’s contractor knew the Challenger’s O-rings could fail in cold weather. Some of them said so, the night before. But the launch culture had a name the Rogers Commission would later make famous – “go fever” – a pressure to proceed in which raising a concern felt like disloyalty, like not being on the team. The dissent existed. The culture just made it unspeakable. Seven people died.

Most silence doesn’t end in a disaster that makes the news. It ends in the product flaw no one flagged, the star performer who quit without warning, the strategy everyone privately doubted and no one challenged. Same mechanism, smaller invoice, paid over and over.

Caption: When it isn’t safe vs when it is – silence hides problems until they’re expensive.

Safety is infrastructure, not a vibe

The fix isn’t a poster about “speaking up” or an open door you announce and never test. Psychological safety is infrastructure – something you build into how the team operates, in the Culture cylinder of CREATE. It gets built in small, visible moments: what happens the first time someone brings you bad news (do you thank them, or shoot the messenger?), how you respond to the dissent you didn’t want to hear, whether the person who flagged the risk that turned out to be real gets the credit or gets forgotten.

Amy Edmondson’s research keeps landing on a counterintuitive point: the highest-performing teams aren’t the ones with the fewest problems raised – they’re the ones with the most, because safety means problems surface early, while they’re still cheap to fix. If your team has gone quiet under pressure, the work isn’t to demand more candor. It’s to make candor safe, one response at a time, until telling you the truth stops feeling like a risk.

Worth sitting with this week:

  • When did someone last bring you bad news early – and what happened to them afterward?

  • Where is your team’s quiet being read as agreement when it’s actually fear?

  • What would change if the person who raised the unwelcome concern was the one who got rewarded?
