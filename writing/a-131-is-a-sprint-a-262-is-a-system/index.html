A 13.1 Is a Sprint. A 26.2 Is a System.

Matt Goode23 April 20265 min read Get new pieces by email

# Winning feels like a near miss

Patriots Day in Boston. THE marathon was run this past Monday. I haven’t qualified.

My PR is 3:30. For the BQ I’m chasing, I need 3:15. 15 minutes to shave across 26.2 miles. 30 seconds a mile. On paper, modest.

It means moving from an 8:00 pace to a 7:30 pace. In practice, a different race.

The Marine Corps was my first marathon. I’ve run others since. My halves are fast. Once I doubled up, Baltimore on Saturday and then flew to Boston to run the BAA Half Marathon on Sunday, I ran both under 1:30. That’s under 7:00 pace. Twice in 24 hours. I smoked my brother-in-law.

So I can run a 7:00 pace. I’ve proved it. I just can’t hold 7:30 for twice as long.

That’s the gap I’ve been chasing for years. Not a speed gap. An architecture gap.

Finishing a marathon, though, isn’t a big deal. How do you know if someone has run a marathon? Don’t worry: They’ll tell you. 🙋 Guilty.

Oprah ran a marathon, no disrespect. Ashton Kutcher ran 3:54. Ryan Reynolds ran 3:50. I smoked them both. Then Harry Styles ran 3:24 and smoked me.

Finishing is fitness. Qualifying is architecture.

A half-marathon and a full marathon are not the same race doubled. They are physiologically different events. The half finishes before mile 20. Before your glycogen runs out. Before the point where your training either holds or it doesn’t.

You can win a half on fitness and grit. You cannot win a full without the engine underneath.

Mile 22 of a full isn’t mile 12 of a half with more grit. It’s a different event entirely. One is still a sprint. The other is past the horizon of what willpower reaches.

Arlington taught me the rest.

In the Marine Corps Marathon, the course takes you past Arlington National Cemetery in the last mile. You think a marathon is loud until you run past rows of white stones, each one marking a person who gave the last full measure of devotion.

I wasn’t going to slow down. I couldn’t.

That wasn’t physiology. That wasn’t architecture. That was meaning. Meaning can carry the last mile. It cannot build the twenty before it.

I think about this with every founder I work with. They’ve all got a weekend where they ran two halves back to back.

They can point to it. The launch that nearly killed them. The fundraiser. The quarter they pulled out of the fire with a whiteboard and a suitcase. That weekend is real. It’s also the most dangerous reference point they carry.

They look at that weekend and think it’s proof they can scale. It isn’t. A weekend is a sprint. Scale is a system.

Somewhere between 40 and 80 employees, founders try to run a 26.2 at half-marathon pace. The engine that handled 15 people does not handle 60. Meetings that used to decide things now consume them. Issues that were resolved in a Slack thread now need a standup. The standup that used to surface problems now reveals five people solving the same one. The founder works harder. The output grows more slowly.

Not a motivation problem. A physiology problem. Running another heroic weekend doesn’t put fuel back in.

A 13.1 is a sprint. A 26.2 is a system. Confuse them, and you spend a decade trying to outrun the architecture you never built.

This is why I still want to BQ. Not for the medal. Because I want to know I’ve built the engine. Not just the willpower. Not just the peak fitness. The architecture that holds across 26.2.

Boston isn’t a goal. It’s a diagnostic.

The halves prove I can run the pace. I don’t know yet whether I’ve built the system.

THREE QUESTIONS FOR THE FOUNDER AT MILE 20

Before your next leadership meeting, sit with these:

  1. What’s the weekend you point to as proof you can scale? How long ago was it, and what did it prove?

  2. Where in your company are you running 26.2 at half-marathon pace? Where is the effort trying to do what architecture should?

  3. If you removed yourself from the race tomorrow, what mile would the company fail to finish?

If those questions hit a nerve, you’re not alone. Most of the founders I work with arrive at the same place around 40 to 80 employees: their effort is no longer the asset moving the company forward, and nobody has said it out loud yet.

That’s the work. Building the architecture that lets you step back without the company breaking. If you’re the founder at mile 20, reach out.
