The Door to Coaching

A Long Read: Why Independent Schools Already Have the Structure – But Not Always the Skill – To Help Students Understand Themselves

Matt Goode3 December 202522 min read Get new pieces by email

# Schools# Coaching

There’s a subtle moment that plays out dozens of times a day inside every independent school – small enough to miss, but revealing enough to matter. A student pauses in your doorway before asking a question. Another rushes to answer before the thought fully forms. Someone hovers at the edge of a group conversation, close enough to belong but far enough to avoid the vulnerability of joining in. A different student “forgets” an assignment with a look that is less defiance and more self-protection, as if they’re guarding something they don’t yet have the words to explain.

Young people rarely announce what they’re thinking. They don’t present their inner lives with clear captions or tidy labels. Instead, they speak through behaviors – small, often fleeting signals that surface long before they understand the emotion, need, or motivation underneath them.

Adults, meanwhile, do what we were trained to do: we interpret. We parse. We make meaning. Sometimes that interpretation comes from professional judgment; sometimes it’s the comfort of pattern recognition accumulated over years in the classroom. But often, if we’re honest, it’s simply the rapid story-making that allows us to navigate a complex school day and feel oriented inside it.

And this is where the essential distinction between teaching and coaching begins to appear – not as an abstract idea, but as something you can feel in real time. Teaching is an interpretive craft, grounded in experience, informed by patterns, and shaped by the teacher’s expertise. Coaching is an invitational craft, rooted in curiosity, presence, and a willingness to sit with the moment before naming it. Both share the same destination – helping a young person grow – but they begin their journeys from very different starting points.

Over two decades as a teacher, advisor, and middle school dean, I learned that the most meaningful growth rarely begins in grand epiphanies or dramatic conversations. It begins in these tiny, easily overlooked moments – moments that act as the front door to self-understanding. Suppose we want students to walk through that door with confidence and agency. In that case, we need to learn how to meet them at the threshold with a different kind of presence: one that listens first, interprets later, and resists the instinct to rush toward meaning before the student has a chance to discover it themselves.

Teaching Lives in Patterns. Coaching Lives in Moments.

One of the core ideas I introduced during a recent Train-the-Trainer session at an independent school was deceptively simple. Yet, it shifted the room the moment it landed:

Teaching answers the what.
Coaching uncovers the why.

Teaching is built on patterns – on what happens over time, what the evidence suggests, and how a student tends to behave across units, seasons, and years. These patterns become the architecture teachers depend on. They help us plan instruction, anticipate needs, and create a sense of coherence in a profession full of moving parts. Patterns allow teachers to stay grounded in their expertise.

Coaching, however, begins in a different place entirely. It begins inside the immediacy of a single moment, before any interpretation has formed. Coaching invites us to work with what actually happened – the simple, observable behavior – without rushing to connect it to a broader narrative.

When I teach this distinction, I often offer a metaphor:

Behaviors are the door.
Patterns are the room.

Teachers, by instinct and training, tend to walk directly into the room. They look for the throughline, the recurring signal, the larger shape the behavior seems to form. This isn’t a flaw – it’s a professional strength. Teachers organize enormous complexity by making sense of patterns, and those patterns often provide the clarity needed to teach well.

But coaching asks something different. It asks us to pause at the door.

And this pause – brief, almost invisible from the outside – is where I watched some faculty struggle most. Not because they resisted the idea, but because interpretation wasn’t just something they did; it was part of who they were. Their sense of professional competence was often tied to their ability to correctly read a situation, anticipate a need, or discern what a student “really meant.” For many, letting go of the instinct to interpret felt like letting go of a part of their identity.

This surprised some of the faculty who consider themselves constructivist teachers – educators who pride themselves on facilitating conversations, drawing out student voices, and helping learners construct their own meaning. In academic spaces, stepping back from interpretation felt natural to them. But when faced with a student’s behavior, the instinct to interpret surfaced instantly. They could easily let go of control over the content of a discussion; releasing control over the meaning of a student’s behavior felt far more personal. It revealed how deeply intertwined interpretation and teaching identity can be, even for those who consider themselves student-centered.

And I should say clearly: this wasn’t just their challenge. It has always been mine as well. With Strategic as one of my top CliftonStrengths, my mind is a pattern-making machine. Give me a handful of data points – an expression, a posture, a shift in tone – and a storyline forms almost automatically. That instinct has served me well in so many parts of my work: designing systems, reading group dynamics, anticipating needs, and coaching leaders. But in these moments with students, that same instinct requires deliberate discipline. I have to notice the pattern without imposing it. I have to keep the interpretation internal long enough for the student to reveal their own meaning first. Coaching begins when I resist the urge to articulate the insight I already see and instead make space for the insight they are trying to reach.

This is why the distinction between teaching and coaching, while intellectually simple, feels so emotionally significant in practice. It asks educators to temporarily suspend the lens they’ve learned to trust. It requires trading certainty for curiosity and expertise for presence – not because the adult’s interpretation is wrong, but because the student has to come first.

That slight pause at the door may look subtle on paper. But in a real conversation, with a real young person trying to understand themselves, it becomes transformational. It marks the moment when adults stop shaping the narrative and begin making room for students to shape it themselves.

The Independent School Advantage: Advisory as a Coaching Engine

If independent schools have a structural superpower, it isn’t class size or resources or facilities – though those certainly matter. The real differentiator is something quieter and far more human: advisory.

Advisory is the one place in a school where the long arc of a student’s development is held with consistency. It’s where an adult has permission to see a student as a whole person, not just a learner, an athlete, a performer, or a GPA. It’s the space where the micro-signals of adolescence – those small hesitations, shifts, bursts of courage, or moments of retreat – actually have room to be noticed, explored, and made sense of.

In other words: Advisory is a coaching architecture disguised as a schedule block.

A strong advisory doesn’t function as a homeroom or a morning check-in. It’s the nexus between school and self. Advisors occupy a rare position in the ecosystem of a student’s life – able to see both the daily pressures of performance and the broader trajectory of who that student is becoming. They straddle the line between the immediate and the developmental.

And because of that, they can ask the kinds of questions classroom teaching rarely makes space for:

  • “I noticed you hesitated before speaking today. What was happening there?”

  • “I saw how the group was drifting and how you pulled them back – how did that feel for you?”

  • “You asked a lot of questions in lab. What were you trying to figure out?”

These are not leading questions.
They’re not veiled judgments.
They’re invitations – gentle openings into self-understanding.

And invitations, more than content, technique, or “best practices,” are the true currency of coaching.

The irony, of course, is that independent schools already have the structural advantage. They have the advisory model. They have the scheduling commitment. They have adults who know students deeply, sometimes over multiple years. What they often lack is the shared coaching skill set required to unlock the potential of that structure.

Without that skill set – without a shared language for noticing, naming, and exploring behavior – advisory can become something far less powerful: a glorified study hall, a logistics hub, a place to check forms and deadlines. The architecture remains, but the purpose evaporates.

This was one of the most striking insights that emerged while working with the faculty of this particular school. They wanted to coach. They cared about their advisees. They believed in the model. But no one had ever formally taught them the mechanics of coaching – how to listen beneath the surface, how to stay with the behavior instead of leaping to the story, how to create psychological safety without diluting accountability, and how to help students see themselves more clearly.

Independent schools don’t need to reinvent themselves to become coaching-centered institutions. They simply need to activate the latent potential of the system they already built.

And advisory is the doorway.

The Hardest Part for Faculty: Holding Back the Meaning

When I led the recent Train-the-Trainer workshops – built around a simple two-minute coaching move (Notice → Explore → Aim) – I watched something unfold that I’ve seen many times before: even the most experienced, thoughtful teachers struggled with one deceptively small move.

They couldn’t help assigning meaning to a student’s behavior. Not immediately. Not even for a breath.

And it wasn’t because they were judgmental. It was because they were good.

Teaching, at its core, is an art of interpretation. Over years in the classroom, teachers become exquisitely skilled at reading patterns, noticing what’s typical or atypical for a student, and connecting dots that others might miss. This is what allows a teacher to sense a shift in the room before anyone speaks, or to pick up on the early signs that a student is carrying something heavy.

That pattern-making instinct is part of what makes teachers effective.

But in coaching, the very strength that anchors good teaching can get in the way.

During our sessions, I watched as teachers observed a behavior – a student blurting out an answer, sitting on the edge of a group, asking a long string of questions – and their minds immediately leapt to interpretation:

  • “She’s impulsive.”

  • “He’s withdrawing.”

  • “They’re anxious.”

  • “Something’s wrong.”

These interpretations were never unkind. In fact, many came from a place of deep care. But the moment an adult names the meaning, the student loses the chance to develop it for themselves. And that is the moment where agency is either expanded or quietly taken off the table.

What coaching demands – what makes it so deceptively difficult – is the discipline of lingering with the observable before leaping to the interpretive. It asks adults to hold back the story they could write and wait long enough for the student to begin writing their own.

In role-play, the shift was always subtle but dramatic. Instead of a teacher saying:

“Why are you always disengaged lately?”
(which is really a conclusion disguised as a question)

They practiced saying:

“I noticed you stayed quiet in group work today. What was going on for you?”
(which holds open the possibility that five different truths might be living inside that one behavior)

Every time, the emotional temperature in the room changed. Students – played by colleagues – relaxed into the conversation. There was a sense of being seen rather than categorized. The dialogue slowed, widened, softened. And what emerged was almost always more accurate and far more human than the adult’s initial interpretation.

Students will usually acknowledge what happened – the behavior – because the behavior is real and observable. But they often resist what it means, especially when the meaning is supplied by someone else. Coaching honors that distinction. It gives young people the space to explore the gap between what they did and why they did it.

And that is where growth begins.

For many teachers – especially those who pride themselves on being constructivist, student-centered, or discussion-driven – this was the most surprising realization. Even educators who are masters at helping students build their own academic meaning can struggle to suspend their interpretations in interpersonal contexts. It is one thing to facilitate meaning-making around a poem or a lab experiment; it is another to pause our interpretations about a young person long enough for them to articulate their own inner world.

That pause is not small. It is the hinge on which agency turns. Coaching isn’t about withholding expertise; it’s about redirecting it. Instead of generating the narrative ourselves, we create the conditions for students to generate it with us – and, eventually, without us.

When adults separate behavior from meaning, students step forward. They take ownership. They become authors of their own story rather than characters in someone else’s interpretation.

And that shift – the intentional slowing down of the adult mind – is what transforms a moment into a catalyst for growth.

Why “Just the Behavior” Is So Hard for Adults to See

One of the most revealing moments in every faculty workshop comes when we try a deceptively simple exercise: describing only the behavior we observed, without adding interpretation. The room shifts almost immediately. Teachers lean back, eyebrows rise, and you can feel a quiet ripple of discomfort – not because anyone feels defensive, but because this small discipline exposes something fundamental about how educators make sense of students. For most teachers, very few behaviors ever arrive as neutral data points. They come pre-loaded with meaning.

Consider something as ordinary as classroom participation.

When a student speaks up frequently, many teachers instinctively register engagement, preparation, or intellectual curiosity. But that same behavior might also be carrying something more complicated: a student who answers quickly to avoid uncertainty, who fills the silence because stillness feels unsafe, or who unintentionally occupies the space their classmates need to step forward. The behavior is identical; the meanings behind it diverge entirely.

The reverse is just as nuanced.

One teacher sees a quiet student and assumes a lack of confidence. Another imagines withdrawal. A third sees deep processing and patience. And now and then, the student themselves reveals something altogether different – something the adults never considered – because the interpretation simply didn’t match their internal reality.

This is the core reason coaching invites us back to the most elemental layer of the interaction: the observable moment. Not the narrative we’ve built around it. Not the label we’ve used before. Not the months of patterns that shape what we think we know about a student. Just the behavior.

“I noticed you contributed several times today, especially early in the discussion.”
– or –
“I noticed you didn’t jump in today, even though the conversation moved into an area you usually care about.”

For many educators – especially those with years of classroom experience – this feels oddly counterintuitive. Their professional identity rests on interpretation. That instinct is earned and essential. It allows them to anticipate a student’s needs before those needs are spoken aloud, and it’s one of the great strengths of the teaching craft.

And here’s an important nuance: school counselors often find this shift a bit more natural – not because they are “better” at it, but because their training gives them more repetitions in this particular muscle. Counselors, especially those with clinical experience, have practiced staying with the observable moment, resisting the urge to collapse ambiguity into a single story, and holding open multiple truths long enough for a student to find the one that feels authentic. They’ve simply had more “reps in the gym,” as it were, in the discipline of neutrality.

For teachers, however, interpretation is not just a habit; it’s a professional reflex. In the classroom, that reflex is an asset – it’s what allows teachers to keep a lesson on track, sense confusion before it surfaces, and anticipate the needs of twenty-five different learners at once. But in coaching, that same instinct can quickly become a barrier. The moment we tell a student what their behavior means, we compress every possibility into a single adult-authored narrative – one the student might disagree with, might not be ready to explore, or might never have considered.

And there’s another layer here that matters: Students can get defensive.

Even the gentlest interpretation from an adult can feel, to an adolescent, like an indictment – a suggestion that something is wrong with them, or that their motives have been misunderstood. Young people are already navigating a swirl of internal uncertainty and external expectations. They are exquisitely sensitive to being mislabeled, misread, or prematurely defined. When an adult steps in too quickly with meaning, students often brace themselves. They shut down. They withdraw. Or, just as frequently, they adopt the adult’s interpretation at the cost of their own, because it feels too risky to contradict someone in a position of authority.

This is why the coaching pause matters so deeply. Staying with the behavior rather than jumping to meaning gives students room to locate their truth before they are pressured – explicitly or implicitly – to accept ours.

When we separate behavior from meaning, we create a different kind of space altogether – one where students can voice their inner world without feeling corrected or misunderstood. It’s often surprising what emerges. The student who “talks too much” may be protecting themselves from doubt. The quiet one might be drafting the perfect sentence in their head. Participation can be rooted in confidence or fear; silence can signal avoidance or courage. The surface behavior is constant, but its underlying causes are anything but.

The point is not that teachers misinterpret students. It’s that every behavior contains multiple potential truths, and coaching begins when we hold those truths open long enough for the student to step into their own meaning.

That is why “just the behavior” feels challenging. It asks adults to pause the very skill that makes them exceptional educators: rapid meaning-making. Yet in that brief pause – in that moment of spacious neutrality – we offer something more potent than insight.

We offer agency.
We offer choice.
We offer a different kind of dignity.

We give students back the pen.

The 2-Minute Coaching Move: A Simple Structure for Complex Moments

After all the reframing, all the conversation about neutrality, curiosity, and the separation of behavior from meaning, the human question inevitably emerges: “Okay… but what do I actually say?”

This is the moment every faculty member leans forward. Because philosophy is interesting, but practice is what changes a school. And while coaching isn’t something that can be reduced to a script, it can be supported by a structure – a pattern teachers can carry with them into any advisory meeting, hallway conversation, or quick check-in before class.

The model I teach is deliberately simple. It’s fast. It’s portable. And it honors the student’s agency at every step.

It has three moves:

1. NOTICE – What happened?

A single, neutral observation grounded in the visible moment.

Not the story.
Not the interpretation.
Not the “why” you’ve already inferred.

Just the behavior.

  • “I noticed you asked a lot of questions during the lab today.”

  • “I noticed you jumped in early during the discussion.”

  • “I noticed you stayed quiet during group work.”

A true NOTICE statement is almost boring in its clarity – which is exactly why it works. It creates a doorway without pushing the student through it. There is no accusation to defend, no assumption to correct.

2. EXPLORE – What did it mean?

A single curious question that invites the student into their own thinking.

This is where most adult instincts flare up – we want to help, to interpret, to solve. But coaching asks us to trust the student enough to let them make meaning before we do.

  • “What were you trying to figure out?”

  • “What was happening for you in that moment?”

  • “What made that feel like the right move?”

The EXPLORE question is the heart of the work. It nudges the student to slow down, look inward, and articulate something they may never have given language to before. And when students hear themselves explain their reasoning – sometimes for the first time – they begin building the muscle of self-awareness that advisory is designed to cultivate.

3. AIM – What will you try next?

A small, doable next step the student chooses.

Not the teacher’s plan.
Not a correction.
Not a fix.

Just the next right move from their perspective.

  • “What’s one thing you might try next time?”

  • “What could help you get clarity earlier?”

  • “What might make participation feel easier next time?”

The AIM is where insight becomes intention. It takes a moment of awareness and shapes it into a future-oriented choice – one that the student owns. This final step reinforces a truth that adolescents rarely experience in a school day: Their behavior can be understood, and their next move can be chosen.

That is agency.
That is belonging.
That is wellness.

And inside independent schools – especially those with advisory programs rooted in whole-student development – this simple three-step arc becomes a powerful daily practice.

The Work Ahead: Helping Students Make Sense of Themselves

What stayed with me long after the workshop ended wasn’t the difficulty of the coaching work. It was the willingness I saw in so many teachers. You could feel the collective desire to see students more clearly, to release some of the assumptions that had calcified over the years, to shift from “What’s wrong with this student?” to “What might this student be trying to tell me?” The energy in the room was full of that quiet, hopeful charge that appears when adults genuinely want to grow.

But it would be incomplete to suggest that everyone stepped into the work with the same ease.

Just as students occupy different emotional and cognitive zones when they encounter something new, teachers do too. Some entered the experience already in a learning posture – curious, open, ready to stretch a well-worn professional identity in a new direction. Others were standing right at the edge of discomfort, not because they lacked care or ability, but because the work touched something foundational: the instinct to interpret, the reflex that has served as their North Star in the classroom for years.

And a few were still in that early phase where uncertainty feels sharper than possibility, where the unfamiliar creates hesitation. That is human. That is normal. And in many ways, that variation is precisely what made the workshop feel real. Growth rarely arrives in perfect synchrony.

What mattered was not uniform readiness, but the movement – the small shifts in language, the tentative but sincere attempts to stay neutral, the moments where someone paused just half a second longer before interpreting. Those gestures were meaningful. They revealed a faculty willing to stretch, even when it felt unfamiliar or exposed.

And that shift – quiet, incremental, deeply human – is precisely what young people need from the adults around them.

Adolescence is, in many ways, a long stretch of living at the edges of comfort. Students spend years negotiating who they are and who they are expected to be, often without the language to articulate the tension. Coaching helps them build that language. It offers a way to explore behavior without shame, to reflect without defensiveness, and to move forward with a sense of ownership.

When an adult notices a behavior, resists the urge to interpret, and asks a clean, curious question, they are teaching two lifelong skills:

  • How to reflect without shame.

  • How to choose what comes next with intention.

The coaching arc ends with that final move – AIM – a tiny, student-chosen next step. Not a fix, not a verdict, not an adult-driven solution. Just a small, future-facing action that belongs entirely to the student.

In that moment, something shifts. The student becomes the author again.

And that is the heart of coaching. It is also the heart of education itself: helping young people understand themselves well enough to navigate complexity with clarity and agency.

A Personal Reflection: Where I Actually Learned to Coach

I’ve often thought about where these skills came from for me – not the frameworks or the theory, but the instinct. The ability to pause at the threshold of a moment, stay with the behavior, and help a student make meaning without imposing my own.

And the truth is straightforward:

I never once received professional development on how to be a stronger advisor. Not once in two decades.

I was trained to teach. I was trained to design curriculum, manage a classroom, differentiate instruction, write comments, communicate with families, navigate conferences, and hold high academic standards. I was trained in everything except the part of the job that might matter most in an independent school: helping young people understand themselves.

No one ever sat me down and said, “Here’s how you support a student through the messy, contradictory, beautiful confusion of growing up.”

Whatever I learned about advising came not from manuals or PD days, but from the dual vantage point I held for years as both a teacher and a dean. Those roles forced me to see students across contexts – how the same child could appear bold in English, tentative in math, joyful in advisory, and overwhelmed in the hallway. When you sit at that intersection long enough, you begin to understand that behavior is rarely what it seems on the surface.

And truthfully, most of the coaching I did in those years didn’t happen in advisory meetings at all. It happened in the in-between spaces: the walk from class to class, the restorative conversation after a conflict, the difficult moment when a student’s behavior collided with a community norm. Those moments could easily be mislabeled as “discipline,” but the work was never really about enforcing rules.

It was about meaning. It was about helping a student understand the impulse behind a choice, the emotion underneath a reaction, the story they were starting to tell themselves. It was about guiding them toward reflection without shame and forward movement without pressure.

And I was fortunate – deeply fortunate – to have mentors who modeled what this could look like when done well. They taught me, often without naming it, how to bring curiosity and steadiness into a moment that felt charged. They coached me as I learned to coach students. Looking back, I realize how rare that kind of apprenticeship truly is.

Which raises a larger, more structural question:

If teachers are being asked to coach their advisees toward reflection, agency, and growth using the tools of teaching instead of the posture of coaching… how could they not feel frustrated?

Teaching and coaching share the same destination. But they begin from entirely different starting points.

Teaching begins with clarity, expertise, and a planned direction – an understanding of where you’re trying to take the student. Coaching begins with curiosity, neutrality, and a willingness to discover where the student needs to go.

When we expect teachers to coach without equipping them, we are essentially handing them a compass and withholding the map. Their desire is not the issue. Their preparation is.

And that’s why I’m writing this section as more than a reflection. It’s also an invitation.

Independent schools are filled with extraordinary educators who care deeply about the young people in their care. They want to guide students toward self-awareness, confidence, and agency. They want advisory to matter – not as an add-on, not as a compliance block, but as a genuine engine of whole-student development.

What they often lack is not commitment.
It’s training.
It’s a shared language.
It’s a structure that helps them shift from meaning-making to meaning-inviting.

And I know, from experience, how transformative that shift can be.

So to independent school heads, deans, wellness directors, and administrators:
If you’re looking to strengthen your advisory program, equip faculty with fundamental coaching tools, integrate CliftonStrengths into a whole-school wellness model, or build a culture where students feel genuinely known, I would LOVE to help.

This is the part of my work I feel most called to give back to. I’ve lived it from multiple angles. I’ve designed these frameworks carefully and coached adults through them. I’ve seen how even one slight shift in faculty practice can ripple into a student’s entire sense of themselves.

And if your school is ready to take this next step, it would be an honor to lend my experience, my training, and my voice to support that work.

The Door and the Room

Teaching and coaching are not opposites. They are two movements of the same essential work: guiding a young person from where they are toward who they are becoming. They share a destination, but they travel different paths to get there.

Teaching lives in the room – where patterns unfold, where growth is traced over time, where instruction and feedback shape a student’s long-term development. It is the space of planning, expertise, and intentional direction.

Coaching lives at the door – at the threshold of a single moment, where behavior becomes insight and insight becomes agency. It is the space of curiosity, neutrality, and discovery. It asks us to notice what happened, stay with the moment, and let the student’s voice lead us forward.

Independent schools already have the infrastructure for both. Advisory, small communities, relational teaching, and wellness-centered programming create a natural ecosystem for this dual identity. What schools often lack is a shared skill set that helps every adult move fluidly between the two modes – knowing when a situation calls for clarity and direction, and when it calls for presence and inquiry.

That is the work I’ve dedicated much of my career to building.
Not because teachers don’t care – they care deeply.
Not because they don’t want to coach – they often desperately want to.
But because no one ever taught them how.

And the truth is simple: The moments we notice shape the people our students become.

When we start with the behavior, not the story –
when we stay curious rather than certain –
when we invite students to articulate their own meaning –
we give them something far more important than advice.

We give them the space, the language, and the confidence to understand themselves.

And that is the purpose of advisory.
It is the purpose of coaching.
It is, at its core, the purpose of education.
