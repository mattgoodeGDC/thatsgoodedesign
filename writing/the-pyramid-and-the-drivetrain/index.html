The Pyramid and the Drivetrain

Lencioni mapped the downward spiral. Here’s how to build the upward one.

Matt Goode15 September 20265 min read Get new pieces by email

# The culture is drifting# CREATE

I was in a colleague’s office recently and noticed The Five Dysfunctions of a Team on his bookshelf. It had the soft spine of a book that’s been read more than once.

Seeing it brought back a question I worked through while writing CLUTCH. If Lencioni gave us such a clear map of how teams break, why do so many teams that have read the book stay broken?

Patrick Lencioni’s 2002 fable has probably been handed to more new managers than any leadership book of the last twenty-five years. It endures because it names something every team has felt: teams rarely fail because of a lack of talent. They fail from a chain of avoidance, one step at a time.

My answer is that the pyramid tells you what is going wrong. It doesn’t tell you what to build to stop it.

The cascade, briefly

Without trust, teams avoid conflict and settle into artificial harmony. Without honest conflict, nobody truly commits. Without commitment, nobody holds peers accountable. Without accountability, individual status beats team results. The fable’s fix is mostly about behavior: the leader goes first, shows vulnerability, draws out conflict, and holds the standard. Lencioni’s later work, especially The Advantage, moves toward structure, but the fable is the book on the shelves.

That’s where CLUTCH picks up.

Where the two books meet

CLUTCH argues that leadership bottlenecks are design problems, not effort or character problems. When a team keeps stalling in the same place, it’s usually missing something that would make the right behavior easy. Read that way, each level of the pyramid points to a part of the drivetrain that is missing or slipping.

  • Trust erodes when ownership is unclear. It rarely goes in one dramatic betrayal. When nobody knows who owns what, people protect themselves, and self-protection is the opposite of vulnerability. Asking a team to be more open while leaving ownership vague is asking them to take a risk the structure keeps punishing. In CLUTCH, this sits in the engine: trust is an outcome you design for, not a feeling you hope for.

  • Conflict is where behavior still does the most work. Most teams that avoid conflict aren’t cowardly. They’ve learned that disagreement costs more than it’s worth, because the person with the most power reacts badly to it. The fix is to engineer safety for dissent: make it easy for someone to say “I’m not sure that’s right,” then thank them, whether or not you act on it. If no one has pushed back on you in the last month, it isn’t because there’s nothing to push back on. Quiet rooms aren’t aligned rooms. This is why CLUTCH treats emotional intelligence as the clutch, the part that lets a team change gears without grinding them. It’s the one level where design can’t fully replace the leader.

  • Commitment dies after the debate, not during it. I cite Lencioni by name in the 6C chapter because he traces lack of commitment to unresolved conflict and unclear decision rights. The second half of that gets less attention than it should. People leave the room unsure whether they were consulted, whether they had a vote, or whether the call was made before the meeting started.

The 6C Decision Compass closes that gap by naming the mode before the discussion: Command, Consultative, Collaborative, Consensus, Core Expert, or Choice. Someone overruled in a clearly named Consultative decision can still commit, because the process did what it said it would. Someone overruled in what they thought was a Consensus decision checks out quietly. Same decision, different outcome. The only difference was clarity about how it would be made.

  • Accountability needs a License, not just a Title. Lencioni rightly argues that the strongest accountability runs between peers. But peers can’t hold each other to work nobody owns, and most organizations have work that's assigned but not handed over. A Title is the name on the task. A License is the authority to make decisions about it: the budget, the scope, the right to say no. When someone has the Title without the License, accountability becomes theater. They can be blamed, but they can’t act. Their peers sense this and hold back, and they’re right to. The DELEGATE framework transfers both.

  • Results follow what the system rewards. When one heroic person keeps saving the quarter, the team learns that results belong to the hero. When wins count as team wins, the output feeds back into the engine as trust. That’s the flywheel: what the system produces becomes what it runs on.

The reverse cascade

Trust enables candor. Candor sharpens decisions. Decisions build accountability. Accountability generates results. Lencioni showed the downward spiral. Design builds the upward one.

The cascade runs in both directions. Every clean handoff reinforces trust. Every clearly named decision makes the next disagreement safer.

Where they differ

The two books aren’t saying the same thing, and pretending otherwise flattens both.

In the fable, Kathryn turns her team around by modeling vulnerability, holding the line, and refusing to let artificial harmony win. That’s real. But a fix that depends on the leader’s courage in every meeting has a ceiling. It works while that leader is in the room and has the energy. CLUTCH is written for the moment that stops working: the founder at 60 people who can’t be in every conversation, or the head of operations holding the culture together alone.

So the question I’d add to Lencioni’s is this. You’ve modeled the behavior. What have you built so it survives when you’re not there?

If the book is on your shelf too

Take down your copy and pick the level of the pyramid where your team stalls most often. Then, instead of asking who needs to behave differently, ask what is missing from the design.

Is ownership unclear enough that vulnerability feels risky? Are decisions made without anyone naming how they’ll be made? Are people carrying Titles without Licenses?

The dysfunction is the symptom. The design is the fix. That’s the difference between a team that has read the book and a team that no longer needs it.

Put CLUTCH next to Lencioni on the shelf.
