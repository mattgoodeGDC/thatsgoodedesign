Your Best Move Is Killing Your Team

The Zidane Problem: When Your Greatest Strength Becomes Your Team's Biggest Cost

Matt Goode10 February 20265 min read Get new pieces by email

# Everything runs through me

Zinedine Zidane was the greatest player on the planet. The 2006 World Cup Final was his last match. He’d already scored a stunning Panenka penalty to put France ahead. He’d carried the entire tournament – goals against Spain, Portugal, Brazil. Ten minutes from the trophy.

Dear Reader, if you don’t know what a “Panenka” is, please Google it.

Then Materazzi said something about his sister. And the same fire that made Zidane unstoppable – the intensity, the pride, the refusal to be diminished – put his forehead into Materazzi’s chest on the biggest stage in sports.

Red card. France lost the shootout without him. Italy lifted the trophy.

The quality that made him extraordinary is the same one that destroyed the moment he’d spent his entire career building toward. Not a different Zidane. The same one. That’s what makes it tragic.

Nobody told him to stop being intense. Intensity was the whole point. The problem wasn’t the strength. It was the context. What once set him apart became the thing that took him out – and his team paid the price.

Leadership works the same way.

The thing that got you promoted – the decisiveness, the high standards, the open door, the ability to see the big picture – didn’t stop being valuable. But somewhere along the way, it shifted from an asset to a default. And defaults, left unchecked, create shadow effects that are invisible to the user.

Here’s what I mean.

The “high standards” leader who built her reputation on excellence. She’s the reason the work is sharp, the brand is trusted, and the team takes pride in output. However, over time, those standards have calcified into something more rigid. Feedback sessions feel like audits. People stop bringing unfinished ideas because the cost of imperfection is too high. The creative risk-taking that drove early wins has quietly disappeared–replaced by work that meets the bar but never exceeds it. Her strength didn’t change. Her team’s relationship to it did.

The “always available” leader who built his reputation on accessibility. He’s the first person people call. His door is never closed. He prides himself on being responsive, approachable, and present. But his availability has become a dependency. The team routes every decision through him – not because they can’t decide, but because they’ve learned that he’ll always be there to weigh in. His responsiveness trained them to defer to his judgment. He’s not developing leaders. He’s developing a line outside his office.

The “decisive” leader who built her reputation on speed. When others deliberated, she moved. When ambiguity paralyzed the room, she cut through it. But her speed has a cost she doesn’t see: no one else’s judgment is ever tested. The team has learned to wait – not out of laziness, but out of logic. Why invest in forming a position when she’ll have one in thirty seconds? Her decisiveness created clarity. It created passivity.

Three strengths. Three leaders who, from the inside, feel like they’re doing exactly what made them successful. Three teams that are quietly paying the price.

Every leadership strength has two expressions. There’s the version that elevates – where your standards sharpen the work, your availability builds trust, your decisiveness creates momentum. And there’s the version that distorts – where those same qualities, overused or applied without awareness, start producing the opposite of what you intended.

The critical insight: the two versions aren’t different behaviors. They’re the same behavior in different doses.

You don’t cross from one to the other by doing something new. You cross by doing what you’ve always done – just a little too long, a little too often, or in a context where it no longer fits.

And here’s what makes this hard to catch: from the inside, the distorted version feels identical to the elevated one. You’re still being decisive. You’re still holding high standards. You’re still available. The behavior hasn’t changed. But the impact has.

The gap between your intent and your team’s experience – that’s where the damage accumulates. Silently. Gradually. Until someone leaves, or stops trying, or tells you something in an exit interview that you wish you’d heard two years earlier.

This isn’t about becoming less of who you are. It’s about developing the awareness to notice when your signature strength has crossed a line you can’t see from where you’re standing.

The leaders who navigate this well don’t abandon their strengths. They develop a range around them. The high-standards leader learns when to celebrate progress, not just critique gaps. The available leader learns when not to answer the call – so someone else must. The decisive leader learns when to hold the silence long enough for another voice to fill it.

The strength stays. The rigidity goes.

Five strengths and their shadows. Find yours.

  1. Decisiveness on the balcony is speed, clarity, and momentum. In the basement, nobody else’s judgment ever develops – because yours is always faster.

  2. High Standards built your reputation. On the balcony, the work is sharp, the brand is trusted, and people take pride in output. In the basement, perfectionism has quietly killed every creative risk your team might have taken.

  3. Availability feels generous – and on the balcony, it is. Trust, responsiveness, and an open door. But in the basement, that open door has become a dependency. Your team can’t function without you in the room.

  4. Vision from the balcony provides people with meaning, direction, and strategic clarity. In the basement, it disconnects from execution. People are moved but confused – inspired with nowhere to go.

  5. Warmth creates safety, belonging, and loyalty. That’s the balcony. The basement version looks almost identical – except hard truths go unspoken, and conflict gets absorbed instead of resolved.

The balcony is what got you here. The basement might be what’s keeping your team from reaching its goals.

The question isn’t whether your strength has a shadow. It does. The question is whether you’ve noticed–or whether your team is bearing the cost of something you can’t see.
