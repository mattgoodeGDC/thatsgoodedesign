They Educated It Out

Matt Goode4 April 202611 min read Get new pieces by email

# Schools# Neuroinclusion

Everything this week argued for formation – the capacity built into how a person sees, not installed by a program. Plato showed us how organizations select against the people who did that work. Sophocles showed us what breaks when systems can’t develop their leaders or deliver truth to them. Homer showed us who pays. Yesterday: Marcus, the Stop Work Order, the interior architecture that no framework can reach.

The series made a case. But it left something unnamed – the capacity underneath all five of Monday’s lessons. Something that makes them possible.

Creativity.

Not as an artistic expression. As the capacity to generate the frame before you choose between options, to see what isn’t there yet, to make something that didn’t exist before your mind encountered the problem. This is what the humanities practice that the MBA cannot teach, structurally,.

The MBA teaches optimization. Decision frameworks. Financial modeling. The rigorous discipline of choosing well between options once they’re on the table. What it assumes – what it cannot examine by structural design – is the frame itself. Someone has to generate the options before you can optimize them. Someone has to see that the current frame is a choice before you can deploy the framework. The MBA begins at step two. It has no instrument for step one.

That prior act is what the humanities practice.

Every discipline in them is a sustained exercise in making something from incomplete materials. Literature produces meaning where none has been specified. Philosophy constructs arguments from first principles rather than received authority. History reads causation in complex systems and imagines the trajectories that didn’t happen. Drama inhabits a consciousness not your own and makes it real from the inside.

These aren’t complementary skills for the analytically trained. They are the prior capacity – and once you see that, Monday’s five lessons look different.

Narrative intelligence is not a communication skill. It’s the generative act of authoring coherence before it exists – the leader constructing a frame for why the change matters, not selecting the most accurate description of one. Moral imagination is not ethical training. It’s generating the consequence before it’s visible, seeing the cost the accounting system hasn’t named yet. Tolerance for ambiguity is the creative confidence that something can be built from the unresolved – that uncertainty is survivable because you trust your own capacity to make something from it. Deep listening and systems thinking are the same act at different scales: generating hypotheses from incomplete signals and constructing the invisible architecture of cause beneath the visible behavior.

Creativity isn’t the sixth capacity. It’s the engine underneath the other five. Without it, you can perform versions of them – assertive communication substitutes for narrative intelligence, rule-following substitutes for moral imagination – but the load-bearing kind requires generation. Remove it, and what remains are performances of leadership rather than the generative act itself.

Agamemnon had Odysseus. The best strategic mind in the Greek world, running the embassy with full institutional authority, carrying an offer of staggering material compensation. Ships. Cities. Kingdoms. His own daughter in marriage. The strategic case was airtight.

He could not generate. He had the full toolkit of optimization, and not one instrument for questioning the frame. The possibility that the currency itself was wrong – that what Achilles needed existed entirely outside the institution’s accounting system – was simply not available to him. He sent more ships. He sent more treasure. He escalated the same answer and called it problem-solving.

Not a failure of intelligence. A creativity failure – the specific kind the MBA produces: a leader who can optimize anything on the table and cannot conceive of what isn’t.

Agamemnon learned to optimize inside an institution. So did every leader you’ve ever promoted. The institution was school, and school was not designed to produce generators.

The most significant thing I watched schools do in twenty years in classrooms was not teach content badly. It was teach students, with remarkable consistency and efficiency, that there was a correct answer and their job was to find it. Every rubric. Every standardized assessment. Every lesson structured around a predetermined destination was the same instruction repeated: reproduce, don’t generate. The bell. The row. The coverage schedule that moved forward regardless of whether students had developed anything other than the ability to move with it.

By the time those students arrived in my classroom – or in leadership roles – the habit was installed. They were waiting for the frame. When the problem was genuinely open, the audience real, the answer not in the back of the book, the response was anxiety rather than curiosity. Not incapacity. Twelve years of reproduction practice, become reflex.

Sir Ken Robinson spent his career naming the mechanism: schools don’t merely fail to develop creativity – they actively educate it out. If you’ve never seen that famous TED talk, do yourself a favor: devote 20 minutes to watching this. You cannot build the habit of generating original thought in a system that consistently rewards reproducing the expected answer. The two practices are in direct competition, and school runs that competition for twelve years in one direction.

VC investor Ted Dintersmith drove to all fifty states in a single school year and found the diagnosis confirmed. He also found teachers who had refused the logic – not with resources, but with a different question. Not what should my students know but what do they need to be able to generate?

At McDonogh School in Owings Mills, Jennifer Smith (Read her work here) or founded the Middle School Academy as a structural answer to that question: an immersive term, separate from the regular curriculum, in which students take courses across the humanities, science, mathematics, and the arts – across grade levels, fifth graders alongside eighth graders – with no age-conferred expertise and no predetermined authority structure. When you can’t rely on age to determine who leads, you have to generate something else: shared frameworks, negotiated expertise, working relationships built from scratch under genuine uncertainty. That is the creative act at the organizational level. The Showcase at the end is where it becomes visible.

A standard assessment tests reproduction. The student demonstrates mastery of the material the teacher delivered, against a standard specified in advance, for an evaluator who already knows the answer. The student’s job is to prove they found it.

The Showcase has none of those features. The prompt was open. The work required generation – the student had to make something that didn’t exist before the Academy began. Originality can’t be pre-scored, so the standard isn’t fully specified. The audience is real – not an evaluator with an answer key but people genuinely encountering the work for the first time, forming their own responses, asking questions the student didn’t script. The frame – what the work is, why it matters, what it means – has to be authored by the presenter in real time, for an audience whose reactions can’t be predicted.

What gets revealed in that gap is whether the generative capacity is structurally available. You can coach eye contact and slide design. You cannot coach a student into having made something genuinely original if the formation that produces original thinking was never built. The student who spent twelve years in a reproduction system reaches for the nearest available frame – the internalized rubric, the presentation the audience probably wants – and when a question arrives that wasn’t scripted, the instinct is to locate the correct answer rather than think in public. Anxiety rather than curiosity. The reproduction habit, present. The student who has been through the humanities arrives already doing the thing: they have a claim – not the teacher’s claim, their claim – and when the frame gets challenged, the response is curiosity. That is what twelve years of rubrics cannot produce.

Twelve years of reproduction practice suppresses the generative reflex. The student who emerges from the factory-education model arrives at university already practiced in waiting for the frame – and the MBA meets them there, hands them the toolkit of optimization, and certifies them in it. Decision frameworks. Financial modeling. The rigorous discipline of choosing between options once they’re on the table. None of it wrong. All of it downstream of generation, and none of it asking the prior question.

By the time that person reaches a management role, the creative capacity has been depressed three times over – by school, by the business program, and by the organization’s own instinct to reward the person who arrives with the answer rather than the person who has the better question. Three institutions. One direction. No coordination required.

Tom Wujec has run the Marshmallow Challenge – build the tallest freestanding structure you can from spaghetti, tape, string, and a marshmallow on top, in eighteen minutes – with thousands of participants across industries. Kindergartners average twenty-six inches. CEOs average twenty-two. MBA graduates average ten. The gap between the CEOs and the MBAs is the telling number: both groups often have the same analytical training, but CEOs have spent years learning through iteration, recovering something the credential suppressed. The kindergartners haven’t been practiced out of the generative reflex yet – they prototype immediately, fail, adjust, and build again, the marshmallow on top from the start. MBA graduates spend the first fifteen minutes planning the optimal solution, build it once, and discover the marshmallow is heavier than the theory accounted for. It isn’t a difference in how their brains are wired. It’s a difference in what twenty years of formation practiced them to do.

Ajax is the organization’s failure – handed one frame for a decade, never asked to make another. The institution optimized him for a function and called it development.

Oedipus is the MBA’s failure – trained to resolve, to analyze, to move from problem to solution with maximum efficiency. Generation requires sitting inside the unresolved. The credential never required it.

Plato’s philosopher-king is school’s failure – the generative capacity was present at five, present at ten, quietly depressed by twelve, and by the time she reaches the promotion committee it reads as hesitation rather than depth. The organization selects the optimizer. It loses the generator.

Three institutions. Three failure modes. One root cause: the generative capacity was never built – or was systematically educated out – and replaced, at each stage, with a more refined version of the same instruction. Reproduce. Optimize. Execute. Never: generate.

The harder analytical point is not about MBA graduates specifically.

Hot take here: The educational system is not broken. It is working exactly as designed. The problem is that it was designed for an economy that peaked around 1890.

As the 19th century gave way to the 20th, public schools were redesigned around the logic of the factory floor. Students sat in rows, followed rigid schedules, progressed in batches, sorted by testing, and evaluated like quality-controlled products. School bells mimicked factory whistles. The goal was punctuality, compliance, and the efficient delivery of standardized content to large groups – because those were precisely the capacities the industrial economy required. The system produced them at scale. It still does. That is not a failure. It is a 130-year success at the wrong objective.

The MBA is not the cause of the problem. It is the logical conclusion of it – the final refinement of a pipeline that begins in kindergarten, runs through twelve years of reproduction practice, and arrives at a credential that certifies the optimizer. The business school took the raw material that the K-12 system produced and perfected it. A person trained from age five to find the correct answer, then trained from age twenty-two to choose efficiently between options, arrives in your organization precisely as the system intended: fluent, credentialed, and structurally unable to question the frame.

The marshmallow data makes this visible in eighteen minutes. But the mechanism runs for twenty-two years before anyone hands out the spaghetti.

What would a system designed for 2026 look like instead? Not a curriculum reform agenda – the design principles that distinguish the schools Dintersmith found from the ones he was expecting.

Replace coverage with depth. The factory model’s core assumption is that more content delivered equals more learning produced. It doesn’t – and in a world where AI can deliver any content on demand in seconds, the assumption doesn’t just fail, it inverts. What AI cannot do is generate the frame. The Showcase works because it demands one thing made well for a real audience rather than everything covered shallowly. The organizational equivalent: fewer initiatives pursued to actual completion, with genuine accountability for the generative quality of the work, not just its delivery on time.

Make the audience real. The single most powerful design change in every high-performing classroom Dintersmith found was this: the student’s work was seen by someone other than the teacher. A real client. A real community. A real panel. The stakes change when the audience is genuine. The organizational equivalent: ideas stress-tested by people who will be honest rather than people who are managed by the presenter.

Reward iteration, not the plan. The MBA graduates in the marshmallow challenge fail because their formation taught them to plan the optimal solution and execute it once. The kindergartners win because they were never trained out of prototyping. Organizations that hold postmortems only on failures – never on the planning process itself – are running the same logic. The design question: where in your system is iteration structurally rewarded rather than treated as evidence that the first plan was wrong?

Separate compliance metrics from formation metrics. The factory model measures what it can count: test scores, completion rates, grade-point averages. These measure reproduction reliably and formation not at all. The organizational equivalent is the performance review that measures execution against plan and calls it a leadership assessment. Formation metrics require observing what someone does when the frame isn’t provided, how they behave when they’re wrong in public, and whether the people around them generate more over time. They require the Showcase structure, not the rubric structure.

The diagnostic question for your next meeting: who in the room questions the frame before moving to solution? That’s the person who has the prior capacity – and who your organization may be selecting against without knowing it.

It’s worth noting who that person often is. The research on neurodivergent cognition – ADHD, autism, dyslexia – consistently shows elevated pattern recognition across unrelated domains, resistance to premature closure, and a tendency to question assumptions that neurotypical thinkers treat as given. These are not incidental features of atypical minds. They are, structurally, the generative capacities this essay has been arguing for. The factory model pathologized them – the student who questioned the frame instead of completing the assignment, who saw the problem differently than the rubric required, who couldn’t perform compliance on demand – and the organization inherited that pathology wholesale. The person most likely to question the frame before moving to solution is also the person most likely to have been identified as a problem somewhere between kindergarten and the MBA.

Most organizations are structured to suppress generation without knowing it. The meeting culture rewards the person who arrives with the answer. The performance review measures execution against plan. The problem that arrives pre-framed gets treated as given. Three mechanisms, one direction, running continuously – the same logic as the school, the same logic as the MBA, compressing the generative reflex one more time.

Interrupting that logic is not a culture initiative. It is an architecture decision – the same one Jennifer Smith made when she built a program whose entire structure refused the reproduction model. The same one the kindergartners execute instinctively before anyone trains it out of them.

The MBA teaches you what to do when the options are on the table.

The humanities teach you to question the table.
