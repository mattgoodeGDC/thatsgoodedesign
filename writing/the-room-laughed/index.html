The room laughed

A congressman, a diesel question, and why the honest answer has to reach the people who asked

Matt Goode16 October 20265 min read Get new pieces by email

# The culture is drifting# CREATE

“The facts are diesel prices have gone up,” the congressman said. “There’s no question about it. Why has it gone up? Because the Democrats’ environmental regulations have not...”

The room laughed before he could finish.

That was Tuesday night in Cambridge, Maryland, at a League of Women Voters forum. Rep. Andy Harris was debating his Democratic challenger, Dan Schwartz, on the administration’s deal to buy diesel from Russia. When the audience laughed, Harris told a woman she was rude. Someone else called back, “You started it.”

This was not a hostile crowd. It was his own district, one he has held since 2011 and one that leans conservative.

This isn’t an essay about who is right on fuel policy. Both parties are playing the blame game on prices this fall. What interests me is the laugh. A room in a district that leans his way heard an explanation that didn’t match what they were paying at the pump, and they said so before he could finish.

That laugh is the sound of credibility running out. I’ve heard it in boardrooms, faculty meetings, and all-hands calls. Usually it’s silent. Here is my argument: when a cost is visible to everyone, your explanation stops being a message and becomes a test. People will forgive a hard truth. They will not forgive being asked to unsee what is in front of them. Leaders who fail that test rarely lack the honest answer. They just give it to the wrong room.

What the room could see

In September, US diesel hit a record about $6.53 a gallon, nearly double the roughly $3.70 it cost a year earlier. The federal Energy Information Administration points to tight diesel markets, high crude prices and constrained oil flows from the Middle East, and international analysts add lost refining in Russia. On the Shore, people had paid that number themselves, in tractors, workboats and trucks.

Regulation may well play some part in what fuel costs. That’s a fair debate. The room wouldn’t accept a single cause, sitting on the other side of the aisle, when the government’s own analysts tie the price to a global squeeze.

People can take a hard truth: prices are up, it hurts, here’s what we’re doing about it. They won’t take an explanation that insults what they can see for themselves.

Candor to the wrong room

After the forum, Harris told reporters he thinks the Russia diesel deal is a mistake. That’s a real position, and not an easy one to take about your own party’s administration.

It’s also not what the room heard. On stage: it’s because of them. Off stage: this decision was a mistake.

Blame looks backward and outward. It finds the cause in the past and in someone else. Accountability looks forward and inward. It names a choice your own side made and says what should change.

Here’s what matters for the rest of us. The honest answer existed. He gave it the same evening. He gave it to reporters instead of the people who came to ask.

Accountability you only offer in private isn’t accountability. It’s a confession to the wrong audience.

Most leaders know this split. The all-hands gets the market, the budget, the board. The one colleague you trust gets “we got it wrong.” The team that needed the second version gets the first.

The bill shows up everywhere except where it started

Diesel is an odd kind of cost. Most people never buy it. It sits inside the price of everything that rides on a truck, from groceries to lumber. It never appears on your receipt, and you pay it on almost every line.

Low trust works the same way inside an organization. It shows up as the meeting that needed a pre-meeting, the sign-off that took a month, the strong performer who left without saying much about why. And the “it’s because of them” answer is one of the fastest ways to run up that bill, even when it’s partly true, because the people in front of you can see it isn’t the whole story.

What it costs when they stop believing you

I once worked in an organization where the head, several rungs above me, kept saying he would do something. He said it more than once. Then nothing happened, and he never followed through. I still don’t know why it wasn’t done. There was no explanation at all, good or bad, and the silence did the same work a bad one would have. After a while, you couldn’t believe him when he made any other declaration.

Your people don’t stop listening. They start translating. Every announcement gets a second meaning in the hallway afterward, and the bad news stops reaching you early.

Then the day comes when you have a hard truth that really is outside your control, and it lands with the same discount. You spent the credibility you needed on an explanation you didn’t.

Build it so blame has nowhere to land

Blame isn’t mainly a character flaw. It thrives wherever nothing in how the team runs asks for anything else. Three pieces of infrastructure change that.

  1. A standing slot for the visible bad news. Open the meeting with what everyone can already see – costs, morale, the slipped date – said plainly, before anyone has to ask. Stating the obvious earns you the right to be believed about what isn’t.

  2. Decisions with names on them. Record who decided what, and when. Once you write down the owner, “it’s because of them” has nowhere to hide, not even from you.

  3. No explanation without a next step. Every account of what went wrong ships with what happens now and when the team will hear again. Blame ends the conversation. Accountability schedules the next one.

The laugh in Cambridge made the news. The one on your team never will, because they stopped laughing out loud long ago. This week, the question worth sitting with is whether they’ve also stopped believing you.

Accountability is something you build into how a team runs, not a speech you give after things go wrong.
