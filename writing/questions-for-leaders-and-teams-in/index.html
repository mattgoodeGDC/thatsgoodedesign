Questions for Leaders and Teams in 2026

A Followup from Yesterday's Piece

Matt Goode6 January 20266 min read Get new pieces by email

# Coaching

Yesterday, I reflected on an article I read over the weekend in the HBR. If you haven’t read my post from Monday (1/5,) go back and take a look at it now.

The piece explored the architecture around leaders – the cultural scaffolding, the incentives, the systemic drift – the second half turns the lens inward again. Not to return to the old paradigm of “fix the leader,” but to acknowledge something both humbling and empowering:

Even when systems shape us, we still make choices inside those systems.

And the quality of those choices can either reinforce the drift… or interrupt it.

As I sat with the HBR article (and, honestly, with my own patterns), I found myself returning to a quieter truth beneath all the structural analysis: there are questions only leaders can answer for themselves. No strategy, no workshop, no reorganized operating model can substitute for a leader’s willingness to pause and ask, What is actually running me right now? or What version of myself am I reinforcing without realizing it?

And the same is true for teams.

Teams, like individuals, develop a typical self – shaped by habits, shortcuts, fears, and unspoken agreements. And they also have a maximal self – a version of the group that emerges in flashes: the meeting that actually moved something forward. In this project, the collaboration felt frictionless, the moment when honesty felt easier than politeness.

Part II is about returning to those truths.

It offers a set of questions for leaders to examine their own patterns, unlearn what no longer serves them, and reclaim authorship over their impact. And then it widens the frame again – inviting teams to have the kinds of conversations that shift collective identity, not just individual behavior.

If Part I made the case that leadership development must evolve as a system,
Part II is the reminder that systems change through people – one honest reflection, one reset ritual, one small commitment at a time.

Let’s begin with leaders, and then move to teams.

FOUR QUESTIONS EVERY LEADER SHOULD ASK

The article begins with a truth that lands like a mirror held at eye level: “No one operates at maximal performance all the time.”

We are invited to examine the gap between our grounded, centered maximal selves and our reactive, adaptive typical selves – shaped by what the article calls “habitual behaviors, default adaptations, and everyday patterns of interaction.” Closing that gap requires honesty, ritual, and a willingness to examine not just personality but context.

Here are the four questions that matter most.

1. What adaptations run my leadership when I’m under stress?

The article names the unconscious strategies many leaders use to stay safe: “pleasing,” “over-performing,” “self-editing.” None of these adaptations is inherently wrong. They are simply the stories you once needed to survive, though survival strategies have a half-life.

The article offers a weekly reflection: “List three moments when you felt most yourself and three when you felt hijacked.” This exercise is less about cataloging incidents and more about reclaiming authorship. Patterns emerge. Motives come into focus. Choice becomes possible again.

2. Is the system rewarding my best self – or punishing it?

One of the most important insights in the article is this: “Organizations don’t just hire leaders to change them; they hire them to preserve their sense of continuity.”

This is not cynical. It is accurate. It means your maximal self may not be the version of you the system actually rewards. Some cultures punish dissent while preaching innovation. Some elevate harmony over honesty. Some expect psychological safety for teams while offering little of it to leaders.

So ask: “What version of me gets rewarded here – and am I willing to keep reinforcing that version?”

This is not a judgment. It is clarity. And clarity, more than anything else, is what liberates.

3. Where am I performing leadership instead of practicing it?

The article warns that as leaders advance, “the risk of exposure increases,” creating an incentive to cling to expertise, polish, and control – performing leadership rather than practicing it. But maximal performance is not born of certainty. It’s born of curiosity.

The article recommends a practice I love: “Establish an ‘ignorance goal’ – a topic where you intentionally practice not knowing.” It is remarkable how much integrity and clarity surface when leaders stop performing competence long enough actually to explore.

4. What is one thing I need to unlearn this quarter?

The authors write: “Real change requires unlearning.”

Every leader has habits that once served them well – being the fixer, the diligent one, the calm one, the expert – but now constrain the range of who they can become. The quarterly ritual: identify three once-useful habits that now limit impact, then choose one to mute – not eliminate, just lower the volume.

Identity is not a static artifact. It is a living system.

The Final Question Beneath All the Questions

The article offers a quiet, almost whispered truth: “Your best self is a version of you waiting for conditions that support it.”

So the real question becomes: “What system conditions would allow me to show up as my best self more consistently – and who do I need to enlist to help create them?”

Because leadership is not simply personal work. It is ecosystem work. Cultural work. Architectural work. When leaders’ inner work meets an outer system capable of holding it, something remarkable happens: their best selves cease to be aspirational and become operational.

FOUR CONVERSATIONS EVERY TEAM SHOULD HAVE

The article reminds us that “no one operates at maximal performance all the time,” and teams, just like individuals, develop what the authors call “habitual behaviors, default adaptations, and everyday patterns of interaction.” Teams drift. Teams compensate. Teams self-protect in ways that minimize collective intelligence.

If we want leaders to show up as their best selves, we must equip teams to do the same. Here are four conversations that elevate a team’s typical self closer to its maximal one.

1. When are we at our best?

The article invites leaders to recall moments when they felt aligned with their true selves. Teams should engage in the same reflective practice. The point is not nostalgia; it is pattern recognition. When a team names the moments when it felt most like itself – aligned, energized, grounded – it begins to discern the conditions under which those moments arise.

Your best moments are not accidents. They are a blueprint.

2. What patterns pull us away from our best selves?

People slip into “pleasing, over-performing, and self-editing.” Teams slip into their collective equivalents: conflict avoidance, silence mistaken for alignment, protecting turf instead of purpose, local optimization at the expense of shared success, workarounds instead of real conversations, polite meetings followed by private frustration.

The article offers a piercing truth: “Habitual behaviors masquerade as identity.” Teams must stop confusing who they are with what they repeat. This is the moment where a team reclaims authorship.

3. Where does our system work against us – and what’s in our power to change?

HBR reminds us that identity drifts when “structures, incentives, and cultural expectations” contradict aspirations. Teams experience this constantly through fuzzy priorities, unclear decision rights, misaligned metrics, inconsistent direction, and emotional labor that remains unnamed.

When a team feels perpetually “off,” the cause is rarely the team itself. It is the system around the team. Naming this is not blame. It is agency. Teams cannot fix everything – but they can influence far more than they realize.

4. What commitments will help us close the gap this quarter?

The article lands on a truth that holds equally for teams: “Sustaining your best self is a discipline of awareness, intention, and continual reinvention.”

Teams do not transform through inspiration. They transform through rhythm – quarterly commitments, micro-experiments, tension-naming habits, clarity resets, learning rituals. Small commitments compound. Quiet, consistent practices become cultural gravity. And as teams shift, leaders shift alongside them.

Are you ready for 2026?

A leader’s best self is never only an individual achievement. It is a relational phenomenon – reflected, reinforced, or restricted by the people around them. When teams practice clarity, candor, reflection, and reinvention, they expand the space in which leaders can be grounded, courageous, and human. They become part of the architecture that sustains leadership, rather than conditions leaders must work around.

In 2026, the teams that learn to operationalize their best selves will do more than function well. They will become cultural stabilizers – the groups that hold shape when complexity hits, that absorb tension without losing coherence, that elevate leaders instead of overwhelming them.

Teams like that don’t happen accidentally. They are built – through reflection, through alignment, through ritual, and through a shared commitment to becoming the kind of system that makes everyone better.
