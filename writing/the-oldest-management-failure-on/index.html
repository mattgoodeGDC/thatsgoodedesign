The Oldest Management Failure on Record

Matt Goode2 April 202615 min read Get new pieces by email

# DELEGATE

The Iliad opens with a single word.

Menis.

Wrath. Not anger, not frustration, not professional disagreement – wrath. The specific, total, civilization-altering fury of a person who gave everything an institution asked of them and watched the institution corrupt its own accounting system for a commander’s career advancement.

Homer chose this word first. Before the ships, before the battles, before the gods. Before Troy. The first word of the first poem in the Western literary canon is a management problem.

Yesterday I closed in Sophocles with a promise: the person who pays the cost of the Iliad’s leadership failure isn’t Agamemnon or Achilles. It’s Patroclus. Today I want to make good on that – and show something slightly different from what the tragedies showed us. Not what happens when the system fails the leader. What happens when the leader fails the system. And what it costs the people underneath when they do.

The Iliad is not a war story. It is the oldest management failure on record. And it has been waiting three thousand years for someone to read it that way.

I’ve spent the last several months rereading every translation I can find – Fagles, Wilson, the older Lattimore – because I’m working on a novel that retells the Iliad from the trenches of Ypres in 1917. The Wrath of Archie Ellis (Click here) puts Homer’s conflict into the mud of the Western Front, where Agamemnon is a British colonel operating inside a command structure that made dissent not just politically costly but literally insubordinate – and where Achilles is a soldier whose capacity everyone depends on and whose value the system has no language for expressing beyond the blunt instrument of rank. The colonel is looking for his general stars. The British officer class of 1917 ran almost entirely on this logic – careers built on the performance of men whose names appear nowhere in the dispatches. Homer understood it three thousand years earlier. He just had the honesty to show us what it costs.

What it costs is everything. And it starts with a prize.

Ten years. No victory. Two kinds of power that the organization has never learned to hold simultaneously.

Agamemnon is the commander – the highest title, the most ships, the political authority that holds the coalition together. Without him, there is no army. His authority is real, structural, and genuinely necessary.

Achilles is the best fighter – the most capable, the most feared, the person whose presence on the battlefield changes the outcome of every engagement he enters. The Trojans don’t fear the Greek army. They fear Achilles. Without him, the Greeks can fight. They simply cannot win.

These two things – Agamemnon’s authority and Achilles’ capacity – are not the same thing. And for ten years, the organization has been held together by the fiction that they are. That the person who holds the title is also the person whose judgment matters most. That command and competence live in the same body.

They don’t. And the Iliad is the story of what happens when that fiction finally breaks.

A good MBA program would call this a misalignment between authority and expertise. It would give you a framework for clarifying decision rights. It would suggest a RACI matrix.

Homer gives you something else. He gives you what that misalignment actually feels like from the inside – what it costs, in human terms, when the organization has no system for holding both kinds of power simultaneously. The case study tells you what went wrong. The poem makes you feel why it was inevitable.

The flashpoint is a prize. Briseis – a captive woman awarded to Achilles as the spoils of a raid. In the Greek honor economy, prizes aren’t property. They’re recognition made visible – the organization’s accounting system for value, the external signal that tells the community what a person is worth.

But there’s a deeper current running beneath the prize. The Greek word is kleos – the glory that outlasts you, the story people tell about you after you’re gone, the thing that makes you immortal in the culture’s memory. Kleos isn’t reputation. It’s narrative. It’s the account of who did what, why it mattered, and who deserves credit for it. And Achilles is generating it at a rate that threatens to eclipse everyone around him – including his commander.

In my book, Agamemnon is a colonel looking for his general stars. And Achilles is making that impossible.

Every Greek victory is becoming an Achilles story. Every battle won is another verse in a poem that has the wrong person at its center. Agamemnon cannot let this continue – not because he is irrational, but because the career logic of his position is entirely rational. He needs to be seen as the reason the Greeks are winning. Achilles is making that story impossible to tell.

So when the plague forces Agamemnon to return his own prize, he doesn’t absorb the recognition wound. He transfers it. He takes Briseis from Achilles – not because he wants her, but because the act of taking her says something in public about who outranks whom. It is a calculated career move dressed as a command prerogative.

He is stealing kleos and calling it authority.

This is the same failure mode that destroyed Ajax in Wednesday’s essay – the organization corrupting its own account of what a person is worth – but one register deeper. Ajax’s organization simply failed to speak. Agamemnon’s actively rewrites the record. The lesson from Monday holds in both cases: narrative intelligence isn’t just a leadership capacity. It’s an organizational obligation. When the institution controls the story of who did what, the integrity of that story is infrastructure. Corrupt it, and everything built on top of it becomes unstable.

You know this person. They’re the manager who presents the team’s work as their own in the executive meeting, who takes the win publicly, and ensures that the story of how it happened has their name at the center. Homer didn’t invent this character. He recognized him. He understood that kleos theft isn’t a moral failing in the first instance. It’s a career strategy. And he understood exactly what it costs when the person being stolen from figures out what’s happened.

In Wrath, my colonel is managing up by managing the narrative around his best officer. Decorated British generals built entire careers this way – taking credit for results, suppressing the dispatches that would tell the real story, ensuring that the kleos of the men underneath them fed upward into their own record rather than outward into the historical account. The Western Front didn’t invent this dynamic. It just made the cost of it visible in the most brutal possible terms.

Achilles understands exactly what has happened. His response is not irrational – within his framework, it is completely logical. If the organization can reclassify my value at will, the accounting system is corrupt. If the accounting system is corrupt, the relationship between my contribution and my reward is a fiction. And if that relationship is fiction, I have no reason to contribute.

He withdraws. Takes his Myrmidons back to the ships. Refuses to fight.

And the Greeks start losing.

Agamemnon sends an embassy. Three men: Odysseus the diplomat, Ajax the warrior (Yes, both of those guys, see yesterday’s post), and Phoenix the old mentor – the full institutional weight of the coalition behind them. They bring offers. Ships. Treasure. Cities. Kingdoms. Agamemnon’s own daughter in marriage. The material compensation is staggering. By any rational accounting, Achilles should return.

In Wrath, this scene happens in a dugout. The embassy arrives not with ships and treasure but with a promotion, a commendation, a letter from the general. The same offer, different currency. The same result.

He refuses everything.

His speech in response to Odysseus is one of the most analytically precise passages in ancient literature – and the dimension of it that most business readers miss is how far it goes beyond the immediate dispute. Achilles doesn’t just refuse the ships. He doesn’t dispute the value of the offers. He disputes the premise. Agamemnon is trying to compensate a recognition wound with resources – settling a debt denominated in honor with currency denominated in gold. It cannot work. Not because Achilles is irrational. Because the wound and the compensation are in different currencies entirely.

And then he goes further. He questions the entire value system that made kleos worth dying for in the first place.

He says: I have two fates available to me. Stay and fight and die young with eternal glory – kleos aphthiton, imperishable fame. Or go home, live long, and be forgotten. I chose the first fate. I gave everything the system asked of me. I fought. I bled. I built the kleos this army runs on. And the system took it anyway – took it for a commander’s career, reclassified it with a single public gesture, made it mean nothing. So what exactly was I fighting for?

That is not a negotiating position. That is an existential crisis produced by an organizational betrayal. Achilles built his entire identity around the value system of the institution. The institution corrupted that value system for a colonel’s promotion prospects. And now Achilles is sitting in his tent asking the question that every disengaged high performer eventually asks, quietly, before they leave: if the accounting was always this dishonest, did any of it mean anything?

The MBA program gives you retention frameworks for this moment. Compensation benchmarking. Engagement surveys. Career pathing conversations. All of them useful. None of them sufficient. Because none of them address what Achilles is actually asking – which is not “am I paid enough” but “does this institution see me accurately and is it willing to say so in public.”

You cannot answer that question with ships. The only thing that answers it is the leader who took the kleos standing up in public and giving it back – acknowledging that the accounting was wrong, that the value is real, that the institution is willing to pay the cost of correcting its own record.

Agamemnon never offers that. He offers everything except the one thing that would work.

Most leaders would rather send more ships.

The embassy fails. The formal channels are exhausted. The organization has done everything it officially knows how to do.

And it wasn’t enough.

What happens next is the dimension of the poem most business readers miss entirely – and it is the most important leadership lesson Homer has to offer.

The cost of the conflict at the top is not paid by Agamemnon. It is not paid by Achilles. It is paid by Patroclus.

Patroclus is Achilles’ closest friend – his companion since childhood, the person who knows him better than anyone in the army. As the Greek losses mount, as the Trojans push the Greeks back toward their ships, Patroclus goes to Achilles. Not with strategy. Not with argument. With grief. He tells Achilles that men are dying. That the army is breaking. That the conflict between two leaders has created a vacuum that is filling with bodies.

Achilles cannot return himself. His withdrawal is now a position – to reverse it publicly would be its own recognition wound, an admission that Agamemnon’s authority was legitimate after all. The impasse is total. So he does something that reveals both his love for Patroclus and the catastrophic depth of his organizational failure: he sends Patroclus into battle wearing his armor.

The proxy. The person who absorbs the consequences of a conflict he didn’t create and cannot resolve.

But notice what the armor is actually doing here. Patroclus is not just standing in for Achilles – he is being asked to perform a role, carry a function, represent a capacity that isn’t his. He looks like Achilles. The Trojans see Achilles’ shield, Achilles’ helmet, Achilles’ plume. They respond to the symbol rather than the substance. And Hector kills him precisely because the gap between the appearance and the reality is eventually visible – because you can put someone in the armor, hand them the symbols of the position, give them the title and the office and the reporting lines, but you cannot give them what the armor actually represents.

Think about what this looks like inside an organization. The senior engineer who quits and the company promotes the next person in line into the role – not because they’re ready, but because the role needs to be filled and the org chart needs to make sense. The head of sales who burns out and the VP who was doing fine as an individual contributor gets handed the team because someone has to. The founder who steps back and puts a COO in place without transferring the actual authority, the relationships, and the institutional knowledge that makes the role function. The promoted person is wearing the armor. They look like the role. They aren’t the role. And the organization, under pressure, discovers the gap.

The armor kills Patroclus. The underprepared promotion – the role without the real transfer of capacity and authority behind it – kills the person who accepts it. And the organization loses both: the performer who withdrew and the proxy who was sent in their place.

I think about Patroclus constantly while writing Wrath. He is everywhere in the First World War – every junior officer sent over the top by a command structure paralyzed by its own internal politics, wearing the symbols of an authority the system never actually transferred. Every middle manager caught in the crossfire of a conflict between the CEO and the head of sales, paying with their career or their health for a wound that lives two levels above them and that they had no power to resolve.

The cost of leadership conflict is almost never paid by the leaders. It is paid by the people underneath them who had no part in creating it and no protection from its consequences.

Homer made this the emotional center of the entire poem. He knew exactly what he was showing us.

Patroclus dies because three things failed simultaneously – and understanding the sequence matters as much as understanding the failures themselves.

It starts with emotional intelligence – what the CLUTCH framework (Click here) calls the clutch itself, the mechanism that enables a leader to read a situation accurately and respond to what’s actually there rather than what’s administratively convenient. Agamemnon cannot read what is actually in front of him. He looks at Achilles and sees a threat to his authority rather than a wound that needs repair. He sends Odysseus with ships and treasure because ships and treasure are what his framework tells him value looks like – and his framework has no instrument for measuring the distance between what someone needs and what the accounting system can provide. He authorizes the wrong currency not because he is malicious but because he genuinely cannot perceive the correct one.

A leader with developed emotional intelligence reads the situation before the embassy. They feel the gap between what they’re offering and what the situation requires. They recognize that Achilles’ withdrawal isn’t about the ships – that the speech in Book 9 isn’t a negotiating position, it’s a crisis of institutional faith, and the only response that works is the one that costs the leader personally. That’s not a strategic insight. It’s an emotional one. It requires the capacity to feel the situation accurately enough to respond to what’s real rather than what’s administratively available.

Agamemnon never develops this capacity - he must have been a STEM major, I jest. The EQ fails. And that perceptual failure lands in a system with two other structural failures already in place – each one making the EQ failure worse, each one removing another possible off-ramp before Patroclus puts on the armor.

The first is what you might call the integrity of the accounting system – the structural protection that ensures the relationship between contribution and recognition is honest and cannot be manipulated by those with the most power. An organization with that protection in place has a mechanism that says: you cannot publicly reclassify someone’s contribution for your own advancement. You cannot steal kleos and call it authority. Without it, the accounting system belongs to whoever holds the most power – which means it can always be weaponized by the person at the top. Agamemnon can steal kleos in the first place because there is no structural protection for the honesty of how value is assigned. The EQ failure would have had nothing to exploit if that protection were in place.

The second is the relational infrastructure – the trust architecture that makes it possible to survive a conflict like this, to have the hard conversation before it becomes a position, to repair the rupture before it calcifies into something irreversible. An organization with a healthy relational infrastructure has a repair mechanism built underneath the formal channels. There is a relationship between the two parties that predates and outlasts the conflict. There is a trusted third party with the standing to hold both people simultaneously. There is a structure for repair, not just escalation. The Greeks have none of this. When the embassy fails, there is nothing underneath it – no relationship between Agamemnon and Achilles that could survive the wound, no trusted third party with standing, no mechanism that creates the conditions for repair before the positions harden. Because the relational infrastructure isn’t there, the EQ failure compounds rather than corrects. The wound deepens. The impasse holds.

And Patroclus puts on the armor.

This is the complete causal chain. The EQ fails – Agamemnon cannot perceive what Achilles actually needs. The accounting system has no structural protection – he can steal kleos in the first place. The relational infrastructure has no repair mechanism – the wound calcifies into a position rather than being healed. Each failure enables the next. All three are operating simultaneously, each one closing another door before the final one shuts.

That is the architecture of the Iliad’s organizational collapse. Not any single failure. The sequence.

The MBA program gives you that diagnosis accurately. Misaligned incentives. Inadequate retention strategy. Failed conflict resolution. Poor succession planning. Every box is right. Every framework is applicable.

What the MBA program cannot give you is what Homer gives you – the weight of the sequence, the feel of each door closing, the specific grief of watching Patroclus put on armor that was never meant for him, and knowing exactly how this ends and being unable to stop it. That is not sentiment. That is information of a different order – information that gets inside you and changes how you see, that makes you recognize the sequence earlier, that makes you feel the moment when the accounting system is being corrupted before the withdrawal happens, when the relational infrastructure is thin before the conflict arrives, when the EQ is failing before the embassy is sent.

The case study tells you what went wrong. The poem makes you feel why it was inevitable. And feeling it accurately is the difference between a leader who diagnoses the recognition wound before the embassy and one who sends more ships.

This week I’ve been outside – in the systems, the structures, the organizational architecture that holds culture and protects how value is assigned and develops people beyond the function they’re currently filling. Every essay has been about what you build around the leaders in your organization.

Tomorrow I want to turn inward. Because there is one system, none of those frameworks can be built for you.

I’ve been writing the Book 9 scene for Wrath – the moment when Achilles refuses to return, transposed to a dugout on the Western Front in 1917, the embassy arriving not with ships and treasure but with a promotion, a commendation, a letter from the general. Achilles’ speech in my version is a soldier telling his commanding officer exactly what the institution took from him and exactly why a promotion cannot give it back.
