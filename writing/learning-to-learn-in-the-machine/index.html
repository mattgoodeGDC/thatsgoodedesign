4. Learning to Learn in the Machine Age

Essay 4 of 7

Matt Goode6 November 202510 min read Get new pieces by email

# EQ# AI

Adaptability Is the New Intelligence

The half-life of knowledge keeps shrinking. What we mastered five years ago may already be obsolete.

In this era of automation, the advantage no longer belongs to the most informed – but to the most adaptable. That capacity to absorb, unlearn, and relearn is what leadership coach Erika Andersen calls learning agility – the meta-skill behind every other skill, the quiet engine of human relevance.

But agility isn’t merely intellectual. It’s existential.

It’s the art of staying alive to change – curious enough to explore, humble enough to revise, vulnerable enough to be seen learning again. It is the discipline of remaining a student even when you are expected to teach.

Artificial intelligence can master content. It can summarize, synthesize, and scale information far beyond human capacity. What it cannot do is wrestle with uncertainty, reframe belief, or find meaning in the space between knowing and not knowing. That’s the province of emotional intelligence – the awareness that transforms knowledge into wisdom and motion into understanding.

This has profound implications for how we design learning itself. Most corporate L&D programs, and most schools, still cling to a “canon” of knowledge – knowledge-that – as though mastery were a static state. But the future belongs to those who cultivate knowledge-how – the capacity to think, question, adapt, and connect ideas across shifting contexts.

Curricula may change, but the deeper challenge remains: we are still training people for certainty in an age that rewards awareness. We have optimized for recall when the real work is reflection. We have rewarded compliance when the future demands curiosity.

From my Educational Philosophy, something that I started at Hopkins nearly 25 years ago, but has remained largely unchanged since then.

For me, learning has never been about subjects. It has always been about life.
I aspire to know, even if through a glass darkly.
I do not lead an unexamined life.
I am the dialectic. I am a student.

And perhaps that’s the only durable learning model left – to stay unfinished on purpose.

Because in the machine age, the question isn’t how much we know. It’s whether we still have the courage to keep learning. And the quiet secret of agility is emotional intelligence: curiosity, humility, vulnerability, and the resilience to begin again.

Washington’s Hidden Superpower: Learning in Public

George Washington was not the most brilliant strategist of his age, but he was, undeniably, the most learnable. Over the course of two decades, he evolved from a rigid young officer chasing glory into a statesman capable of shaping a republic. That arc – from ambition to wisdom – remains one of the clearest portraits of learning agility in history.

We’ve seen the same pattern echoed in later leaders. Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. once said of Franklin D. Roosevelt, “A second-class intellect, but a first-class temperament.” What he meant as faint praise turned out to be the essence of Roosevelt’s genius. In moments of crisis, it wasn’t intellect that steadied the nation – it was emotional resilience. The fireside calm, the confident uncertainty, the steady voice that turned collective fear into forward motion. In a world saturated with information, it’s still emotional intelligence that moves people.

Leadership doesn’t live in the mind alone; it lives in the nervous system. It’s not about how smart you are but how grounded you can remain when everything else begins to unravel. Washington understood that long before anyone could name it. When he resigned his commission at the end of the Revolution, he did so with a single, unadorned line: “Having now finished the work assigned me, I retire from the great theatre of action.”

That restraint – the willingness to let go when he could have held on – is what learning agility looks like in motion. It is the quiet pivot from command to consciousness, from controlling outcomes to designing systems that endure beyond the individual. Hamilton captured it best when he reflected, “If I say goodbye, the nation learns to move on. It outlives me when I’m gone.”

What Washington modeled in that moment is what modern leaders still struggle to master: the ability to trust the culture they’ve built more than the control they once held. Real leadership is not the accumulation of authority; it’s the distribution of stability. It’s knowing when to ease off the clutch, to create enough space for others to accelerate.

That kind of wisdom can’t be automated. It can only be learned – slowly, through humility, awareness, and the steady rhythm of emotional self-regulation that keeps a leader from mistaking movement for progress.

Four Human Catalysts of Learning Agility

Leadership coach Erika Andersen describes learning agility as being “learnable” – the ability to keep growing even as conditions shift around you. She identifies four elements that make this possible: Aspiration, Self-Awareness, Curiosity, and Vulnerability. Each one fuels the emotional resilience required to navigate change without losing direction.

Washington modeled all four, centuries before the language existed. His evolution wasn’t just political; it was psychological. It showed what emotional intelligence looks like when lived out over time – a kind of adaptive consciousness that turns ambition into stewardship.

Aspiration was his anchor. Every action pointed to a cause larger than himself – liberty, unity, the fragile idea of democracy. In EQ terms, this was Self-Actualization in motion: purpose translated into perseverance. Washington’s vision expanded his resilience. He endured winter after winter not through willpower alone, but through meaning. For modern leaders, the lesson is clear: when your vision outlasts your comfort zone, endurance becomes a form of hope.

Coaching Insight:
Keep a vision that outlasts your comfort zone. Purpose expands resilience – it converts pressure into progress.

Reflection Point:
What vision in your work still feels bigger than your current capacity – and how might that stretch sustain you?

Self-Awareness defined his leadership style. He was unthreatened by brilliance, surrounding himself with minds as forceful as Hamilton and Jefferson. That required Self-Regard balanced with Reality Testing – a grounded sense of self strong enough to invite dissent. The best leaders still do this: they build teams that expose their blind spots rather than protect their egos. Awareness, when practiced with humility, becomes a design tool for better systems.

Coaching Insight:
Build teams that expose your blind spots rather than reinforce them. Awareness, practiced with humility, becomes a design tool for better systems.

Reflection Point:
Who in your circle tells you the truth you least want to hear – and when was the last time you thanked them for it?

Curiosity kept him evolving. Washington studied agriculture, European tactics, and governance until the end of his life – not out of vanity, but from genuine fascination. This was Flexibility paired with Problem Solving, a restless engagement with the world that turned information into insight. In the modern organization, curiosity functions the same way: it’s the renewable energy that keeps learning loops alive. Every domain, even the non-obvious ones, becomes usable data for how you lead.

Coaching Insight:
Treat every domain as usable data for how you lead – even the non-obvious ones. Curiosity keeps the system adaptive.

Reflection Point:
Where in your current role have you stopped asking questions – and what might reopen that line of inquiry?

And finally, Vulnerability – the quiet trait most often mistaken for weakness. His “spectacles moment” at Newburgh remains one of the purest demonstrations of emotional intelligence in leadership history. When Washington acknowledged his failing eyesight before his officers, he revealed humanity without losing authority. That’s Emotional Expression integrated with deep Empathy. It was a small act of truth-telling that dissolved rebellion more effectively than any command. The modern corollary is simple: let people see the human behind the leader. Presence is what earns trust when power cannot.

Coaching Insight:
Let people see the human behind the leader. It doesn’t diminish authority – it deepens it. When trust has form, collaboration has flow.

Reflection Point:
Where does trust in your organization exist only as intention – and what small act of honesty could give it structure?

Together, these four elements form the emotional circuitry of learning agility – the bridge between head and heart, between knowing and becoming.

In the language of CREATE, they ignite Agility, the piston that connects Culture, Execution, and Ethics. When aspiration fuels awareness, when curiosity coexists with vulnerability, leadership stops being a posture and becomes a practice – a dynamic equilibrium between confidence and humility, control and learning.

Washington mastered that balance not through brilliance, but through presence.
And presence, as ever, is what keeps leadership human in the age of automation.

From “Expert” to “Experimenter”

AI will soon handle expertise more efficiently than we ever could.
What remains irreplaceable is the experimenter’s mindset – the willingness to hold uncertainty without paralysis, to move forward without needing the map to be perfect.

George Washington practiced this on a national scale. The Constitution itself was an experiment; his presidency, a prototype. Every decision he made helped design the office he occupied.

In a letter to James Madison just before taking office, he wrote,

“It is my wish and intention to conform to the public desire and expectation, with respect to the style proper for the Chief Magistrate to live in; it might be well to know (as far as the nature of the case will admit) what these are before he enters upon it.”

That phrasing – “before he enters upon it” – reveals the experiment in real time. Washington understood that he wasn’t simply inheriting a role; he was inventing one. He approached the unknown not with certainty but with design thinking – observing, testing, iterating. Where Jefferson wrote ideas and Adams argued principles, Washington modeled precedent. He didn’t just think through democracy; he embodied it.

That kind of emotional foresight – the capacity to sense how leadership will feel to others before history confirms it – is what makes Washington more relevant in the age of AI than many who followed. He made decisions not because he knew they’d work, but because he trusted that iteration would. His leadership was a series of hypotheses tested in public view – refined not by ego, but by feedback.

Modern leadership requires the same humility. The systems we build, the cultures we shape, the technologies we unleash – all of them are experiments in human alignment. Expertise alone can’t navigate that terrain. Only emotional intelligence can hold the tension between confidence and curiosity long enough to learn.

Coaching Insight:
Replace perfectionism with iterative integrity – act, learn, adjust. In a world of accelerating feedback loops, reflection speed is more valuable than reaction speed.

Reflection Point:
Where in your leadership are you waiting to be certain – when what’s needed is the courage to begin?

Emotional Intelligence as the Engine of Adaptation

Learning agility isn’t cognitive alone – it’s emotional endurance.

The data tell the same story: according to EQ-i 2.0, high performers navigating rapid change consistently score strongest in Optimism, Stress Tolerance, and Flexibility. Those aren’t moods; they’re muscles – trained through self-awareness, purpose, and deliberate practice.

George Washington built those muscles long before psychology had the language for them. His rituals were simple but profound: daily letters, moments of prayer, solitary walks. These weren’t distractions from leadership – they were leadership. Each routine created a boundary between stimulus and response, giving emotion a place to settle before decision.

He practiced what we might now call emotional hygiene – metabolizing stress before passing it on. In doing so, he turned chaos into calm direction. His stillness wasn’t passivity; it was preparation. He understood that composure was contagious.

Modern leaders face the same demand. In a world of constant acceleration, our nervous systems become the true governors of strategy. Emotional endurance is what allows intellect to function under pressure – the stabilizing current beneath adaptation itself.

From History to Habit: The CREATE Application

In the end, agility isn’t just an individual trait – it’s a cultural rhythm. Every domain of CREATE contains a hidden invitation to learn: a way to translate emotional intelligence into momentum.

When practiced together, they form a learning flywheel – a system that refuels itself through reflection, humility, and shared growth.

  • Culture → Aspiration
    Revisit the why before you redesign the how. Meaning renews motion.

  • Relationships → Vulnerability
    Let people see the learner, not just the leader. Credibility grows through honesty, not perfection.

  • Execution → Curiosity
    Institutionalize retrospectives – fail, learn, document. Progress is iteration made visible.

  • Agility → Self-Awareness
    Pause quarterly to map your growth edges. Speed without reflection is drift.

  • Teaching → Modeling
    Share your learning process, not just your results. Transparency multiplies wisdom.

  • Ethical Decision-Making → Humility
    Admit uncertainty; invite collective intelligence. Integrity is a shared act of discernment.

Each domain strengthens the others – creating a living loop where emotional intelligence sustains velocity without losing humanity.

That’s the true engine of adaptive leadership: not perpetual motion, but perpetual learning.

The Human Advantage

AI will master calculation.

That’s inevitable – and, in many ways, desirable. My wife is an accountant; she jokes she may be the last generation to do that work by hand. She’s probably right. Crunching numbers and issuing reports are things machines will always do faster, cheaper, and with fewer errors.

But transformation – the work of becoming – remains human terrain. Only people can metabolize fear into curiosity, pressure into courage, and data into meaning. Only we can bridge the gap between information and insight, between what is known and what is understood.

That’s the real distinction of leadership in the age of AI: not the ability to process inputs, but the willingness to be transformed by them.

George Washington understood this long before algorithms. His evolution from ambitious officer to tempered statesman reminds us that progress isn’t the betrayal of principle – it’s fidelity to purpose over time.

He didn’t fear change; he embodied it with grace. And that’s the invitation before us now.

As machines learn to think, we must remember how to learn – and lead – with heart.
