Leadership Fitness Isn’t Neurology. It’s Design.

Matt Goode21 March 20267 min read Get new pieces by email

# Neuroinclusion

Last week, the President of the United States told reporters that dyslexia should disqualify someone from the presidency. He said it four times in five days.

Embedded in those comments is an assumption worth examining carefully – not because of who said it, but because it doesn’t belong only to him. It lives inside organizations everywhere, quietly filtering out some of the most capable thinkers in the building.

The assumption: leadership fitness is a function of how your brain is wired.

That assumption isn’t just wrong. It’s the exact design flaw that keeps organizations stuck.

The Student the System Couldn’t See

I spent more than two decades teaching. For seventeen of those years, I worked at a K–12 school, which meant I didn’t just watch kids from September to June. I watched them over the years. I knew their families. I saw who they became.

That longitudinal view changes what you can see.

The student flagged for deficiency in fourth grade – I could watch him in eleventh. The kid the system had quietly written off as a struggling reader – I could see where she ended up. And what I saw, often enough that it stopped surprising me, was this: the students whose brains had been labeled as problems were, in many cases, the ones who went on to the most selective universities in the country. They were the best thinkers. The system just couldn’t see it.

I know this more personally than I’d like.

My own son is severely dyslexic. He was counselled out of the school where I taught – the same school where I showed up every day to help other people’s children learn. We found a specialized school. He received Orton-Gillingham instruction. It was, without exaggeration, life-changing. The difference between who he was before that intervention and who he became after it is the difference between a kid the system was losing and a kid who could finally show what he actually knew.

He is currently completing a five-year accelerated BS/MS program in mechanical engineering at one of the most selective engineering schools in the country. Dean’s List. Severely dyslexic.

But here’s what that path cost our family: my entire teacher’s salary – every dollar I earned at one school – went to pay tuition at another. That’s the math we lived. And I know we were the fortunate ones. Not every family can absorb that. Not every kid gets that intervention. Many don’t. They fall through the cracks, disengage, and carry something bitter about education and about their own intelligence for years longer than they should.

This essay is about what happens when those kids grow up – and what their brains are actually capable of when someone finally builds a system designed to find out.

One in Five Brains. Zero Intelligence Deficit.

Dyslexia is a processing difference. It is not an intelligence deficit.

The Yale Center for Dyslexia and Creativity is unequivocal: dyslexia is unrelated to overall intelligence. It affects roughly one in five people, which means it is almost certainly present in every leadership team, every boardroom, and every cabinet, whether anyone has named it or not.

What dyslexic cognition tends to produce isn’t a list of compensations. It’s a list of capacities. Big-picture synthesis. Pattern detection across domains. Narrative thinking – the ability to hold complexity as a story rather than a spreadsheet. Crisis navigation that doesn’t freeze in the face of ambiguity.

Researchers at Cambridge describe this as “explorer cognition” – the brain that thrives when the terrain is unfamiliar, and the old maps don’t work. The conditions that most demand leadership – volatility, uncertainty, crisis, disruption, the moment when the playbook stops working – are precisely the conditions where dyslexic cognition has a structural advantage.

Dyslexic thinkers aren’t scanning for the answer inside the known constraints. They’re looking for the frame that makes the constraints irrelevant.

That’s not a liability for leadership. That’s the job.

What the Research Actually Says

In 2018, EY partnered with Made By Dyslexia to map dyslexic thinking skills directly to workforce demand – in a report written entirely by dyslexic individuals at EY. The methodology is the message.

The six identified skill areas – Reasoning, Connecting, Exploring, Communicating, Imagining, and Visualising – map directly to the World Economic Forum’s most in-demand capabilities for the future of work. In 2022, LinkedIn added “Dyslexic Thinking” as an official skill on its platform. Nine hundred million professionals. The world’s largest career network.

EY’s conclusion was direct: a business where dyslexic strengths are understood and harnessed could become more innovative and better positioned for a rapidly changing world of work. Not more charitable. Not more accommodating. More competitive.

The President sees dyslexia as a disqualifier. LinkedIn sees it as a credential. One of them is reading the future correctly.

Fitness Isn’t Neurology. It’s Design.

The real question was never whether a leader has a learning difference.

The real question is whether the leader has built a system that extracts the best thinking from every kind of mind in the room – including their own.

Those are not the same question. And conflating them is where most organizations go quietly, expensively wrong.

The research locates fitness not in individual neurology but in the system the leader builds. A leader who builds a system that only works for one kind of brain hasn’t built a strong team. They’ve built a vulnerable one – and every problem that requires a different kind of thinking will eventually find the gap.

There’s an irony worth naming. The leaders most likely to build systems that work for every kind of thinker are often the ones who spent a lifetime surviving systems that weren’t built for them. They know what it costs to mask, adapt, and perform competence in someone else’s cognitive language. That instinct – the hard-won understanding that there are multiple ways to think, multiple routes to a right answer, multiple ways to demonstrate what you know – is exactly what leadership at scale requires.

My son is completing an accelerated graduate engineering degree because someone finally built a system designed to find what his brain could actually do. The system that couldn’t see him wasn’t measuring his potential. It was measuring its own limitations.

The same is true in every organization. The same is true in leadership.

The Double Work Nobody Talks About

Right now, in your building, on your team, inside your leadership structure, there are people doing double work. They are executing the job and simultaneously translating their best thinking into a format the system can recognize. They are performing competence in a cognitive language that isn’t native to them, while their actual strongest thinking goes untapped.

The cost isn’t the energy required to accommodate differences. The cost is what you never get – the pattern the conventional thinker didn’t see, the reframe that unlocked the stuck problem, the crisis navigation that didn’t freeze because the person leading it had spent their whole life finding new routes when the standard one was closed.

A leader who looks at dyslexia and sees disqualification has never had to think carefully about what thinking actually is – or what it costs an organization when you suppress the wrong kind.

The Double Work Diagnostic

Before you move on, sit with these four questions. They’re worth bringing to your leadership team:

1. Who on your team is translating? Who is doing double work – executing the job and converting their best thinking into a format the system can recognize? How would you know?

2. What problems keep returning unsolved? If a challenge has cycled through your team more than once without resolution, consider whether you’ve been applying one cognitive style to a problem that requires a different one.

3. Who have you called a poor communicator? Some of the people labeled “hard to follow” or “not concise” are thinking in a mode the system wasn’t designed to receive. Are they unclear – or are they untranslatable in your current structure?

4. Whose best thinking is the system actively suppressing? If your meeting formats, reporting structures, and decision processes were designed around one kind of mind, you already know the answer.

Leadership bottlenecks are design problems. What helps some helps all. A team that can only access one kind of intelligence isn’t a strong team – it’s a team waiting for the problem that exposes it.

If these questions are relevant, I’d like to discuss. The work I do with founders and leadership teams is built around exactly this: finding where your system leaves capacity on the table and redesigning it so that every kind of thinker can contribute at their best.

If those questions hit a nerve, you’re not alone. Most of the organizations I work with – founders, leadership teams, schools, nonprofits, government agencies – arrive at the same place: they can see the problem clearly, but the system hasn’t caught up to what they know. The work is building the infrastructure that closes that gap.
