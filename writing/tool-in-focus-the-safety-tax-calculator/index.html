Tool in Focus: The Safety Tax Calculator

Essay 3.5 of 5

Matt Goode20 November 20257 min read Get new pieces by email

# Psychological safety

How to put rough numbers on what fear is costing your team

In the last essay, The Safety Tax, we named something most teams feel but rarely quantify.

We looked at how fear shows up in everyday behaviors – hesitation to ask for help, avoidance of conflict, delayed escalation – and how those micro-moments become macro-costs: rework, slower decision-making, missed innovation, and the quiet disengagement of smart people who stop offering their best thinking.

We walked through a short diagnostic, adapted from Amy Edmondson’s research, with questions like:

  • It is difficult to ask other members of this team for help.

  • When someone makes a mistake, it is often held against them.

  • Concerns are often escalated too late.

Those reverse-coded items don’t just describe a culture; they diagnose it. When they score high, you’re not just managing projects – you’re managing the unintended consequences of fear.

So if Essay 3 was about seeing the tax, this one is about measuring it.

Not with forensic precision, but with enough clarity that you can finally look your leadership system in the eye and say:

“This is what fear is costing us. And we don’t have to keep paying it.”

That’s where The Safety Tax Calculator comes in.

The Safety Tax Calculator

A practical tool for quantifying silence, hesitation, and psychological risk.

The brilliance of this tool is its simplicity. It’s not built for perfect fidelity. It’s built to:

Make the invisible visible – to convert cultural drag into something you can see, interrogate, and decide against.

If you completed the earlier diagnostic or used the Google Form, this tool is the next step. It takes what your team feels and turns it into something you can roughly estimate, not just vaguely sense.

Here’s how it works.

1. Choose the Behavior

Your leading indicator of fear

Start with one observable behavior that matters in your context. For example:

  • Speaking up when something feels off

  • Sharing dissent or counterpoints

  • Escalating risks early

  • Admitting uncertainty or asking for help

These are the first places fear shows up – weeks or months before it appears as a missed deadline, a blown rollout, or a quiet resignation.

In the last essay, we noted that hesitation before speaking up = delayed insight, and that avoidance of conflict = compounded misalignment. Here, we zoom in on one of those behaviors and treat it like a measurable variable.

Why this matters:

Research from MIT’s Human Dynamics Lab shows that high-performing teams share information early, freely, and frequently. Teams with slower, filtered communication consistently underperform – even when they have more expertise on paper.

Leaders often mistake quietness for personality:

“She’s just introverted.”
“He’s conflict-averse.”

But these behaviors are not quirks. They’re system signals.

2. Estimate the Latency

The delay between knowing and naming

This is the heart of the calculator.

Ask:

  • How long does someone wait before raising the issue?

    • Hours?

    • Days?

    • Two weeks

  • Do people wait for the “right” meeting because they don’t want to “bother” anyone?

  • Do concerns show up only when they can’t be avoided?

Amy Edmondson’s work – and subsequent studies in Administrative Science Quarterly – describe this as interpersonal risk latency: the invisible delay between noticing an issue and feeling safe enough to name it.

Even a two-day delay in a complex system creates measurable downstream drag.

A coaching question to ground this:

  • If your team had perfect psychological safety, how fast would you hear about problems?

  • How far is today from that ideal?

That gap is part of your safety tax.

In Essay 3, we talked about how “Concerns are often escalated too late” is a red flag. Here, we turn that red flag into a rough number.

3. Assign the Cost of Delay

Where culture becomes quantifiable

Now we translate latency into drag.

Ask three questions:

a. What rework does this delay create?

Does a three-day silence:

  • Turn a minor bug into a week of cleanup?

  • Turn a small concern into a complete project reset?

  • Force a team to redo work that could’ve been corrected in an afternoon?

Even rough estimates work:

  • “We lost about 6 hours last time this happened.”

  • “We spent an extra sprint fixing something we could have prevented.”

  • “It pushed our decision cycle back a week.”

b. What opportunity cost did we miss?

This category is rarely tracked – and almost always the largest.

Did the delay:

  • Close a partnership window?

  • Erode a client’s confidence?

  • Give a faster competitor the opening they needed?

This is the part of the tax that almost never shows up on internal dashboards, but leaders feel it in their gut.

c. What emotional labor did it create?

This is the portion leaders consistently underestimate.

Fear creates:

  • Quiet resentment

  • Burnout through anticipation (“I know this will blow up later, but I don’t feel safe to say it yet.”)

  • Lower creativity

  • Second-guessing

  • Avoidance patterns

McKinsey refers to this cumulative effect as organizational drag – the friction that slows execution, fatigues talent, and erodes decision quality.

In Essay 3, we named this in plain language:

  • Over-prepping for routine meetings

  • Postponed decisions

  • Conflict is avoided instead of being addressed

Here, the calculator helps you see those not just as “annoyances” but as quantifiable drag.

Fear is expensive. Drag multiplies.

4. Calculate the Quarterly Burn Rate

Your “fear budget” line item

This is not a finance exercise for your controller. It’s a shift in perspective for your leadership.

Choose:

  • Behavior: e.g., “Escalating risk”

  • Average latency: e.g., 10 days

  • Estimated cost of each delay: e.g., 8 hours of rework + opportunity loss

  • Frequency per quarter: e.g., 4 instances

Run a rough calculation. No spreadsheet perfection required.

Leaders are often stunned when they realize:

  • A single 10-day hesitation, repeated across a 12-person team, can easily exceed the cost of:

    • A training program

    • A consultant

    • An annual offsite

    • A dedicated project manager

In the last essay, we said:

“Because once you can see the tax, you can finally begin to reduce it.”

This is what “seeing it” can look like: not as a vague sense of “things feel slow,” but as an estimated cost you can no longer dismiss.

Fear is often the most expensive – and least budgeted – line item in your organization.

5. Reframe the Numbers

The goal isn’t accuracy – it’s awakening

Let’s be clear:

You are not building a perfect financial model.
You are building awareness.

The calculation shifts fear from:

  • Soft issue → Operational variable

  • Cultural squishiness → Performance bandwidth

  • Interpersonal discomfort → Strategic drag

As soon as leaders see fear as a budget line, they start designing against it:

  • Clear meeting norms (Who speaks when? How do we surface dissent?)

  • Faster decision cycles (Where are decisions stuck? Why?)

  • Explicit dissent pathways (How can people disagree safely?)

  • Sharper role clarity (Who owns which decisions?)

  • Defined escalation protocols (What’s the “early warning” channel?)

  • Psychological safety rituals (Regular “red flag” rounds, after-action reviews, learning debriefs)

This is how safety stops being abstract and becomes architecture.

6. The Coaching Moment

Where the tool becomes a transformation

This is the pivot point.

Ask yourself – or your leadership team:

If fear had a cost center in your organization, what would its budget be?

Then:

What becomes possible if you reduce that spend by just 10%?

This moves the conversation from: guilt → awareness → ownership → redesign.

Because the truth is:

  • The meetings that run longer than they should

  • The ideas that arrive after the window closes

  • The people who play it safe even when you say you want candor

Those aren’t just “communication issues.” They’re cost overruns in disguise.

A Neurodiversity-Forward Note

Not all latency equals fear

One crucial nuance as you start using this tool: Not every delay is fear-based.

For neurodiverse team members and slower processors, hesitation may reflect:

  • genuine processing time

  • sensory overload

  • preference for written communication

  • unclear or chaotic meeting structure

This is why meeting design is an inclusion practice, not a bureaucratic one.

  • Clear agendas

  • Predictable turn-taking

  • Visual thinking tools

  • Asynchronous input channels

These don’t just make things “tidier.” They lower interpersonal risk – and they help you distinguish between:

  • Fear-based latency and

  • Cognitive-processing latency

Both are valid. Only one is a cultural tax.

When you treat all latency as fear, you misdiagnose.
When you ignore fear-based latency, you underdiagnose.
The work is learning to tell the difference.

Coaching Reflection: Where Am I Taxing My Team?

Let’s bring this back to you as a leader.

1. What’s the cost of what’s not being said on my team right now?
Silence isn’t the absence of information; it’s the presence of risk. Studies in Organization Science show that unspoken concerns are some of the strongest predictors of downstream failure – not because people don’t know what to do, but because they don’t feel safe enough to say what they see.

2. Where might my tone, pace, or presence be making it harder to speak up early?
The Yale Center for Emotional Intelligence notes that leaders communicate safety or threat within milliseconds through nonverbal cues alone. Your facial expression, your interruptions, your speed of response – they’re all part of the system.

3. Am I removing blockers – or am I unintentionally being one?
McKinsey’s research on organizational health is clear: leaders often create constraints unintentionally, by being too quick with opinions, too slow with curiosity, or too selective about whose input they solicit. –

4. If my team could safely name the one thing I’m not seeing, what truth might finally become visible?
And how would our system shift if that truth could be said without fear?

In Essay 3, we said:

“Because when reverse-coded items score high, they don’t just describe the culture – they diagnose it.”

The Safety Tax Calculator is what you use next: to estimate what that diagnosis is costing you – and to begin designing something better.

Because psychological safety isn’t the work you do after the work.

It’s the work that makes all other work possible.
