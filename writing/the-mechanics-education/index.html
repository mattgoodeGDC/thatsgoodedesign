The Mechanic’s Education

Why Classics Still Matter When Everything Moves Fast

Matt Goode17 July 20266 min read Get new pieces by email

# Schools

Late one night in 1925, a classics professor named Carol Wight came across a mechanic reading Thucydides on the grimy floor of his shop. When Wight asked the predictable question – “What good will that do you?” – the mechanic gave the answer that changes everything:

“With this,” he said, holding up his lathe, “I can shape steel. And with this,” holding out the book, “I can shape men.”

That sentence contains the entire argument about leadership. The mechanic already did. And it has been waiting a hundred years for your organization to notice.

The mechanic wasn’t studying philosophy to become more cultured. He was reading the ancients because they taught him how to read people – how to understand motivation, consequence, the way a decision moves through a system. He was learning judgment. Not the kind you certify in a workshop. The kind you build into how you see, that shows up under pressure, that lets you hold a difficult decision without collapsing into false clarity.

An Atlantic piece this week noticed something the business schools have been pretending isn’t true for twenty years. When AI labs hit the ceiling of what optimization alone could produce, they didn’t call the MBA programs. They called the philosophers. They didn’t hire more engineers to refine the machine. They hired people trained to read difficult texts and answer difficult questions. Daniel Greco at Yale, Daniel Korman at Stanford – sitting with frontier models, teaching them to think, grading reasoning the way a classics professor grades an essay. Because the thing the machine needed wasn’t another layer of calculation. It was judgment.

Click here to read the piece in The Atlantic.

That’s not a coincidence. That’s the whole story.

Three institutions spent a century manufacturing the opposite of what the AI labs just discovered they needed. School taught you to find the right answer. The MBA taught you to choose well between options. Your organization rewarded you for arriving with the answer already formed. Twelve years of reproduction, six years of optimization, and then a career of performing confidence – all of it practicing suppression of the exact thing the moment just revealed as scarce.

The mechanic in the shop got there differently. He was reading Thucydides in the margins of work that mattered – reading about power, consequence, how empires collapse under the weight of decisions made for the wrong reasons. He wasn’t acquiring a skill. He was being formed. And by the time the moment arrived when he needed to read a person instead of steel, the instrument was already built.

The Atlantic piece lands on something this whole series kept circling: the thing the mechanic had that your organization probably doesn’t.

A relationship with old thinking. Not as nostalgia. As infrastructure.

The classics don’t become important because you need to pass a test on Thucydides. They become important because you spent enough time inside someone else’s impossible decision that when yours arrives, you don’t experience it as unprecedented. You recognize the shape of it. You’ve watched empires fall to pride before. You’ve read about judgment corroding under pressure. You’ve encountered the moment when two good people disagree on something real and no framework resolves it cleanly.

You’ve been formed.

I work with founders and operators – people scaling from 25 to 150 people, the point where your personal judgment can no longer carry the organization. The work I do is build the infrastructure that distributes judgment before one person becomes the bottleneck. Load-test it deliberately. Grow people under weight instead of hoping they’ll absorb judgment they were never asked to practice.

That’s the mechanic’s move translated into an operating system – the way an organization builds people instead of just deploying them.

The mechanic had the Atlantic’s benefit: a hundred years of distance between him and Thucydides, enough perspective to see the pattern repeat. Most of us don’t have that luxury. We’re in the moment. The pressure is now. The decision is real, and the map is incomplete.

So what we can do instead: stop selecting for people who arrive with the answer already formed. Start watching who builds the frame under pressure. Stop measuring what shows up on command. Start observing what holds under load. Stop sending people to workshops. Start designing systems that deliberately shape them over time.

The AI labs already proved the point. They had the technology. It wasn’t enough. They needed philosophers. They needed people trained in the difficult discipline of reading something that didn’t immediately make sense, of sitting inside unresolved tension, of letting a question open rather than closing it too fast.

They needed people the classics had formed.

The mechanic’s move is the move you’re building right now, whether you know it or not. Not Thucydides on the shop floor – though you’d be lucky to have someone reading that way. But the understanding of what old thinking is actually for: not cultural credibility, not self-improvement, but the unglamorous, foundational work of building the interior architecture that lets a person read a situation accurately and respond to what’s actually there.

That’s what I do. That’s what every organization that will win the next decade is quietly building – not better tools, but better people, formed deliberately, under conditions that make judgment visible before the moment it actually matters.

Three questions for this week:

  • What judgment do you have in your organization that your hiring and promotion systems structurally overlook?

  • Who has been reading the hard texts – not in a classical sense, but living through complexity without the playbook – and what have they learned that your official frameworks never captured?

  • What changes if you stop trying to install judgment and start building the conditions that form it?
