Whose Brain Is Missing from the Room Where It Happens?

Matt Goode13 February 20268 min read Get new pieces by email

# Neuroinclusion# 6C

A leadership team I was working with at a government agency had been stuck on the same problem for three hours. These were people making decisions that affected field operations in crisis zones. Smart people. Aligned on values. Genuinely committed to getting it right. But every strategy session looped: the same questions, the same frameworks, the same impasse.

Then I noticed what was actually happening: everyone in the room processed information the same way.

Sequential. Verbal. Collaborative. They could all think on their feet, build on each other’s ideas in real time, and articulate their reasoning as they went. It looked like high-functioning teamwork. It felt like alignment. But they kept missing the same category of problem: the ones that required sitting with ambiguity, zooming out to system-level patterns, or asking a fundamentally different question than the one the data was answering.

They weren’t lacking intelligence. They were all intelligent in the same way.

Earlier this week, I wrote about delegation (aka, Just Handle It) and the three things most leaders fail to make explicit in the first thirty seconds of a handoff. That piece struck a nerve. Yesterday, I published the 6C Decision Matrix, a tool I developed to clarify not only what is decided but also how and by whom. That one struck a different nerve.

(A confession: I was tinkering with the layout yesterday morning and accidentally sent the essay out without the grid image attached. If you’re a subscriber, you received the essay without the grid in your email. Sorry!)

I went to bed last night with my head spinning about both essays and how the grid connects them. Because here’s what I’ve learned since building those frameworks: delegation clarity is necessary, but it’s not sufficient. Decision-mode clarity is necessary but not sufficient. You can nail every element of the handoff, choose exactly the right decision mode, and still fail if everyone involved in the decision processes information in the same way. The question isn’t only how you delegate or how you decide. It’s whose brain is in the room when it happens.

Most organizations treat cognitive diversity as a compliance issue. How do we accommodate this person? How do we help them succeed despite their diagnosis?

Both questions start from the same flawed premise: that there’s an optimal way to think, and the organization’s job is to smooth over the gaps when someone deviates from it.

But here’s what neuroscience keeps showing us: different brains don’t just process differently. They see differently. And that difference isn’t a deficit to manage. It’s a strategic capability most organizations systematically suppress.

The question isn’t “How do we accommodate this person?”

It’s “Which problems actually require this cognitive profile to solve?”

That reframe changes everything.

There’s a model from cognitive neuroscience, the explorer-versus-exploiter model, that maps perfectly to this.

Some brains are wired for exploration. They scan horizons, detect patterns across domains, navigate uncertainty, and get bored in stable systems. They start experimenting before the data says it’s safe. They connect ideas that no one else thought to put together.

Other brains are wired for exploitation. Precision. Consistency. Deep-system optimization. Quality control that doesn’t fatigue under repetition. They see the structural flaw hiding in plain sight, the edge case everyone else assumed wouldn’t matter.

Both are essential. Most organizations only design for one.

They hire for “culture fit,” which usually means “thinks as we think.” They build workflows that assume uniform processing: real-time brainstorming, open-plan offices, popcorn sharing in meetings. All are designed for individuals who think aloud and thrive in social environments. If your brain doesn’t work that way, the system doesn’t call you less capable. It quietly ensures your best thinking never reaches the room.

In a comment thread on the delegation essay, the issue of trust arose, and it’s worth pausing on because it reveals the same blind spot from a different angle.

I’ve served as a certified Speed of Trust facilitator, and the Covey framework does important work: it gives organizations a common language for why trust matters and how trust behaviors compound over time. I used it extensively at USAID. But here’s what I kept running into: the framework assumes trust looks the same everywhere. That building, maintaining, and honoring it follow a universal pattern. They don’t.

In some cultures, trust is built through direct confrontation. You earn credibility by challenging ideas openly, even forcefully. In others, trust requires protecting face. Publicly questioning a colleague’s reasoning doesn’t signal intellectual rigor. It signals disrespect. In some contexts, trust develops through personal relationships first and professional credibility second. In others, it’s the reverse. Erin Meyer maps this beautifully in The Culture Map, and anyone leading across borders or identities should read it.

At a global humanitarian organization with colleagues in Amman, Kyiv, Nairobi, and Washington, the Western, boardroom-centric trust model wasn’t wrong. It was incomplete. When we ran Speed of Trust workshops, the American and Northern European staff nodded along. Colleagues from East Africa, the Middle East, and South Asia were polite, but the framework didn’t land the same way because it wasn’t built for how they experienced trust. The behaviors Covey identifies as trust-building, things like “talk straight” and “confront reality,” can read very differently depending on the cultural operating system of the person hearing them.

This isn’t a flaw in those cultures. It’s glorious, actually. The range of ways humans build trust is one of the most sophisticated things about us. But you have to be aware of it, in much the same way you have to be aware of neurodiversity. Because when you design for one trust model and call it universal, you make the same mistake as when you design for one cognitive profile and call it normal. You end up measuring everyone against a standard that was only ever one way of being human. And people who don’t fit the template aren’t lacking in trustworthiness or intelligence. They operate from a different set of assumptions about how collaboration is supposed to work.

Trust frameworks tell you the relational conditions for sound decision-making. They don’t tell you whether you have the cognitive or cultural diversity to actually make them.

When I trace recurring organizational problems back to their root cause, the pattern is almost always the same: it’s not an execution failure. It’s a cognitive homogeneity problem.

Innovation keeps stalling? You probably need explorer brains, the ones who get bored with optimization and start seeing possibilities before the data proves it’s safe. If your roadmap feels incremental and no one is asking whether you’re solving the right problem, you don’t have a strategy issue. You have a thinking-style gap.

Quality keeps failing in the same category? You probably need exploiter brains, the ones who model structural integrity in real time and don’t miss the edge case. They’re not being pedantic. They’re seeing a resolution of detail that the rest of the team literally cannot access.

Strategic blind spots keep surprising you? You probably need pattern detectors, the people who will tell you what you don’t want to hear before it becomes what you can’t ignore. If your leadership team keeps getting ambushed by risks “no one saw coming,” it’s not that no one saw them. It’s that the people who saw them either weren’t in the room or weren’t heard when they spoke.

Execution keeps breaking at the handoffs? You probably need to pair systems thinkers with kinetic activators: one holds the whole architecture in mind, the other moves fast and adapts in real time. Separately, they struggle. Together, they’re the most effective execution team you’ve ever seen.

A startup I worked with decided to test this deliberately. They stopped hiring for culture fit and started treating cognitive composition as a strategic design decision, just as they’d always approached skill diversity and domain expertise. Within six months, the patterns shifted visibly. Innovation cycles are shortened. Quality failures dropped to a fraction of their previous rate. Strategic risks that had previously surfaced only in crises began getting flagged early. And attrition among their strongest performers, a persistent problem, fell dramatically.

Not because they got nicer. Because they got smarter about which brains they needed in which rooms.

The diagnosis is simple. Think about your last strategic planning session and ask four questions:

  • Who noticed a pattern nobody else saw?

  • Who asked the question that actually exposed the flaw in the group’s logic?

  • Who came back three days later with the synthesis everyone needed?

  • Who spotted the risk the optimists dismissed?

If you can name those people, you have cognitive diversity. If you can’t, or if those people have left, that’s your signal.

And if you’re the leader who just learned to delegate well, ask one more: Did I hand this off to someone who sees the problem the same way I do? Because a clean handoff to a cognitive twin isn’t delegation. It’s duplication.

The org chart shows who reports to whom. It doesn’t show whose brain you’re missing. And the problems you keep running into aren’t execution, strategy, or people problems.

They’re the sound of one kind of thinking trying to solve every kind of problem. And until you ask whose brain is missing from the room where it happens, the loop won’t break.
