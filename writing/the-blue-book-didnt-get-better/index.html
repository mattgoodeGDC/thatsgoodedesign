The Blue Book Didn’t Get Better

AI didn’t break assessment. It revealed that most of what we were asking could always be looked up.

Matt Goode7 August 202623 min read Get new pieces by email

# Schools# AI

I knew the material. I had done the reading. I could hold the whole argument in my head at once – the shape of it, where it turned, what the counterexample was. What I could not do was get it out of my hand and onto a ruled page in real time, in letters another human being could read.

Nobody called it dyslexia. The word on offer was lazy, and it usually came paired with bright, which made it worse, because the pairing located the entire problem in my character. Bright, so the capacity was real. Lazy, so the gap was mine to close.

So I read Walt Hunter’s defense of the blue book in The Atlantic with a particular kind of attention. Most of it is right. OK, actually, it made me mad. Mad enough to write a really, really long essay about it.

Indulge me, please. When I have an idea, sometimes words just explode.

What He Gets Right

The integrity problem is real.

Take-home work is no longer evidence of anything. Duh. Hunter points to Princeton, which spent 133 years trusting a signed pledge and voted this May to proctor exams for the first time since the Gilded Age.

Nobody abandons a tradition because they are hankering for more surveillance.

And he is right about something deeper. What a written exam captures at its best is not recall but the moment a student does not yet know what they think and has to find out by producing sentences, which he calls “thinking on the page.”

That phrase is the most important thing in his essay, and he does not follow it far enough. Thinking on the page is generation – building a frame that did not exist before you built it, rather than optimizing inside one somebody else handed you.

Hunter has identified the right thing. He has attached it to the wrong instrument, and underneath the instrument problem sits a question problem that neither the blue book nor the take-home essay was ever going to solve.

The Conflation

His essay does what every defense of a traditional instrument does. It fuses a legitimate demand with an inherited interface, then defends them as a single object.

  • The demand: compose an “original” thought, under time, without outside help, in a supervised room.

  • The interface: a paper booklet, a pen, cursive, ninety minutes, no revision, no tools.

Those are separable, and everything he wants – the risk, the uncertainty, the improvisation, the idea that arrives at the buzzer – lives in the demand, not in the pen, which was only ever what happened to be available in 1920.

He treats the two as one because for him they are. When the cost of transcription is near zero, the instrument disappears, and you experience it as pure thought. That is why it feels timeless.

A tool is invisible to the people it fits. That’s what made me mad. It doesn’t fit everyone, myself included.

The Blue Book Is a Test

There are two kinds of instruments, and almost every institution owns only one of them.

A test asks you to reproduce what is already known. The standard is set in advance, you perform against it, and an evaluator holding the answer key scores the result. The audience is one person, and that person already knows what you are supposed to say. Tests catch skill because skill is what shows up on command.

A showcase asks you to make something that did not exist before you made it. The prompt is open. The standard cannot be fully specified, because original work cannot be pre-scored. And the audience is real and present – not an evaluator with a key, but people encountering the work for the first time, forming their own responses, asking questions nobody scripted. The frame has to be authored in the moment, for a room whose reactions cannot be predicted.

Neither instrument teaches anything. Both surface what was already built in. A test reveals how well you modeled the evaluator. A showcase reveals whether you can generate under conditions you did not design.

The blue book is a test.

Consider this: a competent student does something specific in the weeks before the exam. They read the syllabus backward, noting which themes the professor kept circling, which texts got a full session, which framing showed up on the midterm, and by Thursday most of them have the field down to three plausible prompts. The good ones get it to two.

Then they stop reviewing and start drafting. They build the argument in advance, choose the evidence, rehearse the transitions, and walk into the room on Friday carrying a substantially pre-composed essay, which they spend ninety minutes transcribing from memory against a clock.

That is retrieval wearing the costume of generation.

I know because I taught it.

For years I built benchmark essays for my students – model responses at each band of the rubric – and then handed them the rubric and made them grade the models. It is excellent instruction. Students who have scored an essay understand what an evaluator is looking for in a way that no amount of being told will produce, and their own writing improves immediately and measurably. What I was actually teaching them was how to reverse-engineer an evaluator. I was very good at it. Their scores went up.

Nobody in that room, myself included, would have described the exercise as training retrieval.

We called it teaching “writing.”

So what do blue book results actually measure?

  1. How accurately a student predicted the prompt.

  2. How much prepared text they could hold and reproduce.

  3. How fast and legibly they could get it onto paper.

Three trainable skills, all demonstrable on command, none of them the thing Hunter is trying to protect.

And the AI guarantee is thinner than it looks. A student can spend Wednesday night refining three candidate essays with a chatbot, memorize the strongest skeleton, and reproduce it in ink on Friday. Nothing in that room catches it. Proctoring defends ninety minutes and leaves the week untouched – and the week is where the essay was always written.

What defends the week is the thing the blue book removed. You can pre-compose an essay. You cannot pre-compose an answer to a question nobody has asked yet.

The tell arrives fast. A student running retrieval hears an unscripted question and reaches to locate the correct answer, scanning for the response the room probably wants. A student who made something hears the same question and gets curious, because the frame is theirs, and a challenge to something you built yourself is interesting long before it is threatening. Thirty seconds of live questioning separates the two, and no amount of Wednesday-night preparation closes the gap.

The obvious objection is cost, and it is a real one. A blue book exam scales to two hundred students in a single ninety-minute block. The same two hundred students at fifteen minutes of live questioning each is fifty hours of faculty time, which is why the oral defense survives in doctoral programs and almost nowhere else. Universities did not abandon it because it stopped working. They abandoned it because it does not divide.

That is the trade the blue book represents. Hunter chose an instrument that scales over an instrument that reveals. Most institutions make that choice, most of the time, and the pressure to make it is structural, not lazy. But a compromise made for throughput should be defended as a compromise. What is being sold here is integrity restored, and a proctored room delivers supervision instead. The gap between those two is where the argument for the blue book quietly fails.

Hunter went looking for a room AI could not enter. It found one. What it did not ask is whether that room was ever measuring the thing worth protecting.

Revision

I know the pattern is accurate because I lived it; I am a student of the 1990s. I took my high school AP exams on paper. I took every college exam in blue books – my university bookstore sold them for 5 cents a book. I sat my graduate exams at the LSE in a room much like the one Hunter describes. Rows and rows of desks. Nobody from my cohort anywhere near me, by design, so that I spent five hours beside strangers I would never see again. Every choice in that hall served one purpose: making collusion impossible. Not one of them served the thing Hunter says the format is for.

The British have a word for what I did in the weeks before. Revision.

In an American writing classroom, revision means re-seeing a draft – opening the argument back up, discovering you were wrong at paragraph four, generating something the first pass could not reach. In a British exam hall, revision means committing known material to memory so you can produce it on demand. Same word. Opposite acts. The exam culture borrowed the vocabulary of generation to describe an act of retrieval, and nobody noticed, because the two have been conflated for so long that the language stopped objecting.

My revision was serious work: dozens of practice essays written out longhand and against a clock, organized and discarded and rebuilt for months, all of it aimed at learning how much argument I could hold at once and how fast I could get it down before my hand gave out. Hunter would recognize every hour of it, because he describes exactly this – the stamina, the athleticism, the training that makes the performance possible.

He is right that it is athletic. That is the problem.

Consider this analogy: Running a marathon is not hard in the way people assume. It is long, it hurts, and it costs you four or five months of your life – but discipline is the whole of it. Follow the plan, log the miles, respect the taper, and nearly anyone finishes. Hell, Oprah Winfrey ran one in 1994. That was never a mystery, and treating it as one insults the ordinary miracle of what patience can do.

Now go run a sub-twenty 5K. Humble brag, I ran an eighteen-minute 5K and a sub-4-hour marathon after forty. The 5K training succcccked way more than the marathon, and it was harder in a different way.

Same sport, entirely different question. Most runners who want a sub-twenty never get it, and plenty of them out-train the marathoners trying. What is being asked for there is capacity, not volume, and capacity does not yield to patience alone. You can want it, plan for it, put in the work for three years, and the clock can still say 20:41.

The blue book is a marathon. Every one of my practice essays was a 15-mile training run. What Hunter actually wants – the frame authored in the moment, the idea that arrives unbidden – is the sub-twenty. It belongs to the same family as anything creative, which is to say it cannot be disciplined into existence.

And notice what the format never asks. It does not ask for anything original, because originality cannot be pre-scored against a rubric written at the beginning of the semester. It does not press you to clarify, the way a viva does, where the examiner hears the soft spot in your reasoning and goes straight at it. It does not hand you a situation nobody has seen and ask you to apply the semester to it.

Ink Can Only Carry One Shape

Hunter draws a hard line between the blue book and the five-paragraph essay – thesis statement, three obedient supports, rote deployment of argument and evidence – and observes, correctly, that any AI can mimic that template in seconds.

He has it exactly backward. The blue book is the format that most reliably manufactures it.

Consider what ink and a clock actually permit. You cannot reorder. You cannot cut paragraph two after discovering at paragraph five that it was the real opening. You cannot hold two threads and braid them, because braiding requires seeing both at once, and you can only see what you have already written, in the order you wrote it. Every structural decision has to be made before you know where the argument is going, and it has to be made once.

So you reach for the only shape that survives being composed in sequence. State the claim, support it three times, restate the claim. Blame the medium rather than the student, because ink and a clock can carry no other architecture.

Which means the format does not merely reward linear thinkers. It requires everyone to think linearly, or to perform it convincingly. The mind that arrives at its thesis last. The one that reasons by branching outward and pulling the branches together at the end. The one whose best move is a connection nobody asked for, arriving sideways. All of them have to be flattened into sequence before the pen touches the page, and the flattening happens in the same working memory that was supposed to be building the argument. That is a second tax stacked on the first, and it lands on many of the same people.

Then the part that should have stopped the revival cold. The template Hunter names as the thing a machine can trivially counterfeit is the exact artifact his preferred instrument is built to produce. We brought the blue book back to defeat the chatbot, and set it to manufacturing the one output the chatbot already makes for free.

The Tax

Now add my problem to that one. Whatever the blue book measures, it measures it minus the cost of getting it down.

For most students, that subtraction rounds to nothing. For a dyslexic student, or a dysgraphic one, or the substantial overlap between them, it is the single largest variable in the exam.

Writing by hand is not automatic for us. Letter formation, spelling retrieval, and motor execution run in the foreground, in working memory, in the same limited space where the argument is supposed to be assembled. Every word I stopped to spell cost me a clause I had been holding.

So I learned to write worse on purpose. Not shorter – dumber. I substituted words I could spell for words I meant, ninety minutes of continuous downgrade, and what I handed in accurately represented my orthography while systematically understating my thinking.

Mine is one failure mode out of several.

ADHD is another, and the format catches it just as reliably. The popular version is an inability to focus, which is wrong in a way that matters here. It is a difficulty allocating attention on command – to a task chosen by someone else, at a time chosen by someone else, for a duration chosen by someone else. The same student who cannot begin the essay at nine will vanish into a problem at midnight and surface at four having solved it. The blue book asks for ninety undifferentiated minutes of sustained output, on a Friday, on a prompt they did not choose, with no external scaffolding and a clock they cannot feel. It is close to a purpose-built generator of false negatives.

Then there is my daughter. She is autistic, and she does her math in pen, not out of preference but because the sound of a pencil dragging across paper is for her what fingernails on a chalkboard are for the rest of us. Which makes a room with sixty of them going at once something closer to an ordeal than an exam.

Nobody who designed that room ever considered the sound. It was not weighed and dismissed. It was not a variable at all, which is precisely what an interface looks like to the people it fits.

Ask her about the gas giants, and you will get thirty minutes. Jupiter’s storm, the mechanics of Saturn’s rings, why Uranus is on its side – reasoned rather than recited, with the obvious follow-up questions already anticipated and answered before you can ask them. She wants to be the first person to see them with her own eyes, and she means it in the specific way children mean things before anyone has explained the odds to them. Look out, NASA.

That is thinking on the page. It arrives in a voice, in a room, from a kid who would sit near the bottom of the distribution on the one instrument we have decided is rigorous.

I have spent a year inside the Iliad, writing a novel that moves it to the trenches of 1917, and the thing that stays with me is this. The Iliad and the Odyssey were composed and transmitted orally, probably sung for hundreds of years, by people who could not write and did not need to. They are the foundation of the Western canon, and not one line of them was thought on a page. Behind them sits a longer silence: when the Bronze Age palaces collapsed around 1200 BCE, Greek writing vanished for roughly four centuries. Linear B, the script that recorded it, went unread until 1952. Linear A we still cannot read at all. Through all of that, the poems survived, intact, in voices.

Writing is about five thousand years old. Our species is about three hundred thousand years old. On a twenty-four-hour clock of human existence, writing appears around twenty-five minutes before midnight, and mass literacy in the final sixty seconds. We have taken the most recent minute of the day, declared it the measure of the mind, and built every serious instrument of judgment on top of it.

Writing matters enormously. I have given my working life to it. It is a technology for holding thought still, not the thing that produces thought.

Handwriting fluency is a skill. Thinking on the page is a formation. The blue book scores both and reports one number.

Hunter loves that the format leaves a record – the crossings-out, the frantic erasures, all of it read as documentation of a mind at work. Mine documented something else. My strike-throughs were not first thoughts being revised. They were words I could not spell being traded for worse ones I could. A reader cannot tell those apart. The evidence is identical.

And my daughter’s page would carry no erasures at all. Not because she was certain, but because she was holding a pen, for reasons that have nothing to do with her thinking and everything to do with the room being survivable. Hunter would read that page as confidence. It would be a record of what it cost her to be there.

What’s more… Researchers have been documenting this since 1927, when a study handed forty-three teachers the same essay in good and poor handwriting and watched the score drop nearly nine points. A century of replication later, the meta-analysis puts the effect at roughly a full standard deviation, and finds that legibility moves a grade more than spelling, grammar, or whether the thing was typed. The field calls it the presentation effect. It is not a moral failure on the grader’s part; it is processing fluency doing what processing fluency does. A page that is hard to read reads as a mind that is hard to follow.

The effect does not require a grader who cares about penmanship. In one experiment, the raters were students rather than teachers, working from explicit content criteria, with no professional stake in neatness at all. Legible essays still scored about half a grade higher on a six-point scale, and the raters also judged the authors themselves more capable. Nobody in that room was punishing sloppiness. They were reading something that was easier to read and mistaking the ease for quality.

So the error runs at both ends. At the input, transcription eats the working memory that composition needs. At the output, the grader marks the handwriting and believes they are marking the argument.

Hunter sees some of this. He acknowledges, plainly, that not every student can sustain ninety minutes with a pencil, and that even strong students struggle to organize their thoughts under that clock. Then he offers it as one more reason to love the format.

There it is. The instrument produces uneven results. The unevenness gets reassigned to the students. The reassignment is then read as evidence that the instrument is rigorous.

Your Promotion Process Is a Blue Book

That last move is the most durable design pattern I encounter in organizations, and everything above is also a description of how your company decides who is capable.

The whiteboard interview that filters for people who think out loud fluently under observation, in a role where nobody will ever be asked to do that again. The promotion bar that rewards the fastest verbal processor and calls the result executive presence. The review cycle that scores visibility and reports it as impact. In each one, a known population reliably scores below their capability for reasons unrelated to the demand, and in none of them do we say the instrument is badly designed. We say he needs to work on his communication.

Accommodation is the tell rather than the fix. Extra time does not remove the transcription tax; it extends the meter, so that you are still paying, just longer, and now alone in a testing office while everyone else is outside. When a meaningful share of the room needs a modified version of your instrument for the instrument to measure them accurately, the instrument was never neutral to begin with.

Then run the rehearsal test on your own hiring loop. Tell me about a time you handled a difficult client. That question has a correct answer, published in every interview-prep guide on the internet, and any serious candidate arrived with a rehearsed version and delivered it from memory. They did revision. You scored the transcription.

Your performance review is worse. The criteria went out in January, the self-assessment was drafted the week before the meeting, and both parties know the format, the prompts, and roughly what a strong answer sounds like. There is no unseen case anywhere in the process, no question whose shape the candidate has not already met.

You brought back the blue book. You just called it calibration.

What the Labs Already Did

Hunter’s case rests on a claim about AI, so it is worth checking what the people building the AI actually concluded about tests.

For most of the last decade, the field ran on them. Progress was measured by benchmark: a fixed set of questions, a scoring key, a number for the press release. Models were graded the way students are graded. The scores climbed, and for a while everyone took the climb at face value.

Then the field noticed something any professor would recognize instantly. The benchmark questions were on the internet. The internet was the training data. A model acing an exam might be reasoning its way to the answer, or it might have already seen the answer key – and from the outside, those two things produce an identical transcript.

That is the rehearsal problem, discovered independently by people with several billion dollars riding on the finding. The test could not distinguish thinking from retrieval, and it never could; what changed is that the stakes finally got large enough for someone to check.

The field responded in two ways. It started building held-out evaluations – questions written after the fact and never published, so that no stored answer exists to retrieve – which is the unseen case at industrial scale. And it started paying humans to sit with the models and interrogate them.

Daniel Greco, a philosopher at Yale, has described the work in the Chronicle of Higher Education. He hands a frontier model questions built specifically so that nothing can be looked up, forces it to generate, then grades the reasoning so the next version can be trained on the difference. A question, an answer, a follow-up aimed straight at the soft spot.

He is running a viva. On a machine.

What strikes me is that the labs chose the expensive option, even though they had every incentive not to. Tests are cheap and infinitely repeatable, while hand-graded philosophical interrogation is slow, unscalable, and costs a professor’s hourly rate. Nobody pays that premium out of sentiment. You pay it when the cheap instrument has stopped telling you the truth.

Then there is the question of who they called. These are the most sophisticated buyers of cognition that have ever existed, with near-unlimited capital and one defining problem, and when they hit the ceiling of what the machine could do alone, they did not phone the business school. They went to the philosophy department.

Which leaves the argument in an odd place. Hunter wants to reinstate the exam because AI made it necessary. The AI industry retired the exam because it could not tell memorization from thought, and replaced it with an unseen case and an oral defense.

They did not arrive there out of a commitment to inclusion. They arrived there because it works, and because they had a benchmark riding on the answer.

Where I Started, and Where I Landed

I should tell you how this essay got made, because the method is part of the argument.

I dictated it. Walked the room and talked it out, because speech is the medium in which I think fastest and take the most risk. Then I revised it. Not one word was composed in ink against a clock. If it had been, you would be reading something considerably stupider.

I would wager Hunter did not write his Atlantic piece in longhand either.

I dictated it because Hunter’s essay made me angry, and my first thought was a single word.

Ableism.

I do not mean that as a claim about intent. Hunter is plainly a good teacher who likes his students, and I would guess the word would dismay him. But ableism has never required malice, only three things: a design with a known failure population, a decision to keep the design anyway, and a story that relocates the failure into the people it excludes. All three are in that essay.

Name a population the instrument cannot see. Keep the instrument. Call the blindness rigor.

I still think that is true. It is just not where I ended up, because it turns out to be the smaller half of the problem.

Suppose you fixed only the exclusion. Every student typing. Every accommodation granted before it had to be requested. The sensory environment designed by someone who had actually thought about sound. You would have an equitable version of an instrument that still cannot tell thinking from retrieval. That is a real improvement and a poor destination.

The question is not who can sit the exam. It is whether the question was worth asking.

The test takes about four seconds. Can it be Googled?

If it can, the delivery mechanism is irrelevant. Take-home or proctored, laptop or blue book, honor code or armed guard – it is the same question, measuring the same retrieval, and all the surveillance changes are how expensive the retrieval is to perform. You have not improved the assessment. You have made cheating harder on an assessment that was never measuring the thing you wanted.

If the question can be Googled, the room is theater.

Which means the panic has named the wrong culprit. AI did not break assessment; it revealed that an enormous share of what we have been asking was always retrievable, and that we could not see it because retrieval used to be slow enough to look like work. A student in 1974 spent nine hours in the stacks to produce what a chatbot now produces in nine seconds, and we mistook the nine hours for the learning.

Which makes this a design problem. We have spent two years treating it as a policing problem, and the policing has gone rather well.

Three Questions Before You Trust an Assessment

This works on an exam, a review cycle, a promotion bar, or a hiring loop.

  1. Can it be Googled? If the answer exists anywhere – in a search, a chatbot, a prep guide, or a memorized skeleton – you are measuring retrieval, and no amount of supervision changes that. Fix the question before you touch the room.

  2. State the demand without naming the format. If you cannot describe what you are measuring without referencing the artifact – the blue book, the deck, the meeting, the annual review – you are defending the artifact.

  3. Who reliably scores lower for reasons unrelated to the demand? If you can name them, you do not have a people problem. You have a design problem wearing one.

I am not arguing against the supervised room. I am arguing that the room should apply load, not ask for a demonstration, and that a good question makes the room almost incidental.

Hand someone a situation that did not exist when the syllabus was written and ask them to apply the semester to it. Ask them to defend a claim while somebody probes the weakest seam in it. Put them in front of a judgment where no answer key exists and watch what they reach for. None of those can be cheated in any format, which is what makes them worth the trouble of writing. Their organizational equivalents are the unfamiliar case, the live problem with follow-up, and the structured work sample, and all three beat the meeting.

The questions that defeat the chatbot are, almost without exception, the questions my daughter could answer. Ask her to apply something to a situation nobody has seen. Ask her to defend her reasoning while you push on it. She would be extraordinary. So would the student who cannot start at nine and solves it at four in the morning. So would the sixteen-year-old I was.

That is not a lowered bar. It is a bar finally pointed at the thing.

The Margins

Hunter ends with the thing I found most moving in the piece: the sudden brilliant idea scrawled sideways at the buzzer, the sentence on the back inside cover that redeems the whole exam.

He offers it as the blue book’s finest evidence.

It happens in the margin. On the inside of the back cover. At minute eighty-nine, after the rehearsed text has run out and there is nothing left to transcribe. Every example of generation Hunter loves occurred at the moment the test stopped being a test – off the ruled page, past the prepared material, at the exact point the question finally became one the student could not have answered in advance.

His best argument for the instrument is a description of what happened when the instrument ran out, in the margins.

I wrote those too. Constantly. That was the only moment the pressure of the page lifted enough for me to say the real thing, and it always came out at the end, crooked, in handwriting that had degraded past legibility by minute eighty.

None of which is an argument against exams. It is an argument for being precise about what our instruments can and cannot see, because the people who pay for our imprecision are never the ones who were cheating.

Solving a new problem with a nostalgic instrument re-imports every exclusion that instrument was built around, and does it with the moral confidence of people who believe they are restoring rigor.

The blue book didn’t get better. The alternatives got compromised. Those are different facts, and only one of them is a reason.

We spent years arguing about the room. The room was never the problem. If we are going to make people prove what they know, let’s stop asking them for something they could have written the night before – and start asking them something nobody has answered yet.

Three questions to sit with this week:

  1. What are you currently measuring on command that only ever shows itself under load?

  2. Who in your organization has been scored by an instrument that was never designed to see them?

  3. What becomes possible if the next assessment you run has no answer key?
