Drowning in Decisions That Were Never Yours

Matt Goode29 June 20262 min read Get new pieces by email

# 6C# Everything runs through me

Three months into her job as VP of Product, Elena hit a wall she never saw coming. The team was strong. The strategy was sound. And every morning her inbox told a different story: a dozen threads waiting on her sign-off, six messages asking “is this mine to decide?”, a calendar packed with meetings that existed only because nobody knew who owned the call.

She wasn’t failing at strategy. She was drowning in decisions that were never hers.

Decision fog, and where it comes from

Michael Porter argued that the essence of strategy is choosing what not to do. Decision fog is what happens when no one ever made that choice about authority itself. The same call gets made three times by three people. Talented contributors stop taking initiative because the rules are invisible. Everything ambiguous routes back to the leader, not for lack of capability, but for lack of clarity.

The 6C Compass clears it. Six modes for any decision, each for different terrain. Command when seconds matter and hesitation costs more than a wrong guess. Consultative when you’ll decide, but you want the field of view first. Collaborative when the call is truly shared. Consensus when full buy-in is worth the time it takes. Core Expert when the person closest to the problem should own it. Choice when you hand the decision over completely, inside clear lines.

Caption: The 6C Compass – six decision modes, each for different terrain.

The common failure isn’t picking the wrong mode. It’s never naming one, and letting the room guess. Every decision is a message. It tells your team who matters, what’s expected, and whether they’re allowed to move or have to wait for you.

What the fog costs, and what clearing it buys

That waiting has a price, and it compounds. Every call that loops back to you is a day your roadmap doesn’t move. Multiply that across a quarter, and the fog isn’t an annoyance; it’s the reason you missed the window. When leaders get explicit about who decides and how, decision latency drops sharply, often close to half. Half. That’s features shipping in weeks instead of months, and a team that stops parking work on your desk because they finally know which calls are theirs to make.

There’s a human dividend, too. Ambiguity is exhausting in a way people rarely name. The relief on a team when ownership gets clear is visible in a single meeting – the moment someone realizes a decision they’d been dreading was theirs to make all along, and they were allowed to just make it.

And the relief, when it comes, is structural, not motivational. You don’t inspire a team out of decision fog. You clear it, by saying in advance which mode the next call is in. Try it once. Name the mode out loud before the discussion starts, and watch how fast the room stops performing for you and starts working the actual problem.

Some questions to hold this week:

  • Which decisions in your inbox right now actually belong to someone else?

  • Where have you asked for input and then let the head-count make the call for you?

  • Does your team know how the next big decision will be made, or are they reading your face for clues?
