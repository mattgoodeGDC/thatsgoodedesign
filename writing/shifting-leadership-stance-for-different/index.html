Shifting Leadership Stance for Different Brains

Essay 6: Neurodiversity Series

Matt Goode14 January 202616 min read Get new pieces by email

# Neuroinclusion# VOICES

A few months ago, I was coaching a founder who couldn’t figure out why his team kept stalling.

He was brilliant – genuinely visionary. He could paint the future in a way that made investors lean forward and early employees say yes before they’d even seen a pitch deck. His energy was infectious. His conviction was magnetic.

But three months into building the product, his engineering lead – autistic, meticulous, exceptional at systems thinking – had quietly started looking for other jobs.

“I don’t understand,” the founder said. “I’ve been so clear about where we’re going. I’ve told them the vision a dozen times. Why aren’t they moving?”

I asked him to walk me through a recent team meeting.

He opened with the big picture. The problem they were solving. The market opportunity. The world they were building toward. Twenty minutes of storytelling. Pure Visionary gear.

Then he said, “So… make it happen. You all know what to do.”

But they didn’t.

Because vision without structure isn’t inspiration for some brains.

It’s cognitive overwhelm.

His engineering lead needed milestones, dependencies, decision rights, and quality gates. She needed to see the system beneath the vision – how the pieces connected, who owned what, where the handoffs happened.

But the founder had one gear: Visionary.

And when that didn’t work, he shifted to his second gear: Operator – checking in daily, asking for updates, trying to drive execution through proximity.

But he never shifted into the gear she actually needed: Sculptor.

The one where you stop trying to inspire or manage people into performance and start redesigning the systems that make performance possible.

That’s when I introduced him to VOICES – not as a leadership philosophy, but as a gearbox.

Because the problem wasn’t that he was a bad leader.

It’s that he was stuck in two gears – regardless of what the moment, or the brain he was leading, actually needed.

You’ve seen this pattern before in this series – leaders stuck in two gears, unable to shift when the moment or the person requires something different. Now let’s unpack what all six gears actually do, and when neurodivergent teams need each one.

The Over-Reliance Problem

We’ve established that most leaders get stuck in Visionary + Operator mode. But what does it actually look like to shift through all six gears? And more importantly – which gears do different brains need most?

Because leadership isn’t about finding the right gear and staying there.

It’s about shifting – reading what the person and the moment need, and toggling between stances in real time.

And when you’re leading neurodivergent teams, that shifting becomes even more essential.

Because different brains don’t just need different communication styles.

They need different leadership gears – and at different times.

What feels like clarity to one brain feels like ambiguity to another.

What feels like autonomy to one brain feels like abandonment to another.

What feels like collaboration to one brain feels like cognitive overload to another.

If you stay in one gear, you’ll connect with the brains wired like yours – and lose everyone else.

VOICES Applied to Neurodivergent Leadership

Let’s walk through the gearbox – not as theory, but as diagnostic.

Because each gear unlocks capacity for some brains and creates friction for others.

Visionary: Big Picture Activation

When it works:

Dyslexic thinkers thrive here. Big picture, pattern detection across domains, strategic narrative. They see where things are going before the data proves it. They connect ideas that seem unrelated. They synthesize complexity into clarity.

For these brains, Visionary leadership isn’t just inspiring – it’s cognitively activating. It gives them the altitude they need to see the whole system at once.

When it breaks:

Autistic professionals can feel untethered without concrete steps. Vision without structure creates anxiety, not momentum. “Where are we going?” is interesting. “How do we get there?” is essential.

ADHD brains lose focus without tangible next actions. Big picture is great – for about 90 seconds. Then the question becomes: “Okay, but what do I do right now?” If the answer is “just keep the vision in mind,” you’ve lost them.

Neuroinclusive shift:

Pair vision with visible milestones. Give the “why” and the “what by when.” Paint the future and map the first three steps. Connect the big picture to the small actions that make it real.

You’re not abandoning vision. You’re making it actionable for brains that need structure to move.

Operator: Systematic Execution

When it works:

Autistic professionals often excel here. Clear systems. Repeatable processes. Quality control that doesn’t fatigue. Execution with precision.

For these brains, Operator mode feels like cognitive relief. The ambiguity is gone. The rules are clear. The path is visible. They can focus on doing the work exceptionally well instead of decoding what “good” even means.

When it breaks:

ADHD brains stagnate in rigid structures. “Follow this exact process in this exact order” is the fastest way to kill momentum. They need room to iterate, to pivot, to discover the path by walking it.

Dyslexic thinkers feel micromanaged. Operator mode can read as “I don’t trust you to find your own way” – which for brains wired for exploration feels like being put in a box.

Neuroinclusive shift:

Offer process options, not process mandates. “Here are three ways to get to the outcome. Pick the one that works for your brain.” “The deliverable is X. The path is yours to design.” “We need to hit these milestones. How you structure your days is up to you.”

You’re not abandoning accountability. You’re calibrating autonomy to cognitive style.

Inclusive: Multiple Entry Points

When it works:

Inclusive mode creates safety for neurodivergent voices to surface without masking. It says, “Your perspective matters. Your way of thinking adds value. You don’t have to perform normalcy to belong here.”

For many neurodivergent professionals, this is the gear that finally lets them breathe.

When it breaks:

If “inclusion” means “everyone processes verbally in real-time,” you’ve excluded half your neurodivergent team.

If “inclusive” looks like open brainstorms, popcorn sharing, and spontaneous collaboration, you’ve just designed a system that only includes one kind of brain.

Inclusive isn’t about creating one big conversation where everyone participates the same way.

It’s about creating multiple pathways so different brains can contribute in the ways they actually work.

Neuroinclusive shift:

Multiple input channels. Async written contributions for brains that think before they speak. Verbal processing for brains that discover their ideas by talking. Visual mapping for brains that think spatially.

Real inclusion isn’t one-size-fits-all participation.

It’s designing systems where every brain has a viable entry point.

Coach: Tailored Development

When it works:

Coach mode, when done well, is transformational.

Tailored feedback in the person’s processing style:

  • Written feedback for autistic brains who need to re-read and integrate without social performance pressure.

  • Verbal walkthroughs for ADHD brains who process kinetically and need to talk it through.

  • Narrative framing for dyslexic brains who think in stories and need context to make meaning.

When you adapt your coaching to their processing mode, feedback actually lands. It integrates. It shifts behavior.

When it breaks:

Generic feedback. Neurotypical-normed “presence” coaching. Performance reviews that penalize people for not making eye contact, not “showing enthusiasm,” not “being more visible.”

This isn’t coaching. It’s camouflage enforcement.

You’re teaching people to perform neurotypicality – not to grow in their actual work.

Neuroinclusive shift:

Ask, “How do you receive feedback best?” – and honor it.

Then deliver feedback on outcomes and specific contributions, not on their performance of engagement.

You’re not lowering standards. You’re raising uptake by meeting people where their brains actually learn.

Encourager: Outcome Recognition

When it works:

Encourager mode recognizes effort and progress – not just neurotypical markers of “engagement.”

It sees the quiet focus. The deep thinking. The meticulous follow-through. The pattern someone noticed that no one else saw.

For neurodivergent employees who’ve spent their careers being told they’re “not collaborative enough” or “need to work on presence,” hearing “your work is exceptional” – without the implicit “but” – can be transformational.

When it breaks:

Praising the wrong things.

“Great eye contact in that meeting!” “I love how enthusiastic you sounded on that call!” “You really brought the energy today!”

If the person spent cognitive budget performing those things instead of doing their actual work, you’ve just reinforced masking.

Neuroinclusive shift:

Celebrate outcomes and specific contributions, not performance of normalcy.

“The way you caught that edge case saved us a week of rework.” “Your analysis shifted how I’m thinking about the whole problem.” “You saw a pattern no one else noticed – that’s the kind of thinking we need more of.”

You’re not ignoring social skills. You’re prioritizing impact over optics.

Sculptor: Systems Redesign

When it works:

This is the neuroinclusive gear.

Sculptor is where you stop trying to coach individuals into broken systems and start redesigning the systems themselves.

You’re not asking, “How do I help this person succeed in our current meeting structure?”

You’re asking, “What if our meeting structure is the problem?”

You’re not asking, “How do I coach this person to be more visible?”

You’re asking, “What if visibility is a neurotypical performance metric that has nothing to do with leadership impact?”

Sculptor mode is where you shift from accommodating difference to designing for it.

It’s the gear where you say:

“Our stand-ups are forcing real-time verbal processing. Let’s redesign them with async options.”

“Our performance reviews penalize people for not performing charisma. Let’s rewrite the criteria to measure impact.”

“Our office layout is sensory chaos for half the team. Let’s create quiet zones and flexible work options.”

When it breaks:

When leaders skip Sculptor entirely and try to solve systemic barriers through Coaching or Encouragement.

“I know the open office is hard for you – have you tried noise-canceling headphones?” “I know you struggle in brainstorms – maybe practice speaking up more?” “I know you feel excluded – keep bringing your whole self, it’ll get easier.”

This isn’t leadership. It’s individualizing a systems problem.

You’re putting the burden of adaptation on the person instead of redesigning the conditions that create the friction.

Neuroinclusive shift:

Make Sculptor your default for systemic barriers.

Before you coach an individual struggle, ask: Is this a person problem or a system problem?

If the system is broken, Coach won’t fix it. Only Sculptor will.

Gear-Switching Scenarios

Let’s show VOICES in action across common situations:

Sprint planning with ADHD product manager:

  • Start: Visionary (here’s where we’re going, here’s why it matters)

  • Middle: Sculptor (here’s the flexible structure that lets you innovate within guardrails)

  • End: Operator (here are the non-negotiable deliverables and dates)

Performance feedback with autistic analyst:

  • Start: Coach (I want to understand your experience first)

  • Middle: Inclusive (here’s what I’m observing – what am I missing?)

  • End: Operator (here’s what needs to change, and here’s exactly how to measure it)

Team pivot with mixed cognitive styles:

  • Announce: Command (we’re changing direction, here’s why)

  • Process: Sculptor (build 48-hour transition buffer, offer async processing time)

  • Engage: Consultative (I need your input on implementation after you’ve had time to think)

Different cognitive styles require different gear sequences – and most leaders never learn to shift.

The Turn: From Coaching Individuals to Sculpting Systems

Here’s what most neurodiversity training gets wrong:

It tries to teach leaders to be better Coaches or more Inclusive.

But the real work is Sculptor.

Because you can coach someone on “executive presence” all day long – but if your definition of executive presence is neurotypical communication performance, you haven’t helped them grow. You’ve just taught them to mask more convincingly.

You can create “inclusive” spaces where everyone’s voice is welcome – but if the only recognized mode of contribution is real-time verbal processing, you haven’t included everyone. You’ve just included the people whose brains happen to work that way already.

The shift happens when you stop asking, “How do I help neurodivergent employees succeed in my systems?”

And start asking, “What systems design would let all brains contribute without camouflage?”

That’s Sculptor work.

And it’s the gear most leaders never shift into – because it requires admitting that the systems you’ve built, the norms you’ve normalized, the “best practices” you’ve institutionalized might not be neutral.

They might just be familiar.

Integrating the Frameworks

We’ve tuned the CREATE engine for cognitive diversity. We’ve built the 6C navigation system for decision friction. Now let’s see how the VOICES gearbox integrates with both.

VOICES + CREATE: Which Gears Activate Which Cylinders

Let’s connect this back to CREATE – because each VOICES gear activates different parts of the leadership engine.

Sculptor shapes Culture and Execution. When you redesign norms and workflows, you’re not just being nice – you’re recalibrating how the whole system operates.

Inclusive protects Relationships and Ethics. When you create multiple pathways for contribution, you’re building trust and protecting the right to work differently.

Coach drives Teaching and Agility. When you tailor feedback and normalize iteration, you’re making growth sustainable – not just for high performers, but for all cognitive styles.

Visionary anchors Culture and Agility. When you pair vision with structural clarity, you create both meaning and momentum.

Operator powers Execution and Relationships. When you offer process options (not mandates), you create accountability and autonomy.

Encourager reinforces Culture and Teaching. When you celebrate outcomes (not optics), you signal what actually matters – and people learn what to optimize for.

The gears don’t operate in isolation. They work together – when you know which gear the moment needs.

VOICES + 6C: Matching Gears to Decision Modes

Different VOICES gears naturally pair with different 6C Decision Compass modes.

Visionary often uses Consultative – “Here’s where we’re going, I need your input on how we get there.”

Operator uses Command or Core Expertise – “Here’s the process” or “You own this domain, execute accordingly.”

Inclusive uses Collaborative or Consensus – “We’re building this together, everyone’s input shapes the outcome.”

Sculptor uses Choice – “Here’s the outcome and the guardrails. You decide how to get there.”

Coach uses Consultative – “I’m here to help you grow, but the learning is yours to own.”

Encourager doesn’t decide – it reinforces. It recognizes progress and contribution wherever it shows up.

The integration point: when you know which gear you’re in, you know which decision mode fits.

And when the whole team can name both, you’ve built a system where leadership doesn’t depend on one person holding all the context.

The VOICES Gear Shift Guide for Neurodivergent Teams

Let’s make this immediately usable.

Which gear each cognitive style needs most:

  • Autistic brains: Operator (clear systems) + Sculptor (redesign ambiguity out)

  • ADHD brains: Visionary (big picture + urgency) + Coach (tailored, kinetic feedback)

  • Dyslexic brains: Visionary (strategic narrative) + Inclusive (multiple input modes)

  • Anxiety-driven brains: Operator (predictability) + Encourager (progress recognition)

Which gear creates friction for which brain type:

  • Autistic: Visionary without milestones creates untethered anxiety

  • ADHD: Operator without autonomy kills momentum

  • Dyslexic: Operator without context feels micromanaged

  • Anxiety-driven: Inclusive without structure creates decision paralysis

When to shift gears based on cognitive load, not just task type:

  • High cognitive load (transitions, crises, ambiguity) → Shift to Operator (clarity) or Sculptor (redesign the stressor)

  • Low cognitive load (steady state, routine work) → Shift to Coach (growth) or Encourager (recognition)

The “Sculptor First” principle:

Before you Coach an individual struggle, check if the system needs redesign.

If three people are struggling with the same thing, it’s not three people problems. It’s one system problem.

Sculptor first. Then Coach for individual growth within redesigned systems.

Self-Diagnostic: Which Gears Are You Stuck In?

Look at your calendar from the last two weeks:

  • How many meetings started with vision but had no concrete next steps? (Visionary overuse)

  • How many times did you specify exact processes rather than outcomes? (Operator overuse)

  • When did you last redesign a system rather than coach an individual? (Sculptor underuse)

Ask your team: “What do you need from me that I’m not giving you?” Their answer will tell you which gear you’re avoiding.

Pattern recognition: If you keep coaching the same struggle across multiple people, that’s your signal to shift to Sculptor.

Three Diagnostic Questions

Before we close, three questions to surface which gears you’re overusing – and which you’re avoiding:

1. When was the last time you shifted out of Visionary or Operator mode – and what gear did you shift into?

If you can’t remember, you’re stuck in two gears. That’s not bad leadership – it’s predictable leadership. And predictable leadership can’t read the room well enough to meet different brains where they work.

2. Which gear makes you most uncomfortable – and is that the gear your team needs most right now?

Leaders avoid gears that don’t feel natural. But if your team is struggling and you’re only using your comfortable gears, you’re not leading adaptively. You’re leading rigidly and hoping people adjust. Ask your team: “What do you need from me that I’m not giving you?” Their answer will tell you which gear you’re avoiding.

3. Are you trying to Coach someone through a problem that requires Sculptor work?

If you keep coaching the same struggle across multiple people, stop coaching. Start redesigning. Individual adaptation can’t fix systemic barriers. Sculptor first, then Coach for growth within better-designed systems.

There’s no such thing as one leadership style that works for everyone.

Just leaders who know how to shift gears – and leaders who don’t.

The question isn’t which gear you prefer.

It’s whether you can read what the person in front of you needs right now – and toggle accordingly.

Because neuroinclusive leadership isn’t about being all things to all people.

It’s about having enough gears that you can meet different brains where they actually work.

And when you do, you don’t just get better morale.

You get better outcomes – because you’ve unlocked cognitive capacity that rigid, one-gear leadership leaves on the table.

VOICES Gear Flexibility Self-Assessment

Rate your agreement with each statement on a scale of 1-5: (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree)

1. I can clearly identify which VOICES gear I’m using in real-time and shift gears based on what the person or situation needs.

  • 1 = Stuck in 1-2 gears / 2 = Limited shifting / 3 = Some flexibility / 4 = Good flexibility / 5 = Fluid gear-shifting

2. When I see individual struggles repeated across multiple people, my first response is to redesign the system (Sculptor) rather than coach individuals.

  • 1 = Always coach / 2 = Usually coach / 3 = Mixed / 4 = Usually redesign / 5 = Always redesign first

3. I adapt my feedback delivery (Coach gear) to match how each person processes information, not just my preferred style.

  • 1 = One format / 2 = Minimal adaptation / 3 = Some adaptation / 4 = Regular adaptation / 5 = Fully personalized

4. I can recognize when vision (Visionary) creates anxiety rather than clarity for certain brain types, and pair it with structure.

  • 1 = Rarely recognize / 2 = Sometimes / 3 = Often / 4 = Usually / 5 = Always

5. When I recognize outcomes (Encourager), I celebrate impact and specific contributions rather than performance of engagement.

  • 1 = Celebrate performance / 2 = Mostly performance / 3 = Mixed / 4 = Mostly impact / 5 = Impact-focused

6. I use all six VOICES gears regularly, not just my comfortable 1-2 gears.

  • 1 = Use 1-2 only / 2 = Use 2-3 / 3 = Use 3-4 / 4 = Use 4-5 / 5 = Use all 6

Scoring Guide:

25-30 points: You’re likely shifting gears fluidly. Consider: Can you teach this flexibility to other leaders? What makes gear-shifting natural for you?

18-24 points: You’re expanding beyond 1-2 gears but still have underused gears. Consider: Which gear scored lowest? That’s likely the gear your team needs most from you right now.

12-17 points: You’re probably stuck in Visionary + Operator mode. Consider: Try Sculptor next time you see a repeated individual struggle – redesign the system, not the person.

6-11 points: Limited gear flexibility is likely creating friction. Consider: Ask your team “What do you need from me that I’m not giving you?” – their answer reveals which gears you’re avoiding.

Next Steps Based on Your Score:

If you scored 20+:

  • Document which gear sequences work for which situations

  • Mentor other leaders on gear flexibility

  • Identify: which gear do you still underuse?

If you scored 12-19:

  • Identify your lowest-scoring gear

  • Use it deliberately in your next three leadership interactions

  • Notice: what changes in team response?

If you scored below 12:

  • Start with Sculptor: identify one systemic barrier and redesign it

  • Before your next 1:1: ask “How do you receive feedback best?” and honor it (Coach)

  • When you recognize good work: name the specific contribution, not the person’s “energy” (Encourager)

Regardless of score: Read Essay 7 to learn how to design workflows that work for all brains from the ground up – because even with gear flexibility, you need systems architecture that doesn’t require constant leadership intervention.
