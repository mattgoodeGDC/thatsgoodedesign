One Year Later: What USAID Meant, and What Remains

January 2026

Matt Goode21 January 202616 min read Get new pieces by email

# Off the clock

Honestly, I don’t remember the exact date. I just remember I was at my desk when the Stop Work Order came in around 9 am. For a while, we stood around seeking guidance. What did that mean? Could we, starting when… But, having a sinking suspicion that I might not be coming back for a while, they said it would initially be a 90-day review. I packed up two Wegman’s bags and my backpack of all the personal items from my desk – I keep a pair of running shoes, an extra toothbrush, a Lego set of suculants, etc. – and, with another colleague, took the MARC train home in the middle of the morning.

A year ago this month, I lost my job.

That sentence is both true and insufficient. What I lost was more complicated than employment – and what I’ve gained since has been stranger and richer than I could have imagined while standing in the wreckage.

I was a leadership development specialist at USAID’s Bureau of Humanitarian Assistance. I facilitated retreats and training sessions, helped teams navigate conflict, built frameworks that let people work together more effectively under impossible pressure. Behind the scenes, I focused on strengthening organizational effectiveness – streamlining processes, bridging the gap between strategic planning and on-the-ground execution, empowering forward-facing teams to deliver life-saving aid in rapidly changing crises. I had completed CBRNE (Chemical, Biological, Radiological, Nuclear, and high-yield Explosives) training, and some SHOP training we were “not supposed to talk about” – IYKYK, lol – in preparing for overseas deployments that would never come. Later that spring, I was planning to go to Thailand for a temporary duty assignment. I was ready to represent the United States in the field, fulfilling the mission stamped on every sack of flour and every shipment of medicine: From the American People.

Then DOGE came, and that mission ended – not with a policy debate or a budget negotiation, but with mass emails, locked systems, and the quiet violence of being erased.

$36.18

That’s how much the average American taxpayer contributed to USAID each year – at least by one calculation. Other estimates put it at $64 per person (about 17 cents a day, according to researchers at UCLA), or around $105 per citizen, according to Oxfam. The numbers vary depending on the year and what you count. But by any measure, less than a streaming subscription. Less than a single dinner out.

For that sum, we fed children in famine zones. We vaccinated communities against preventable diseases. We trained nurses, engineers, and teachers. We responded to earthquakes, floods, and the slow-motion disasters of conflict and displacement. We helped countries become trading partners instead of aid recipients. We did the work that aircraft carriers cannot do.

But I understand that abstractions don’t move people. Policy arguments bounce off the armor of partisan identity. What moves people is something else.

The Flour

My former supervisor, Lika, grew up in Georgia during the chaos of the early 1990s. Civil war. Regional conflicts. Banks collapsed overnight. Her family lost everything and fled, eventually cramming more than twenty people into a single house belonging to distant relatives.

What kept them alive? Large sacks of wheat flour stamped with the USAID logo. From the American People. It wasn’t luxurious, Lika wrote. But it kept them alive.

Years later, by chance or fate, she found herself working for USAID. “It was more than a job,” she said. “It was a full-circle moment.”

In the comments on her post, another colleague, Linda, shared her own story. South Sudan in the early 1990s. Conflict and genocide. Her father killed. Her mother walked the children to the Kenyan border, where USAID was building a refugee camp. They received food once a day – sometimes only two or three times a week – but the generosity of the American people literally saved her family’s life.

She eventually came to the United States as a refugee. Years later, she walked alongside USAID as a partner in the work.

This is what the agency was. Not an abstraction. Not a line item. A circle of survival and service that stretched across decades and continents, binding together the people who received aid and the people who delivered it – often the same people, at different points in their lives.

Being Someone’s First American

I often think about soft power. Not as a policy concept, but as a human reality.

For more than half the world, the only interaction they will ever have with an American is someone helping them at their most vulnerable moment. A USAID worker arriving with food after a flood. A health worker administering vaccines. A shelter specialist teaches local partners how to rig tarps for displaced families.

That is who we are to them. That is America, in the only form they will ever encounter it.

I learned this the other way around. During graduate school abroad, I was often the first American my classmates had ever known in any meaningful way – not as a tourist or a TV character, but as a roommate, a friend, a person who stayed up late arguing about politics and history and what it means to belong somewhere.

They asked hard questions. About slavery, about gun violence, about why healthcare was tied to employment, about why we seemed so divided by color and zip code and party. I didn’t always have answers. But I learned that presence – being willing to sit in the complexity without rushing to defend or explain – was often more valuable than certainty.

Some of them later told me those conversations shifted their views. Knowing me complicated the caricatures they’d grown up with. They saw the U.S. as a more human, nuanced, and self-critical place than the headlines suggested.

We don’t always realize we’re acting as ambassadors. But when you’re someone’s first American, you are.

USAID understood this. The agency was, in its best moments, a machine for creating those encounters at scale – millions of moments where America showed up not with weapons but with water, not with demands but with aid when they needed it most.

The Destruction

The dismantling of USAID wasn’t policy. It was performance art.

Elon Musk arrived in Washington with a chainsaw – literally, he held one up at CPAC – and a promise to cut $2 trillion from the federal budget. He called USAID “a criminal organization.” He said he would put it “through the wood chipper.” President Trump claimed the agency was run by “radical left lunatics” and rife with “tremendous fraud.”

The fraud was never found. Independent analysis after independent analysis confirmed what those of us who worked there already knew: USAID’s programs were rigorously evaluated, meticulously documented, and subject to constant scrutiny. But evidence didn’t matter. The spectacle did.

Fraud. Waste. Abuse. They said those words like an incantation, as if repetition made them true. But here we are, one year later, and I have a simple question:

Where are the indictments?

If USAID were a “criminal organization,” where are the prosecutions? If there was “tremendous fraud,” where are the charges? If billions were stolen or misspent, where are the perp walks, the court dates, the convictions? They had full access to every system, every record, every email. They sent teenagers with security concerns into our databases. They claimed to have found corruption at the highest levels.

And yet: nothing. No indictments. No trials. No evidence was presented in any court of law.

Because there was no fraud. There was only a story they needed to tell to justify destruction.

In fact, JUST THIS MORNING (1/20/26), media outlets, including Politico, are reporting that Elon Musk's DOGE team at the Social Security Administration was in contact with an advocacy group seeking to overturn election results and may have used SSA data to match voter rolls, leading to Hatch Act referrals. The filing also disclosed that DOGE personnel stored data on unapproved third-party servers and briefly accessed private information a court had ruled off-limits. Fraud, waste, and abuse indeed.

Among Musk’s handpicked operatives was a 19-year-old high school graduate named Edward Coristine, known online by his chosen nickname: “Big Balls.” Before joining DOGE, he had been fired from a cybersecurity internship after concerns about his handling of sensitive data. A former FBI agent told reporters he would not have recommended Coristine for government work. No matter. He was given access to Treasury Department payment systems, Homeland Security databases, and FEMA records. When reporters identified him, Musk accused the media of “doxxing” his team member.

This was who they sent to dismantle sixty years of American humanitarian leadership.

The chaos was immediate. DOGE’s “wall of receipts” – a website meant to document savings – was riddled with errors from day one. A USAID contract worth $655 million was listed three times, inflating claimed savings by nearly $2 billion. A Social Security contract that was never actually terminated was counted as a quarter-billion in savings. An $8 million ICE contract was entered as $8 billion – a typo that briefly made headlines before being quietly corrected to zero.

The New York Times investigated the 40 largest claimed savings for DOGE. Only twelve appeared to be accurate.

Musk had promised $2 trillion in cuts. Then $1 trillion. Then $150 billion. By April, independent analysts at NPR and the Manhattan Institute estimated that DOGE had generated approximately $2 billion in actual savings – less than one-tenth of 1 percent of federal spending. As budget expert Jessica Riedl put it: “Think of Congress and its budget as the debt-ridden dad on the way to buy a $250,000 Ferrari on the credit card, and DOGE is the $2 off gas card he used along the way.”

But here’s what makes the math obscene.

The Treasury Department and IRS officials warned that DOGE-driven workforce reductions could result in a 10% drop in tax receipts – equivalent to over $500 billion in lost federal revenue. The Partnership for Public Service estimated that DOGE’s disruptions would cost taxpayers $135 billion in 2025 alone, due to productivity losses, paid leave, and the costs of firing and rehiring employees. A Senate subcommittee found DOGE had generated over $21.7 billion in waste across federal agencies. The administration that promised efficiency may have created the most expensive reorganization in federal history.

And that’s just the money.

The Body Count

A study published in The Lancet in June 2025 estimated that USAID programs had prevented over 91 million deaths between 2001 and 2021. Ninety-one million people who lived because America showed up.

The same researchers projected that if the cuts remained in place through 2030, between 8 and 19 million additional people would die – including 4.5 million children under five. That’s roughly 700,000 extra child deaths per year. The equivalent of a major pandemic or armed conflict, except this one was, in the researchers’ words, “a conscious and avoidable policy choice.”

By May 2025, Professor Brooke Nichols at Boston University had built a real-time tracker estimating deaths from the aid cuts. Her counter showed one person dying from tuberculosis every 7.7 minutes. Over 176,000 projected deaths from HIV if funding wasn’t restored. By the time Musk departed DOGE at the end of May, her estimates suggested 300,000 people had already died as a result of the cuts – mostly children.

UN human rights experts called it “a humanitarian emergency, not a policy adjustment.” They wrote: “The poorest people in the world are dying by the minute as a result of opaque decision-making by some of the richest people to have ever walked our planet.”

Close to 100 people have been dying every hour since January 20, 2025.

For what? If you distributed DOGE’s verified $2 billion in savings to every American, each person would receive about $6. Not enough for a fast-food meal. That’s the trade: six dollars per person, and hundreds of thousands dead.

The Erasure

When the administration dismantled USAID, they didn’t just cut programs. They deleted the evidence.

My colleague Michele, writing while being forced to repatriate from an overseas assignment, put it plainly: “USAID evaluations were wiped from public access and erased by DOGE. All of our monitoring and evaluation work was canceled. Now, taxpayers can’t even judge USAID’s impact for themselves.”

Her team had been about to launch a multi-phase evaluation of a five-year conflict prevention program in Mozambique. The work was hard. It required constant analysis and scrutiny. That was the point. That was how you learned what worked and what didn’t.

Now that the records are gone. You cannot defend what you cannot cite. The destruction of the institutional memory wasn’t collateral damage – it was the goal. If there’s no evidence of success, you can claim there was no success. If there’s no documentation, you can claim fraud without proving fraud.

They called us crooks. They called the agency a criminal organization.

We were the best of us.

On June 30, 2025 – the last day of USAID as an independent agency – former Presidents Bush and Obama broke their public silence. A World Food Program official sobbed through a vow that the mission would return someday. And Bono, of all people, recited a poem he had written for the occasion.

“They called you crooks,” he said. “When you were the best of us.”

The People

I haven’t mentioned them enough. The colleagues. The team.

Paul. Hasina. Kyle. Annie. Jan. Ashley. Lee. Kevin. Maggie. Elizabeth. Chris. Sanyika. Gina. Wayan. Arlene. Nancy. Kai. Gia. Aleksandra. Athena. Alex. Nancy. Sophia. There are too many to name – and I’m leaving out dozens who deserve to be here. People who showed up after midnight because of a crisis didn’t care about time zones. People who left their families for weeks to deploy to places most Americans will never see. People who cried in private and kept working in public because the work couldn’t wait.

I worked alongside individuals ranging from political appointees to technical experts in fields as varied as breastfeeding support, volcanology, civil engineering, pharmacy, nursing, and security. I was among the few white native-born males on my team – which underscored the rich diversity of perspectives and experiences we all brought. Many of my teammates were first-generation Americans or naturalized citizens, each contributing to a workforce that represented the world and exemplified what makes America great.

One of my most personal contributions was working with the Shelter and Settlement teams. It brought back memories of backpacking adventures and the simple yet vital skills I’d learned along the way. I had the opportunity to demonstrate practical tarp setup techniques – specific knots, anchoring methods – with colleagues, adapting these ideas for humanitarian use in Gaza. A small, almost imperceptible gesture. But it symbolized our collective commitment to alleviating human suffering.

Gaza was tearing us apart from the inside.

We watched the famine conditions emerge. We knew what was needed: food, shelter, medical supplies, the basics of keeping people alive. We had the expertise. We had the relationships. We had the logistics. And the Biden administration kept pumping the brakes on what we could do – caught between humanitarian imperatives and political constraints that none of us controlled. It was agonizing to have the capacity to help and be told to wait.

That was before DOGE. Before the new administration decided the answer wasn’t to do more, but to do nothing at all.

After the RIF scattered us, we created a WhatsApp group called DC DART – our own disaster response team - for the disaster that had happened to us. DART – Disaster Assistance Response Team – was the name USAID gave to the teams that deployed to humanitarian crises. The 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami. The 2010 Haiti earthquake. Syria. Yemen. Ukraine. And Gaza.

We shared job leads, legal updates, and gallows humor. We checked in on each other during the court battles and the headlines. We mourned together when the June 30 deadline arrived.

We are still in that group. We are still a team.

Institutions can be dismantled. But the people who made them work don’t disappear. They scatter, regroup, and carry the mission forward in whatever form they can. We keep in touch.

The Vest

In the weeks after the RIF, I did something small and perhaps foolish. I started wearing my USAID vest and baseball cap everywhere – to Walmart, to the grocery store, around my neighborhood in Maryland.

I wanted people to know that we were not an abstract concept. We were their neighbors.

One day, a man in a Ravens camo hoodie – the kind with the American flag on the sleeve, a tribute to soldiers – gave me a long look and asked: “You work at USAID? What do you think about what’s going on?” he asked jeeringly.

I had prepared for this. I asked if he was a Trump voter. He confirmed with enthusiasm.

I didn’t argue. I asked questions instead.

  • Have you ever been forced from your home in the middle of the night by gunmen?

  • Have you ever watched your child starve, powerless to help?

  • Have you ever lost everything to a wall of water or an earthquake?

Then I gave him numbers. 108 million people were displaced by conflict and disaster last year. 1.5 million people die annually from waterborne illness. 270 million dead from malaria over the past century – more than all the wars combined.

And I told him what USAID does. We save babies from preventable deaths. We provide clean water. We support American farmers. We prevent costly military interventions. Every dollar spent on aid is a dollar not spent on bullets – and an American kid not coming home in a body bag.

All of this for somewhere between $36 and $105 per American per year, depending on how you count it.

I don’t know if I changed his mind. But I know he listened. And I know that showing up – being visible, being willing to have the conversation – is its own form of resistance.

What I Built

A year ago, I was in shock. I wrote a piece three days after the announcement (so I guess I could figure out the exact date, but choose not to do so), full of grief and unfulfilled longing. I had trained for overseas deployment and never got to go. The mission I’d spent my whole life preparing for – growing up on military bases, attending foreign schools – ended before it could fully begin.

But grief, if you let it, becomes fuel.

In the twelve months since I started my own consulting practice, Goode Design & Consulting, focused on neuroinclusive leadership and organizational systems. I became a published author. Just recently, I completed a 28,000-word essay series on neuroinclusive leadership. I wrote about rejection and resilience, about international students and soft power, about what we owe each other in moments of crisis.

And I finished a novel – a romance I had promised my wife I would write twenty years ago. I wanted it done before the one-year anniversary. Before the calendar could define what that year meant.

The RIF gave me something back, even as it took. Not a silver lining – I don’t believe in those. Something harder to name. Maybe just time. And I used it.

What Remains

I still believe what I believed a year ago: that USAID made America safer, stronger, and more prosperous – and that in the end, money spent on aid was cheaper than Americans coming home in body bags. That the work mattered. That the people I served alongside – first-generation Americans, naturalized citizens from Poland and France and Afghanistan and Mali and Jamaica and Thailand – represented the best of what this country can be.

I believe that sooner or later, leaders on both sides of the aisle will realize how much that work was needed. The crises haven’t stopped. The displaced haven’t gone home. The diseases haven’t disappeared. The need is still there, and it will eventually demand a response.

But I also know that institutions can be destroyed faster than they can be rebuilt. That knowledge can be erased. That the people who knew how to do the work have scattered to other jobs, other countries, other lives.

Many of us are still unemployed. Others are vastly underemployed – taking whatever work pays the bills while the skills we spent decades developing sit unused. Some have switched career sectors entirely because there isn’t much market demand for niche specialists in maternal health programming, water and sanitation systems, or agricultural development. The expertise that took a generation to build is dispersing, and some of it will never come back.

But there are inspirations too. Former colleagues are starting new businesses. Moving to new cities. Finding ways to continue the mission in different forms – consulting, nonprofits, local government, teaching. The work didn’t stop; it just went underground, became smaller, and became personal.

And here’s the cruel irony that keeps me up at night: all those humanitarians who dedicated their lives to saving lives and alleviating trauma in others – the people who showed up after earthquakes and famines and wars, who held space for survivors, who built systems to help communities heal – now have experienced their own trauma. The helpers need help. The people who specialized in recovery are now the ones who need to recover.

Some of them, like Lika and Linda, carry the memory of what American aid meant in their own bodies – flour that kept them alive, camps that sheltered their families. That memory doesn’t disappear when an agency is dissolved. It persists. It waits.

And when the time comes to rebuild – if that time comes – will we even be able to? So much technical and experiential expertise has been lost. The volcanologists who knew which communities sat in lahar zones. The epidemiologists who could read the early signs of outbreak. The logistics specialists who knew which ports worked and which roads flooded in rainy season. The relationship-builders who had earned trust over decades with local partners. You can’t Google that. You can’t train it in a weekend. You can’t rebuild it with a new appropriation.

Those of us who remember will be ready. But memory alone may not be enough.

One year later, I still wear the vest. I’m also clearly and willfully breaking the Hatch Act by also wearing a “VOTE” pin under the phrase “From the American People.”

** Update: It's not the brown lightweight field vest. That’d be gauche. It is a dark blue winter vest.
