What Are You Practicing?

Matt Goode2 April 202611 min read Get new pieces by email

# Coaching

This week I’ve been in Greece, in Rome, in the oldest texts the Western canon has to offer. Monday, I argued that the humanities produce formation, not skills – lessons built into the structure of how a person sees, not installed by a workshop or certified by a competency model. Tuesday, Plato showed us how organizations systematically select against the people who did that formation. Wednesday, Sophocles showed us three ways good leaders fail inside systems that couldn’t develop them, couldn’t deliver truth to them, couldn’t create the conditions to receive it. Thursday, Homer showed us what those failures cost – not the leaders at the top, but the people underneath them who had no part in creating the problem and no protection from its consequences.

All of it was outside. Systems. Structures. Organizational architecture.

Today I want to turn inward. Because there is one system none of those frameworks can build for you.

A year ago, I was sitting at my desk when a Stop Work Order came in at around 9 am.

I packed two Wegmans bags and a backpack of personal items – running shoes, a toothbrush, a Lego set of succulents – and took the MARC train home in the middle of the morning. I had been a leadership development specialist at USAID’s Bureau of Humanitarian Assistance. I had spent years building something I believed in – frameworks that let people work together more effectively under impossible pressure, systems that helped teams navigate conflict and deliver life-saving aid in rapidly changing crises. I had been preparing for an overseas deployment to Thailand. The work felt necessary and real and mine.

And then it was gone. Not slowly. All at once.

I remember sitting on that train not knowing what came next. That is the honest version – not that I immediately pivoted, not that the path forward was clear, not that the loss resolved itself into opportunity with any kind of speed. There was a period of not knowing. Of sitting inside the uncertainty without a framework for it. Of encountering, for the first time in a long time, a situation that my professional formation had no answer for. I had spent years helping other people navigate impossible pressure. Now the pressure was mine, and the frameworks were someone else’s problem.

I started reading the Stoics. Not for comfort. I was not looking for comfort. I was looking for a philosophy that was honest about the nature of uncontrollable events and rigorous – unsparing, actually – about what to do with them.

The real thing is considerably more demanding than its reputation suggests.

Here is what most people get wrong about Meditations.

They read it as a journal of self-improvement. Marcus Aurelius’s bullet journal. A Roman emperor who was very concerned with being more disciplined, more patient, more resilient. All true. All beside the point.

Meditations is a working document written by a man running an empire under conditions of continuous and compounding uncertainty. Plague. War. Political betrayal. Economic instability. Marcus spent most of his reign on campaign – not because he wanted to be a soldier but because the Danube frontier kept requiring his presence and the frontier didn’t care about his preferences. He wrote Meditations in military camps, in the margins of an impossible job, not as philosophy for posterity but as operational instruction to himself. Notes for how to function when the external environment is fundamentally uncontrollable and the only reliable system is your own response architecture.

That is not self-help. That is systems design for the interior life.

The distinction matters enormously for how you read him – and what you take from him into a leadership context.

The central architecture of Stoic philosophy – the thing Marcus returns to throughout Meditations in different forms, in different registers, with different degrees of exhaustion and conviction – is the dichotomy of control.

There are things within your control and things outside it.

The Stoics are not gentle about this distinction. What is outside your control is almost everything: the market, the behavior of other people, the decisions your board makes, the timing of a recession, whether the plague arrives in your province this winter or the next. What is within your control is almost nothing: your judgments, your intentions, your responses. The space between stimulus and response. That is the entire inventory.

When I first encountered this seriously – not as a quotation on a productivity website but as a working philosophy in the months after USAID – it was not reassuring. It was confrontational. It said: the situation you are in is not something you get to spend your interior resources on. The Stop Work Order, the dismantling of the work, the question of whether any of it would be preserved – those are outside your control. You do not get to carry them. You get to carry only what you choose to do next. It was a message I knew enough about myself that needed to hear in that moment.

That is a hard thing to hear when what you want to do is grieve the thing that was lost. The Stoics don’t say you can’t grieve. They say: grieve precisely, and then return to the question of what is actually yours to govern. Because the time you spend inside what you cannot control is time stolen from the only system you can actually build.

Most leadership development spends its energy on the things outside that inventory. How to influence others. How to manage up. How to build culture. How to design systems that produce the outcomes you want. All of it important. All of it operating in the territory Marcus spent his life clarifying as ultimately beyond your control.

He wasn’t arguing for passivity. He was arguing for precision. Stop spending interior resources on the things you cannot govern. Spend them entirely on the one thing you can.

That is not a meditation technique. That is operational infrastructure.

In the months after USAID, I started building GDC. I dusted off a manuscript I had written years earlier as a private gift to G – a promise made thirty years ago, finally kept – revised it, and published it as Balance, my first novel. I consolidated a decade of leadership frameworks into a “vehicle metaphor” – cyclinders (CREATE), gears (VOICES), navigation (6D) – and expanded that into a book: CLUTCH. And I kept tinkering with the long-running personal project that refuses to finish – a biography of the number Seven, which requires the kind of deep research that fiction mercifully does not. When you’re writing fiction, you can just make things up. Seven keeps demanding that I actually know things. That tinkering eventually became something more serious, and I started rereading every translation of the Iliad I could find – which became The Wrath of Archie Ellis, which became Thursday’s essay.

None of those things were available to me while I was spending interior resources on questions outside my control. The Stoics were not sympathetic about this. They would say: the months you spent asking whether the institution made the right decision were months that belonged to the work you haven’t built yet.

I think they were right. I also think it took longer than they would have approved of to get there.

Marcus writes: You have power over your mind, not outside events. Realize this, and you will find strength.

The translation flattens it slightly. What he actually means is more structural than inspirational: the architecture of your interior life is the only architecture you will ever fully own. Build it well. Maintain it deliberately. Don’t confuse it with the architecture of the external world, which you can influence but never control.

Here is where Marcus connects to everything this week has been about – and the connection is not a framework. It is a question.

Think about the Oedipus problem from Wednesday. The leader who is the last to know what everyone around them already knows. We spent time analyzing what the organization failed to build – the systems that make truth travel upward, the conditions that make dissent survivable. All of that analysis is real, and the infrastructure is worth building.

But Marcus would ask something prior to all of it: what happens inside the leader when the truth finally arrives?

Because the infrastructure can be perfect – the environment genuinely safe, the people genuinely willing – and the leader can still flinch. Can still make the person who delivered the difficult information pay a subtle cost that everyone in the room notices and no one names. Can still reach for the story that protects them over the story that’s true. Not because they are malicious. Because the interior architecture wasn’t built to receive what the external system finally delivered.

A leader with a developed interior architecture meets difficult information the same way Marcus met the Danube frontier: not with equanimity as a performance, but with equanimity as a practice. Not “I am fine with this” but “I have built the capacity to remain curious in the presence of things I would prefer not to be true – including things about myself.”

That capacity is not installed by a framework. It is built by practice. Daily, ordinary, unglamorous practice. In every small moment before the large ones arrive.

Which brings us to the question Marcus returns to constantly, in various forms, across all twelve books of Meditations: what are you practicing?

Not what are your values? Not what do you believe? What are you actually doing, day by day, in the ordinary moments before the pressure arrives?

A line attributed to the ancient Greek warrior-poet Archilochus has been passed through enough hands that no one checks the source anymore. Navy SEALs adopted it. David Goggins repeated it. James Clear adapted it for Atomic Habits – swapping "training" for "systems," which is actually the more useful frame. The Stoics understood the same thing two millennia ago: you do not rise to the occasion. You fall to the level of your preparation. Everyone who has touched this idea was talking about individuals. The response architecture available to you in the hardest moment is the one you have been building in every moment before it. The question for organizations is whether the system was designed to build that architecture – or just to assume it arrives fully formed.

You are always practicing something. The question is whether it’s what you intend.

Marcus kept writing in the military camps, not because the philosophy was incomplete. Because the practice required daily maintenance. Because the interior architecture, like any infrastructure, degrades without attention. Because even an emperor – perhaps especially an emperor – needed to return every morning to the same fundamental questions: what is within my control today, what is not, and where am I spending my interior resources?

I return to those questions. Not every day – I am not Marcus Aurelius, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. But often enough that they have become part of how I orient myself when the external environment is uncontrollable, which in my experience is most of the time.

He didn’t always succeed. He kept writing anyway.

He was working in one tradition. There are others…

I have a tattoo: Datta Dayadhvam Damyata.

Sanskrit, from the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad – three instructions spoken by Prajapati to three orders of being, each hearing what they most need to hear. T.S. Eliot borrowed it for the closing of The Waste Land, which is where I first encountered it, though the words are several thousand years older than Eliot. I had it put on my skin because I wanted it somewhere I couldn’t put it down. Because the things worth practicing are the things worth making permanent. Because some arguments are too important to keep only in books.

Give. Sympathize. Practice control.

Three words. The whole argument in a different tradition and a different millennium. And – I’ll say it plainly – the whole argument this series has been making, compressed into a form old enough to predate every framework, every play, every poem I’ve cited this week.

Give – the outward orientation, the recognition that leadership is not accumulation but distribution. That kleos stolen is kleos lost. That the systems worth building are the ones that develop other people’s capacity, not protect your own position. The lesson narrative intelligence was pointing toward all along: the story you tell about your people is infrastructure.

Sympathize – the interior capacity to feel the situation accurately. To read what Achilles actually needs before the embassy fails. To hear what your team knows before the culture makes silence rational. To receive difficult truth with curiosity rather than contracting around it. Deep listening, moral imagination, tolerance for ambiguity – all of them are downstream of this one word.

Practice control – not control of the external environment, which Marcus spent twelve books clarifying is not yours to govern, but control of the response. The space between the stimulus and the action. The only inventory that is genuinely yours. The one system no framework can build for you.

This week, I was in Greece, in Rome, in the ancient world’s most enduring literature, looking for what the humanities give a leader that the MBA doesn’t. I found it in the same place across every tradition I visited: the insistence that the external architecture and the interior architecture are not separate problems. That you cannot build the systems without building the person operating them. That the formation is the foundation. That what you practice in the ordinary moments is what will be available to you in the extraordinary ones.

The Rolling Stone piece called these things skills. Five of them, neatly listed, teachable in the right program. This week I’ve been arguing something different – that they are not skills but lessons, not acquired but formed, not installed but lived. Formation doesn’t appear on a resume. It shows up in the moment when the information is incomplete, the stakes are real, and the response architecture is either there or it isn’t.

Give. Sympathize. Practice control.

Not skills. Scars.

I want to know your three words – the principle, the phrase, the thing you’ve put somewhere you can’t put it down. Reply and tell me. I read every response.

And if you’re building both the external infrastructure and the interior architecture – that’s the work I do. Start with CLUTCH…
