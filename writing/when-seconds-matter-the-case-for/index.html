When Seconds Matter: The Case for Command

Matt Goode2 July 20263 min read Get new pieces by email

# 6C# VOICES

Picture the worst ninety seconds of your year. The outage is live, the customer is on the line, the room turns to you – and you say, “What does everyone think?”

It’s a reasonable instinct. It’s also, in that moment, the wrong one. A crisis is the one situation where gathering opinions doesn’t feel like leadership; it feels like the absence of it. The room didn’t turn to you for a discussion. It turned to you for a direction.

What a crisis actually asks for

Under acute stress, something physical happens to a team. Attention narrows. Working memory shrinks. People stop generating options and start scanning for instructions they can execute right now. In that state, a wide-open “what should we do?” doesn’t unlock the room’s intelligence – it floods it. What steadies people isn’t more input. It’s certainty: a clear call, clearly owned, so everyone can stop deliberating and start moving. In a crisis, teams don’t need consensus. They need certainty.

The case for Command

This is Command, the first mode on the 6C Compass: you make the call, fast, and you direct the response. It has a bad reputation in modern leadership, and most of the time it earns it. But there’s a reason it exists. When seconds matter, and hesitation costs more than a wrong guess, Command isn’t heavy-handed – it’s the most humane thing you can do, because it ends the paralysis and gives people something to do with their fear.

Apollo 13 is the cleanest example I know. When the spacecraft was crippled, Mission Control didn’t hold a vote. Gene Kranz took the room: here’s the situation, here’s who’s working what, here’s the sequence. He didn’t have the answer – nobody did – but he owned the call about how the room would find it. Command isn’t pretending to be certain. It’s taking responsibility for the next move, so the people around you aren’t frozen waiting for permission.

Image: When Seconds Matter - The Case for Command - graph 1.png Caption: Consensus vs Command – when seconds matter, the room needs certainty, not a vote.

When Command becomes a trap

Here’s the catch, and it’s the reason Command earns its bad name. The same mode that saves a crisis wrecks an ordinary Tuesday. Run Command when there’s no fire and you teach a capable team to stop thinking, wait for orders, and bring you every call – which manufactures the exact dependence the rest of this book is trying to dissolve. Command is a fire extinguisher: indispensable in a fire, absurd as a daily tool.

So the skill isn’t being decisive, and it isn’t being collaborative. It’s reading which situation you’re actually in – and naming the mode out loud, so the team knows whether this is a moment to execute your call or to make their own.

Worth sitting with this week:

  • When a real crisis hit recently, did you reach for certainty or for consensus – and which did the moment need?

  • Where are you running Command on decisions that aren’t fires, and training your team to wait?

  • Could your team tell the difference between “this one’s mine to direct” and “this one’s yours”?

Command is one mode on the 6C Compass. Knowing when to reach for it – and when reaching for it is the mistake – is the whole skill. The full Compass, all six modes, lives in CLUTCH.
