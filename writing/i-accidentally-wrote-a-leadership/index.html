I Accidentally Wrote a Leadership Book. It’s a Romance Novel.

Matt Goode18 February 20268 min read Get new pieces by email

# Off the clock

This year, I wrote three books.

The first one – CLUTCH – is the one that makes sense. It’s the leadership book I’ve been building toward for years, drawn from every coaching engagement, every organizational redesign, every founder who called me because their company was growing faster than their ability to lead it. That one drops March 15 – the Ides of March. The original case study in what happens when a leader mistakes the system for himself.

The second is a biography of the number Seven. Prime, indivisible, resistant to simplification – a number that shows up across myths and maps and memory systems, not because the universe required it, but because we did. That one’s still in progress. It’s weird and wonderful, and I can’t stop working on it. This one is my personal passion project.

The third is a love-triangle romance novel.

I set out to write a love story – under a pen name, with a thin veil of deniability and, if I’m honest, embarrassment. The market for romance novels written by fifty-two-year-old men is, shall we say, unproven. I’ll out myself eventually. Probably when Dan Savage wants to interview me.

It started as a promise to my wife. She’d been saying for years that I should write fiction, and at some point, I ran out of excuses. It was my Misogi project for 2024. And I delivered a single, Kinkos-printed and bound version later that year. But she insisted it was good and that I should actually publish it. What I didn’t expect was the journey it would take me on. I read more fiction in the past year than in the previous ten combined. I read craft books and literary journals. I read Esther Perel and fell down rabbit holes in Slate and The Atlantic about how modern relationships actually work. So, when I was RIF’ed from USAID in February of last year, I suddenly had a lot of free time…

The book is about a couple who stop being careful with each other and start being honest instead. About what happens when love turns out to be bigger and stranger than the shape (a triangle in this case – cue New Order, IYKYK) you’d planned for it. I didn’t intend for me or my wife to be a template for any character. It’s not about us.

But there are moments.

There’s a man in the book who brings the woman he loves tea every morning – Earl Grey, no milk, two lumps of sugar. He does it without being asked. He does it even on the morning after she tells him something that changes everything between them.

That’s not us. But I bring my wife tea every morning. And when we upgraded from a queen bed to a king – more room than we’ve ever had – I still go looking for her in the middle of the night.

Just a little hidden easter egg for her.

OK, now it’s public. But not embarrassed by that. Love you, Gwen. 🍵❤️

Somewhere between the second draft and the fifth, I noticed a thread I hadn’t planned. The book is a love story – full stop. But woven through it is something quieter: women in science mentoring other women in science. It’s not the plot. It’s the thing that keeps showing up in the margins – in a lab late at night, in a hallway conversation that changes someone’s career trajectory, in the way one character grows because another one chose to invest in her instead of just moving on.

It’s subtle. But it ended up mattering to every character it touched. And when I read it back, I realized it was the only leadership philosophy I’ve ever believed in: the patient, invisible work of helping someone become more of who they already are.

Here’s the scene that stopped me.

A junior researcher is spiraling over data that doesn’t make sense, convinced she’s missed something obvious. And a senior scientist – instead of fixing the problem, which would have taken thirty seconds – pulls up a chair:

“What’s your instinct telling you?”

“That I’m missing something obvious. Something everyone else would catch immediately.”

“That’s frustration talking, not instinct.” She kept her voice gentle. “You’re right that the edit should be consistent. So if the cut is the same but the outcome varies, what else could be creating that variance?”

Jess’s eyes moved across the data. She sat up straighter. “Environmental stress. The conditions would change how the cell reads the edit, even if the edit itself is identical.”

“There it is.” Ellie smiled. “The code is only part of the story – context shapes what gets read and what stays silent.”

She spent the next hour beside Jess, not lecturing but questioning – letting Jess work through the implications herself. Which conditions should be standardized? How to document the variables. What it would take to make their results reproducible across labs, across patient populations, across the gap between a petri dish and a living human being.

“When I first started,” Ellie said eventually, “I made the exact same mistake. Spent two weeks convinced my experiment was broken before Dr. Harris asked me the same question I just asked you.”

“What question?”

“’What’s your instinct telling you?’” Ellie smiled. “I’d been so focused on finding the error that I stopped trusting my own thinking. The answer was there the whole time. I just needed someone to remind me I was capable of seeing it.”

“You should present this at the next team meeting. You caught it. You should be the one to flag it.”

Jess’s eyes widened. “Present it? But I nearly missed it entirely –”

“And then you didn’t. That’s science.”

“But you asked the right questions –”

“I asked questions you already knew the answers to. I just gave you permission to say them out loud. That’s what a good mentor does. But the work is yours. And the credit should be yours too.”

Jess swallowed. “Dr. Harris used to do that for you, didn’t he? Ask questions instead of giving answers?”

“How did you know that?”

“You mentioned him once. Early on.” Jess smiled, a little shy. “You said he was the first person who made you feel like a real scientist. Not a student pretending to be one.”

The memory landed with unexpected weight. Harris, with his rumpled blazers and his endless patience. The way he’d pushed her onto stages she wasn’t ready for, then stood in the back of the room, nodding, like he’d known all along she could do it.

“He was,” Ellie said. “And I’m trying to do for you what he did for me. Not because you need it – you’re already good. But because everyone deserves someone in their corner.”

“Say you’ll present. Say you’ll stand up in front of Singh and Kim and everyone else and tell them what you found.” Ellie held her gaze. “Say you’ll let them see what I see.”

A long pause. Then, slowly, Jess nodded. “Okay. I’ll do it.”

“Good.” Ellie squeezed her shoulder. “One more woman who learns to take up space.”

As she moved toward her own bench, she glanced back to see Jess staring at the data with new focus – shoulders looser now, a spark kindled behind her eyes.

This, Ellie, thought. This is why I chose this life. Not the boardrooms or the funding pitches. The light flickering on in someone else’s eyes. The moment when someone stopped doubting themselves long enough to see what they were capable of.

Afterward, a colleague watches this exchange and says, “That was well done. The Socratic approach. Most lead researchers would have fixed it for her and moved on.“

And then: “Building people up takes longer than doing the work yourself. Most senior scientists can’t be bothered. They’re too busy chasing the next publication, the next grant, the next rung.“

And then she says something I keep coming back to:

“I had a mentor like that once. Someone who asked questions instead of giving answers. It changed everything for me – not just how I did science, but how I saw myself doing it. How I understood what kind of leader I wanted to become.”

I wrote that scene thinking I was writing about science. I read it back and realized I was writing about every good manager I’ve ever had. The ones who made me feel like I’d figured something out on my own, only to realize years later that they’d been steering the whole time. The ones who understood that the point was never the problem – it was the person.

The light flickering on in someone else’s eyes. That’s the sentence that stopped me. Because that’s what the best leaders do. Not solve things. Not impress people with how fast they can fix it. They wait. They ask. They stand in the back of the room, nodding, like they knew all along you could do it.

Maybe that’s what all good love stories are – stories about the patient, invisible work of helping someone become more of who they already are. No pirates. No Scottish lords. Just the small, steady acts that hold people together.

The most romantic thing you can do for someone – in a marriage, in a team, in a life – is just keep showing up.

Right now, I’m in the part of writing that nobody romanticizes: the trimming. Cutting words. Killing sentences I love. That’s really hard. Running chapters past the women in my advance reader group and listening – really listening – when they tell me what rings true and what doesn’t. I’m chasing something in the prose that I don’t quite have yet. If you made me name it, I’d say Emily St. John Mandel – that quality where every sentence earns its place and nothing is there just because the writer liked the sound of it.

Between a leadership book launching next month, a novel that’s getting closer to the thing I want it to be, a biography of the number Seven that won’t leave me alone, and continuing to bring my wife her tea every morning, the next few weeks will be slower here while I finish what I started. I’m not going anywhere. I’m just doing the thing I keep telling other people to do: protecting the work that matters most, even when it means saying no to the work that’s merely visible.

I’ll be back soon. In the meantime, if you need me, I’ll be arguing with my editor about my dashes – seriously its the way I’ve always written. People don’t talk in semi-colons…

But before I go, think about the person who pulled up a chair for you.

Who asked instead of fixed. Who stood in the back of the room, nodding.

Now ask yourself: who are you not doing that for?
