Accountability isn't an event

Leaders and their managers agree on what leaders are worst at. Knowing hasn't fixed it

Matt Goode8 October 20266 min read Get new pieces by email

# Winning feels like a near miss# CREATE

Leaders and the managers who report to them disagree about almost everything Gallup measured. They agree on one thing: what leaders are worst at.

In a new Gallup study, Jim Harter and Corey Tatel asked both groups to rate leaders on seven competencies: building relationships, developing people, leading change, inspiring others, thinking critically, communicating clearly, and creating accountability. Creating accountability came last. For both groups.

46% of leaders rate themselves exceptional or outstanding at it.

30% of managers say the same about their own leader.

That 16-point gap sounds large until you see the rest. On six of the seven competencies, managers rate their leaders at least 20 points below the leaders’ own ratings. Accountability is where the two groups come closest.

So leaders still overrate themselves here, just by less than anywhere else. They can see the weakness. Their managers see it too. And seeing it hasn’t fixed it.

The problem isn’t awareness

That matters for how organizations try to fix it. Most develop managers through events: a two-day course, an assessment, a box checked before the budget year closes. A course can teach a skill. It can’t make one stick. And you can’t train your way out of a weakness everyone in the room can already see.

What I hear when leaders describe accountability

I coach about 20 executives and work with about 10 organizations. When I ask a senior leader how they hold their managers accountable, the answer almost always arrives as a story about a moment. A missed quarter. A hard conversation. A performance plan.

Almost nobody describes a Tuesday.

That’s the tell. Accountability, as most leaders practice it, happens after something has already gone wrong. So the manager on the other side only meets it when something is broken. In schools, it even has a season. Department chairs and division heads meet accountability at evaluation time, and the rest of the year it lives in good intentions.

The band doesn’t get tight at the clinic

Any working musician knows the difference between a clinic and a rehearsal. I used to play in a faculty band and work with student bands after school. A clinic teaches you something. Rehearsal is where the band actually locks in, every week, in a room where nobody is paying. The part I always loved was the listening. Everyone in the ensemble listens to everyone else, for cues, for tuning, for knowing you’re in the refrain and not the bridge. That’s where the dragged tempo and the missed change get fixed, while they’re small and cheap.

Last month, U2 marked fifty years together by going back to the Dublin school where it started. In September 1976, a 14-year-old drummer pinned a note to the school noticeboard, and a few classmates with limited musical experience turned up for a first practice in his family’s kitchen. Fifty years later, the same four played the schoolyard, with students from the school on strings. I don’t know what happens in their rehearsal room. But I know what it takes to keep the same four people in one band for half a century, and it isn’t a clinic. It’s decades of listening to each other.

A band that only plays together on stage finds its problems in front of an audience.

Most organizations run accountability the same way. There’s no weekly room where leaders and managers listen for each other’s cues, so the first time a manager hears the standard clearly is the night it matters, with everyone watching. The course was the clinic. Nobody booked the rehearsal room.

Gallup’s earlier work on why performance management fails shows how common this is: only 50% of employees clearly know what is expected of them at work. One reader summed it up for me in a single line: “A core leadership issue- clarity of expectations horizontally and vertically.” Half the band is guessing the set list. No wonder the accountability conversation feels like an ambush on one side and a necessary evil on the other.

What rehearsal looks like

Gallup’s prescription reads like a rehearsal plan, and it’s just as unglamorous: weekly manager conversations that combine coaching, recognition, priority-setting and discussion of individual strengths. Accountability as a regular, fair discipline, not something saved for corrective moments. The earlier piece sets a specific floor: meaningful feedback every week, substantial developmental coaching every month. Every rehearsal, and a proper lesson once a month.

Start with the set list. Agree on the one or two priorities that matter before you meet again, in words you’d both write down the same way.

Then work the hard passage. Ask what’s stuck, and stay on that one thing instead of touring every project. Coaching is a question about a specific problem, not a speech about general improvement.

Nod when someone nails the fill. Name one thing that went well last week, specifically enough that they could do it again on purpose. Vague recognition sounds like politeness. Specific recognition tells people what the standard is.

Write the parts for the players you have. Once a month, go deeper: ask where their work draws on what they do best and where it grinds against it. Sometimes the fix is adjusting the part, not pushing the player.

And close by checking this week’s set list against last week’s. If the same priority keeps showing up unplayed, that’s the conversation to have now, while it’s still a missed change and not a trainwreck on stage.

What changes when it becomes weekly

Marcel Schwantes flagged this research in Inc. this month, under a headline about how most companies train managers once. The number that should change that habit: the 30% of managers who rate their leader exceptional at accountability are three times as likely to be engaged, 51% versus 17%. These are survey ratings, not performance outcomes, so it’s an association, not proof. But it’s hard to wave away a gap that wide.

In my own work, the shift I see when accountability moves from reaction to rhythm is less dramatic than people expect, and more durable. It stops being a verdict and becomes maintenance. Recognition and correction travel through the same channel, so the correction doesn’t arrive from a stranger. Managers learn what good looks like before they’re judged on it.

One CEO I wrote about in CLUTCH ran a $50 million business with 120 employees and couldn’t take a vacation without his laptop. The first ritual we designed together was small: in his weekly 1:1s, he had to ask three questions before offering a single suggestion.

“What have you already tried?”

“What’s your instinct here?”

“What would you do if I weren’t available to ask?”

He hated it at first. It felt slow. Then one day his CFO said something that stopped him cold: “I’ve started actually preparing for our 1:1s now. I used to bring you problems. Now I bring you options.”

This is why I keep saying leadership is infrastructure, not training. The course isn’t useless. I run professional development sessions too, as every consultant does. But the session was never the system. The real consulting is building structures that keep running after I leave. The system is what happens on an ordinary Tuesday, when nothing is on fire, and you still sit down with the person who manages your people.

One thing to try this week

Most founders assume their expectations are obvious. Here’s how to find out in twenty minutes.

Pick the manager whose work you feel least sure about. Book 20 minutes with them before Friday. Open with one question: “In your own words, what do you think I expect of you this month?” Then listen the way a band listens. Don’t correct them while they talk. Write it down.

Then tell them what you would have said.

Put the two answers side by side. Where they match, say so out loud, because that’s recognition with a standard attached. Where they don’t, that’s the work. Before you leave, agree on one sentence you’d both write down the same way, and read it back to each other.

Then put the next conversation on the calendar for the same time next week. Not because something went wrong. Because that’s when rehearsal is.

U2’s fifty years started with one practice in a kitchen. This is yours.

You’ve just booked the rehearsal room.
