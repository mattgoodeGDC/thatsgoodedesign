Cracking the 2 Sigma Code: AI Tutoring and the Future of Learning

Matt Goode23 January 202614 min read Get new pieces by email

# AI# Schools

In 1984, Benjamin Bloom demonstrated something we’ve been ignoring ever since: students who received one-on-one tutoring performed TWO standard deviations better than their peers in traditional classrooms. The average tutored student outperformed 98% of students in conventional settings.

If the name sounds vaguely familiar, yes – this is the same Bloom behind “Bloom’s Taxonomy.” Though ironically, the famous pyramid wasn’t his design; that came later from other researchers. His actual research on learning is far more actionable than any triangle.

If you never took statistics, here’s what TWO standard deviations means: imagine a student sitting exactly in the middle of their class – 50th percentile, perfectly average. With one-on-one tutoring, that same student jumps to the 98th percentile. They’re now outperforming nearly everyone. It’s like taking a runner with an 8-minute mile and watching them run a 5:30. Not a modest improvement. A transformation.

We’ve had the answer for 40 years. We just couldn’t afford to scale it.

So we kept running schools and training programs like factories – knowing a better way existed, but constrained by cost and logistics. The kids who got tutors pulled ahead. Everyone else made do.

That calculus is finally shifting.

The Research That Put a Name to What I Already Knew

I’m neurodivergent. Dyslexic. Probably somewhere on the spectrum too – undiagnosed, like so many of us who learned to mask before anyone thought to look. And long before I encountered Bloom’s research, I understood in my bones that one-size-fits-all instruction doesn’t work – because it never worked for me.

I learned early that I needed things explained differently. I processed at my own pace. That I thrived when someone could meet me where I was, rather than where the curriculum said I should be. This wasn’t a theory. It was survival.

So when I discovered Bloom’s research, it didn’t change how I think about learning. It gave me the language for what I already knew. It validated a philosophy I’d been living – and building my entire career around.

Bloom’s research comes down to two principles that have shaped my entire approach – whether I’m coaching executives, training teams, or working with schools:

  • Mastery Learning – When people learn at their own pace and get tailored feedback, they learn better. Full stop.

  • Individual Attention – Add one-to-one instruction, and results go through the roof.

These principles apply everywhere. A new manager learning to give difficult feedback. A neurodivergent kid learning to read. A senior leader developing executive presence. The mechanism is the same: personalized attention accelerates growth in ways that group instruction can’t match.

These aren’t complicated ideas. They’re just expensive ones.

BTW, here's what Bloom's research looks like in practice: two of my kids – both dyslexic – one tutoring the other in my old classroom at McDonogh. No curriculum. No formal structure. Just one kid who gets it helping another kid get there.

The Challenge of Mentorship and One-on-One Instruction

If private tutoring works so well, why isn’t it universally available? A: Cost and accessibility.

K-12 tutoring is a multi-billion-dollar industry, yet access is deeply uneven. Families that can afford SAT tutors see average score increases of 100-300 points, significantly boosting their children’s chances of admission to elite universities.

But this week, something shifted. Google announced that Gemini – its AI assistant – now offers free, full-length SAT practice tests built with content from The Princeton Review. Not generic AI-generated questions, but rigorously vetted material that mirrors the actual exam format, complete with timed sections, instant feedback, and personalized explanations. If a student gets a question wrong, they can ask Gemini to explain it differently until the concept clicks – replicating the Socratic method of a human tutor.

This is what democratization looks like. A tool that used to cost families thousands of dollars is now available to any student with internet access, on demand, 24/7, at no cost. It’s not a replacement for a great tutor – but for millions of kids who could never afford one, it’s dramatically better than nothing. From medieval guilds to modern technical trades, apprenticeship programs have historically worked because they provide structured, hands-on learning with expert guidance. But such programs have largely disappeared in white-collar professions, leaving a gap in high-skill, high-touch mentorship.

One promising effort to bring tutoring to broader populations is peer-assisted learning. Peer-Assisted Learning Strategies (PALS), developed at Vanderbilt University, pairs students of different skill levels in guided learning activities. Research shows PALS significantly boosts comprehension – studies found a 36% improvement in reading scores and nearly double the progress for struggling students compared to traditional instruction. By having students take turns as “Coach” and “Learner,” PALS reinforces understanding through active engagement, immediate feedback, and structured guidance.

I see this in my own home. The photo above shows two of my kids – both dyslexic – one tutoring the other. Peer-assisted learning isn’t just research to me. It’s what happens at my kitchen table, or in this case, my old classroom, while I was doing something after school.

Despite these successes, peer mentorship programs remain underutilized. Most schools rely on passive, large-group instruction instead of active, one-on-one learning. The same pattern appears in corporate settings.

The corporate world has recognized the power of individualized development – but it’s worth distinguishing between three related but different approaches:

  1. Tutoring is about skill transfer. A tutor has the knowledge you need and helps you acquire it. The relationship is instructional: they explain, you learn, they assess. It’s typically short-term and focused on specific competencies.

  2. Mentoring is about career guidance. A mentor has walked the path you’re on and shares their experience, wisdom, and network. The relationship is advisory: they’ve been where you’re going and can help you navigate. It’s typically longer-term and relationship-based.

  3. Coaching is about unlocking potential. A coach doesn’t need to be an expert in your field – they’re an expert in helping you think. The relationship is facilitative: they ask questions, challenge assumptions, and help you find your own answers. It’s goal-oriented and focused on behavior change and growth.

All three benefit from one-on-one attention. All three suffer when scaled into groups. And all three have a measurable impact.

The data on coaching alone is striking. According to the International Coach Federation, companies that invest in coaching see an average ROI of 7x their initial investment. 86% of organizations report recouping their investment. 70% of coaching clients report improved work performance, and companies with strong coaching cultures grow 27% faster year over year.

Google’s internal coaching initiative increased employee engagement, leadership development, and productivity. IBM’s mentorship program increased employee satisfaction and reduced new-hire onboarding time by 20%. Deloitte found that employees with mentors were 5x more likely to be promoted.

This is why I built my coaching practice around one-on-one work. When I’m helping a founder navigate a difficult conversation, working with a school leader to build psychological safety, or coaching a technical lead through their first management role, the power comes from that individualized attention. I can meet them exactly where they are, adapt in real time, and go as deep as we need to. It’s Bloom’s insight applied to leadership development.

The pattern is undeniable: one-on-one instruction delivers better learning, stronger retention, and improved performance. The challenge has always been scalability.

Who Gets Left Behind: The Neurodiversity Gap

This gap hits neurodivergent learners hardest. And the reasons go deeper than most people realize.

Same kid. Same behavior. Different label.

Yesterday, I wrote about how a neurotypical child who obsessively pursues robotics or spends hours building in Minecraft gets called an “explorer.” We celebrate their creativity. We talk about how they’ve “found their spark.”

Small fan boy moment: Mitch Resnick – the MIT professor who created Scratch – responded to that post. When one of the researchers you’re writing about engages with your work, it’s a good sign you’re onto something.

An autistic child doing the exact same thing? We call it a “restricted interest.” We design interventions to redirect them toward more “appropriate” engagement.

That pattern – celebrating a behavior in some kids while pathologizing it in others – applies to instruction too. Neurodivergent kids often need personalized approaches more than their peers, but they’re the least likely to get them.

Consider what a traditional classroom demands. A neurodivergent student isn’t just learning content – they’re simultaneously managing sensory input, navigating unwritten social rules, suppressing natural behaviors, and monitoring themselves constantly to appear “normal.” Researchers call this masking, and it’s cognitively brutal.

Dr. Dena Gassner, a senior research scientist at the A.J. Drexel Autism Institute, describes masking as “a complex and labor-intensive cognitive process that is especially taxing for autistic people.” It’s not a choice or a performance – it’s survival. And it comes at a cost: burnout, anxiety, depression, and a fractured sense of identity.

A recent Drexel study found that 44% of autistic teens in community settings “passed” as non-autistic in their classrooms – meaning their teachers showed no awareness they were autistic at all. Their brains showed faster automatic responses to faces and dampened emotional reactivity, suggesting they’d adapted neurologically to cope with constant social demands. The researchers called it “the hidden mental effort behind social masking.”

Think about what this means for learning. These kids are spending enormous cognitive resources just appearing typical. What’s left for actual learning?

As Rebecca Winthrop noted in her conversation with Ezra Klein, neurodivergent kids often end up in “passenger mode” – not because they lack capacity, but because the vehicle was built for someone else. They’re not disengaged because they can’t learn. They’re disengaged because the environment demands they spend all their energy on something other than learning.

This is why one-on-one instruction isn’t just “nice to have” for neurodivergent learners. It’s often the difference between thriving and drowning.

In a 1:1 setting, masking becomes unnecessary. There’s no audience to perform for. No social hierarchy to navigate. No sensory overwhelm from 25 other bodies in a fluorescent-lit room. The student can focus entirely on learning – perhaps for the first time.

Researcher Mimi Ito, in her conversation with Mitch Resnick (creator of Scratch), observed that environments like Scratch communities “overindex for neurodivergent kids.” Why? Because they’re spaces where you can “really geek out and go deep on interests that other people might not be interested in, and to talk forever about them.” The behaviors that get pathologized as “antisocial or inappropriate” in mainstream settings become the norm in these spaces.

The same principle applies to one-on-one tutoring. A tutor can follow a student’s interests wherever they lead. A tutor can explain concepts 17 different ways without becoming impatient. A tutor doesn’t penalize a student for needing more time, asking “too many” questions, or approaching problems from an unusual angle.

My two dyslexic kids in that photo? They need instruction delivered differently from how it is delivered to their peers. They need more time, different pathways, and patient repetition. In a classroom of 25, that’s hard to provide. But one-on-one? They thrive.

The 2 Sigma problem isn’t just an educational curiosity. For neurodivergent learners, it’s the difference between being seen as capable or being written off.

AI as a Catalyst for Personalized Learning

I’ve seen enough failed ed-tech revolutions to be skeptical. But AI is different.

AI has the potential to bridge the gap between one-on-one tutoring’s effectiveness and the need for large-scale solutions:

  • Instant Feedback – Every teacher and trainer knows that immediate feedback is crucial for learning. AI-powered tools can provide real-time responses, allowing educators and mentors to focus on higher-level coaching.

  • Adaptive Learning Systems – Platforms like Khan Academy and DreamBox already demonstrate how AI can adjust learning pathways based on individual progress, making mastery learning a reality for more students.

  • AI Tutors – I’ve tested AI-powered assistants in workshops, and when learners can ask unlimited questions without judgment, their confidence and comprehension improve dramatically. No sighing. No impatience. No social penalty for needing something explained seventeen different ways.

  • AI Coaching – I know this is a hot, hot, hot topic among coaches – AI feels like a threat to the profession. But I’ve been experimenting rather than resisting. I built an AI chatbot that lets managers practice difficult 1:1 feedback conversations and receive immediate suggestions in a low-stakes environment. It’s a narrow skill set to be sure, and I’m still tinkering with the engine and the degree of emotional intelligence in the responses. Not ready for distribution yet, but it’s getting close – and early results show that AI-enhanced feedback accelerates growth in ways traditional coaching struggles to achieve. The goal isn’t to replace the coach. It’s to give people reps they’d never get otherwise.

The most direct attempt to solve Bloom’s 2 Sigma problem with AI is Khan Academy’s Khanmigo. Sal Khan has been explicit about the connection – he’s talked for years about Bloom’s research and how cost made it impossible to provide every student with a personal tutor. Khanmigo is his answer: an AI tutor designed not for engagement metrics or streak counts, but for genuine mastery learning.

What makes it different from other ed-tech? It doesn’t just deliver content. It tutors. It asks questions rather than giving answers. It adapts to where the student actually is, not where the curriculum says they should be. It offers infinite patience without judgment – exactly what Bloom’s research suggests matters most.

The key takeaway? AI doesn’t replace human mentorship – it amplifies it. AI can provide continuous, personalized feedback, while human educators and coaches focus on the relational, motivational, and ethical dimensions of learning.

The “Best Available Human” Problem

AI won’t solve this alone. But for the first time, we have tools that could provide what Ethan Mollick calls the “best available human” – not a replacement for the ideal specialist, but dramatically better than nothing.

The ideal scenario for a neurodivergent learner is a trained specialist who understands their specific neurotype, can adapt instruction in real-time, follows their interests, and builds a genuine relationship. But how many kids have access to that? Especially in under-resourced schools, rural districts, or families who can’t afford private specialists?

Winthrop speaks from personal experience: “My youngest son has dyslexia, and the sort of read and write text-to-speech, speech-to-text has been game-changing for him.” Assistive technology has already transformed possibilities for many neurodivergent learners. AI extends that dramatically.

An AI tutor that offers infinite patience, no social judgment, and the ability to explain concepts in multiple ways until one clicks? For a kid with dyslexia, ADHD, or autism, that’s not a compromise. That’s access that didn’t exist before.

Think about what this means for a student who masks all day:

  • No social performance required. The cognitive load of appearing “normal” disappears. They can focus entirely on learning.

  • Interest-following capacity. An AI can follow a student’s interest wherever it goes – across disciplines, into obscure corners, at whatever depth they want. It doesn’t need to redirect them to the curriculum.

  • Adaptation without judgment. Visual learner? Here’s a diagram. Need it explained through a metaphor? Done. Want to approach the problem backwards? Let’s try it.

  • Availability that matches their rhythm. Neurodivergent learners often don’t operate on school schedules. They might be ready to dive deep at 10 pm or need to process for three days before engaging. AI doesn’t have office hours.

The critical question – one Mimi Ito raises – is whether we’ll use AI to support genuine cognitive diversity, or just find more efficient pathways to the same standardized outcomes. Personalized learning that still demands conformity isn’t personalized at all.

AI as a Force Multiplier

AI excels at handling scale and personalization in ways no human instructor can match. It can instantly adjust lesson difficulty, track progress, and provide real-time feedback, ensuring each learner gets tailored support. This frees educators, mentors, and coaches to focus on higher-order thinking, problem-solving, and human connection – the aspects of learning that technology alone cannot replicate.

The integration of AI and human mentorship is already proving effective:

  • Higher Education: Georgia State University’s AI-enhanced chatbot Pounce improved retention rates and student grades. Arizona State University developed the world’s first adaptive-learning biology degree, integrating virtual reality technologies to personalize learning experiences.

  • Corporate Training: Deloitte and PwC have implemented AI coaching tools for real-time, data-driven feedback on leadership development. IBM’s AI-driven mentorship platform matches employees with mentors based on career goals and learning needs.

  • K-12 Education: Los Angeles Unified School District is piloting AI-powered tutors to assist students in math and reading. Carnegie Learning’s AI-driven MATHia software in Pittsburgh Public Schools has led to measurable improvements in math proficiency.

The key insight: AI handles scale. Humans handle meaning. That’s not a compromise – it’s the combination we’ve been waiting for.

The Design Problem

The technology isn’t the answer. The design is (of course it is, lol).

As Winthrop argues, companies creating educational technology should prioritize social benefit and well-being, not just profit maximization. The AI tools racing to capture the education market aren’t being designed with neurodivergent kids and the specialists who understand them. They’re being designed by engineers optimizing for engagement metrics and market share.

The Dutch government offers a different model – partnering with teacher unions, academics, and tech companies to experiment before rolling out. You can’t build AI that serves neurodivergent learners without neurodivergent people and neurodiversity experts in the development process.

This matters because the wrong AI could make things worse. An AI tutor optimized for “time on task” might punish a student who needs to process before responding. An AI designed around neurotypical social patterns might penalize a student who doesn’t make eye contact with an avatar. An AI that treats deviation from the standard path as “wrong” recreates the same conformity pressure as the classroom – just with better graphics.

The opportunity is enormous. But so is the risk of building AI that’s just a more efficient engine for the same factory model that’s been failing neurodivergent learners for generations.

What Comes Next

When Bloom published his findings in 1984, solving the 2 Sigma Problem seemed almost utopian – personalized instruction for every learner simply wasn’t feasible. But his research revealed a fundamental truth: one-on-one instruction works. The challenge was always scalability.

For decades, the answer was pragmatic – cost and scale made the gap unbridgeable. Today, that calculus is shifting.

AI-powered feedback, adaptive learning systems, and intelligent tutoring platforms make mastery learning more accessible than ever. But the human elements – relational connection, motivational support, ethical guidance, contextual understanding – remain irreplaceable.

The future of learning isn’t about choosing between AI and human mentorship. It’s about integrating both. And it’s about ensuring that integration serves all learners – including the neurodivergent kids who have been failed by factory-model education for too long.

The 2 Sigma Problem is no longer an unsolvable challenge. It’s an opportunity waiting to be realized. The question isn’t whether we can scale Bloom’s insights, but whether we’ll design that scale for genuine cognitive diversity – or just find faster paths to the same old conformity.
