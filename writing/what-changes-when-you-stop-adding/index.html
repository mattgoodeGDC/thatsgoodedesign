What Changes When You Stop Adding

Part 2 of 3: The Sculptor Series

Matt Goode5 February 202610 min read Get new pieces by email

# Winning feels like a near miss

Yesterday, I introduced the Sculptor – the leadership gear that removes rather than adds, that redesigns systems rather than optimizing within them. The gear that looks at a recurring problem and asks, “How do I solve this?” but “Should this problem exist at all?”

Today, I want to explore what actually happens when leaders make that shift. Because the results are rarely what anyone expects.

The first thing that happens is nothing.

That’s the terrifying part. Sculptor’s work doesn’t produce immediate visible output. You’re not running a meeting. You’re not sending the email. You’re not putting out the fire. You’re sitting with a harder question: What’s causing this fire to keep starting – and can I redesign so it stops?

That silence feels like failure. Especially for leaders who’ve built careers on visible activity. Especially for founders who got where they are by outworking everyone else. Especially for anyone who’s been rewarded their entire professional life for solving problems quickly.

But the silence is where the real work begins.

The Removal Paradox

Here’s what I’ve observed working with founders and leadership teams over the past several years: the leaders who create the most capacity aren’t the ones who work the hardest. They’re the ones who eliminate the most.

This sounds like a productivity cliché until you observe it in practice. Because elimination isn’t about efficiency. It’s not about time management, priority matrices, or getting things done in fewer steps. It’s about architecture.

The question is not “How do I do this faster?” It’s “Should this exist at all?”

W. Edwards Deming saw this clearly. His insight – that 94% of problems are system problems – leads to an uncomfortable conclusion: most of what leaders spend their time on is managing the symptoms of systems they could redesign. The recurring conflicts. The bottleneck decisions. The quality gaps. The communication breakdowns. These aren’t random failures. They’re system outputs. The architecture is producing them.

Once you see that, you have a choice. You can improve your ability to manage the symptoms – more efficient, more skilled, more responsive. Or you can redesign the architecture.

The first path has a ceiling. The second path compounds.

Let me show you what I mean.

Pattern One: The Problem That Disappeared

A COO I worked with spent two hours every week – sometimes more – mediating the same conflict between her product and engineering teams. The details changed. The underlying pattern didn’t. Priorities weren’t aligned. Handoffs were messy. Both sides felt the other was blocking progress. Every week, she’d bring them into a room, facilitate a productive conversation, and leave believing that something had shifted.

Then the next week, the same conflict. Different surface issue, same fundamental tension.

Her Operator instinct – the instinct most of us have – said: Become a better facilitator. Improve communication. Implement a weekly sync to surface problems earlier. Get everyone aligned.

She tried all of it. The communication improved. The syncs got added. The alignment conversations happened. The conflict persisted.

When she finally asked the Sculptor question – Why does this conflict keep regenerating? – The answer was structural, not interpersonal.

Decision rights were ambiguous. Both teams thought they owned prioritization for the product roadmap. Both were correct, given how their roles had been defined. The conflict wasn’t a communication failure. It was an architectural failure. The system was designed to produce exactly this tension.

No amount of facilitation would fix that. She could mediate that meeting every week for the next decade, and the conflict would still regenerate. Because she wasn’t addressing the root. She was managing the symptom.

The Sculptor move was different. She spent one afternoon – not months, not quarters, one afternoon – mapping decision rights explicitly. Who owns what? Where input is required versus where it’s optional. What happens when there’s genuine disagreement?

The two-hour weekly mediation? Gone. Not because she got better at it. Because she removed the need for it entirely.

That’s the Sculptor result. The problem didn’t get solved. The problem disappeared.

Pattern Two: The Capacity That Appeared

A founder told me he had no time for strategic thinking. His calendar was wall-to-wall. One-on-ones stacked against team meetings, stacked against client escalations, stacked against operational reviews. When he looked at his week, there was no white space. He was drowning in meetings about meetings.

The Operator response would be time management. Batch similar meetings. Protect Friday afternoons. Wake up earlier. Find the margins.

He’d tried all of that. It wasn’t working. Whatever space he created got filled immediately. The system was producing meetings faster than he could eliminate them.

So we tried something different. For one week, he tracked not his meetings but his decisions. Every time someone came to him for input. Every time he approved something. Every time someone escalated because they weren’t sure what to do.

Then we sorted them: Which of these decisions actually required him? Not which ones he was good at making. Not which ones he had opinions about. Which ones genuinely couldn’t have been made by someone else with the right context and authority?

The answer: about 30%.

Thirty percent of his decisions actually needed his involvement. The other 70% reached him not because he was required, but because the system defaulted to higher levels. No one had explicitly defined decision rights. No one had clarified what “take ownership” actually meant. No one had built the guardrails that would let people decide confidently without escalating.

So they escalated. Not because they were incapable – they weren’t. Because escalation was safer than guessing wrong. The system had trained them.

He didn’t need better time management. He needed to redesign the decision architecture.

The Sculptor move wasn’t working more efficiently. It involved making decision rights explicit, defining guardrails clearly, and delegating authority to those with the context to make quick decisions.

Within a month, he’d reclaimed eight hours a week. Not by squeezing harder. By removing what shouldn’t have been there in the first place.

Pattern Three: The Excellence That Emerged

A director approached me, frustrated that her team’s work was good but never great. She’d raised the bar. Given more feedback. Pushed harder. Made her expectations clearer. Nothing changed. The work was competent and consistent, meeting requirements, but lacked spark.

Her Operator instinct said: push harder. More detailed feedback. Higher standards, communicated more forcefully. If the work isn’t excellent, demand excellence.

That’s what she’d been doing. It wasn’t working.

When she asked the Sculptor question – What’s making excellence hard? – the answer surprised her. It wasn’t motivation. It wasn’t capability. It was the process itself.

Her team was working on a three-week sprint cycle. By the time work reached her for final review, they’d already mentally moved on to the next sprint. Her feedback came too late. Not too late to be heard – too late to matter. They’d learned to settle for “good enough” and shift focus because the system didn’t allow iteration. Excellence requires refinement, and the process had no room for refinement.

She wasn’t seeing a motivation problem. She was seeing a systems problem.

The Sculptor move: feedback earlier in the cycle. Fewer approval gates. More iteration loops before the final review. She removed the structural barrier to excellence instead of demanding that people push through it.

The quality changed. Not because she raised her standards – she’d already done that. Because she removed what was preventing her standards from being met.

The Compound Effect

James Clear wrote that you don’t rise to the level of your goals – you fall to the level of your systems. I think about that line constantly when I work with leaders who are exhausted and stuck.

Operator effort is linear. You put in, you get out. Work harder, produce more. The math is straightforward – and limiting. There’s a ceiling: the hours in your day, the energy in your body, the attention you can sustain.

Sculptor’s effort compounds.

Every system you redesign is a problem you stop solving. That’s not a one-time savings. It’s recurring. The two-hour weekly mediation that disappeared? That’s 100 hours a year. The decisions that stopped routing upward? That’s not just time – it’s cognitive load, context-switching, the invisible tax of being the bottleneck for everything.

And the effects multiply. When you’re not mediating the same conflict every week, you have space to notice other patterns. When decisions flow to the right level, your team develops judgment faster because they’re actually exercising it. When excellence becomes structurally possible, people begin to pursue it without external pressure.

Liz Wiseman’s research found that Multipliers – leaders who amplify their team’s capability – get twice the output from the same people as Diminishers. Twice. Not 10% more. Double.

That’s not because Multipliers work twice as hard. It’s because they create systems in which capability can emerge, in which intelligence isn’t suppressed, and in which people can contribute what they’re capable of contributing.

Sculptor work is how you become a Multiplier. Not by adding your brilliance to every problem – that makes you a Diminisher, however well-intentioned. But by removing the structural barriers that prevent other people’s brilliance from showing up.

Why This Is Hard

Three forces work against Sculptor mode. They’re powerful, and they’re worth naming.

The first is visibility. No one congratulates you for the meeting you eliminated. The crisis that didn’t happen doesn’t make the quarterly report. The decision that flowed smoothly to the right person without your involvement – that just looks like nothing.

Organizations reward visible activity. Launches. Announcements. Initiatives. The executive who’s constantly in motion, solving problems, driving results, putting out fires – that person looks like a leader. The executive who quietly redesigned the system so the fires stopped starting – that person looks like they’re not doing much.

Sculptor work is invisible by nature. Its success is measured in absence: the problems that don’t recur, the decisions that don’t escalate, the conflicts that don’t regenerate. That’s hard to put in a performance review.

The second is ego. Sculptor work requires admitting that the system is the problem. And you probably built the system. Or you inherited it and chose not to change it. Either way, moving into Sculptor mode starts with an uncomfortable acknowledgment: this isn’t working, and I’m part of why.

Operator lets you be the hero. You swoop in, solve the problem, save the day. It feels good. It reinforces your value. It confirms why you’re in the room.

Sculptor requires a different posture. You’re not the hero – you’re the architect of a system that needed heroes and shouldn’t have. That’s a harder story to tell yourself.

The third is timing. Sculptor work is slow before it’s fast.

Operator produces visible results immediately. You leave the meeting feeling accomplished. You clear the inbox. You make the decision. The feedback loop is tight – action, result, satisfaction.

Sculptor produces results later. Sometimes much later. You redesign a decision process, and for a week, nothing changes while people adjust to the new model. You eliminate a meeting, and there’s a brief period of uncertainty before the new rhythm settles. You push authority downward, and the first few decisions are shakier than they would’ve been if you’d just made them yourself.

Organizations optimize for short-term visible progress. Sculptor work often looks, in the short term, like nothing is happening. The payoff comes later – but later isn’t what gets rewarded in most cultures.

These three forces – invisibility, ego, and timing – are why most leaders stay in Operator. It’s not that they don’t understand Sculptor intellectually. The books talk about “working on the business.” The consultants mention “systems thinking.” Everyone nods.

But when Monday morning comes, the incentives, the culture, and the psychology all push toward addition. Toward more. Toward Operator.

The Signal to Watch

Here’s what I’ve noticed across all three patterns – and dozens more I could share:

The leaders who successfully shift into Sculptor mode don’t start by asking What should I remove? That question is too abstract. It leads to paralysis or to cutting things that actually matter.

They start with a different question: What keeps regenerating?

The conflict recurs despite your best efforts to facilitate. The decisions that keep escalating despite your requests for ownership. The quality issues that persist despite your feedback. The fires keep reigniting, no matter how many times you put them out.

Recurring problems are system signatures. They’re the architecture making itself visible. When the same issue recurs, the system is telling you something: I’m designed to produce this. You can solve it as many times as you want – I’ll keep generating it.

Once you learn to read those signatures, you start seeing the design choices you didn’t know you’d made. The implicit decisions about authority. The unexamined assumptions about how information should flow. The inherited processes that once made sense no longer do.

What keeps regenerating? is the question that opens Sculptor mode. Not as an accusation. As an investigation. The goal isn’t blame. The goal is to see clearly.

Because you can’t remove what you haven’t seen.
