The Stage You Skipped

Matt Goode19 July 20262 min read Get new pieces by email

# My role is changing

I once watched a founder hand his best engineer a whole product line on a Friday, with a “you’ve got this,” and mean it as the highest compliment. By the next Friday, the engineer was underwater, the launch was slipping, and the founder was privately furious at someone he’d set up to fail.

He hadn’t given too much trust. He’d skipped a stage.

The middle is the part that builds the driver

Developing someone to run without you happens in three steps, and the order matters more than the speed. The Ride-Along: you drive, they watch, you think out loud. The Learner’s Permit: they drive, you ride along, asking questions instead of seizing the wheel. The Solo Drive: they own it, and you’ve stepped back to occasional passenger.

The mistake almost everyone makes is collapsing the middle. You show someone how it’s done once, jump them to solo, and brace for the crash. They didn’t lack talent. They lacked the Learner’s Permit – the only stretch where judgment is actually built, in the reps where they make the call and you’re near enough to coach but far enough to let it be real.

There are two ways to get the middle wrong, and both are everywhere. You can end it too early, mistaking one good week for mastery and pushing someone solo before they’re ready. Or you can never end it at all, hovering in the passenger seat so long that a capable adult is stuck on a permanent learner’s permit, asking permission for calls they could have owned a year ago.

Caption: Readiness × release – push too soon and they crash; hold too long and they stall.

The cost lands on both of you

The person stalls. They start to wonder whether they have a real future here, or whether they’ll be checking in with you forever. That’s when capable people leave – not in a blaze, but in a slow drift toward a job where they’ll be allowed to grow up.

And you stay the bottleneck. You tell yourself you’d delegate more if only they were ready, never clocking that you’re the reason they aren’t. The skipped stage doesn’t just cost you a stronger team member. It re-signs your own contract as the person everything routes through. Sitting in the discomfort of the Learner’s Permit, watching someone struggle and not rescuing them, is the price of ever getting to put the wheel down.

There’s a simple discipline that fixes both failures. Before you push someone to solo or hold them back, ask what specific decision they’ve now made well, on their own, more than once. If you can name it, give them more rope. If you can’t, they’re still on a permit, and the work is reps, not a verdict. Readiness is evidence, not a feeling you have about someone.

Walk your bench, one person at a time:

  • For each person you’re developing, which stage are they really in?

  • Who got pushed to Solo too soon, and who’s been left on a permit far too long?

  • Which of those is more about your comfort than their readiness?
