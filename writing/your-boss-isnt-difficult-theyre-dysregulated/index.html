Your Boss Isn’t Difficult. They’re Dysregulated.

Thoughts on a recent HBR piece: How to Manage an Insecure Leader

Matt Goode7 February 20264 min read Get new pieces by email

# EQ

I once worked with a leader whose door was always closed. Not metaphorically. Literally closed, every day, as a matter of practice.

Feedback of any kind – no matter how carefully delivered – is registered as a direct threat. Those who tried found themselves on the receiving end of a quiet, methodical vindictiveness. And yet this same leader would veer confidently into domains far outside her expertise, challenging specialists in their own lanes with a certainty she never displayed in the core responsibilities of leadership: communication, empathy, and team development.

That combination seems contradictory. It isn’t. The overreach is the insecurity. When a leader can’t tolerate “I don’t know enough about this to weigh in,” they’re usually the same person who can’t tolerate “That’s valid feedback, let me think about it.” Both require sitting with vulnerability – and for this leader, vulnerability wasn’t discomfort. It was dangerous.

The closed door wasn’t privacy. It was a perimeter. The vindictiveness wasn’t a strength. It was a warning system: “Do not come closer.” Don’t see what I don’t have.

Most leadership advice characterizes these individuals as “difficult” and advises you to manage around them. Set boundaries. Document everything. Update your resume. That advice isn’t wrong. But it misses the mechanism entirely.

Insecurity in leadership isn’t rare. It’s the norm. And it’s not a personality flaw. It’s a design problem.

The Patterns Under the Surface

New research from Jeffrey Yip and Dritjon Gruda, published in Harvard Business Review, provides a sharper lens on what’s actually happening: approximately 36% of adults exhibit an insecure attachment style. A 2024 Korn Ferry report found that 71% of U.S. CEOs experience symptoms of impostor syndrome. The leaders who appear most composed are often managing the most turbulent internal dynamics.

Yip and Gruda identify two insecure attachment patterns in leadership. Anxious leaders crave connection and reassurance; they draw teams close, then push people away when overwhelmed. You never know which version of the boss is entering the room. Avoidant leaders project control and resist vulnerability. Working with them feels like working around a wall.

But the leader I described is a third pattern the research doesn’t name cleanly: avoidant and overcompensating. This is the most disorienting version because the confidence looks real. The overreach into other people’s expertise reads as an assertion of authority. It takes time to recognize that the certainty in someone else’s lane is directly proportional to the fragility in their own.

What makes this pattern so destructive is that it attacks the very mechanism teams need to function: honest upward communication. When feedback is perceived as a threat, people stop offering it. When they stop offering it, the leader loses access to the information they need most. The system degrades from the inside – and the leader, sealed off behind a closed door, is often the last to know.

The System, Not the Person

The temptation is to label insecure leaders as the problem and move on. But here’s what twenty years in the classroom taught me: the most disruptive people in the room are usually the most dysregulated. And the response that feels most satisfying – confrontation, withdrawal, quiet resentment – almost never produces the outcome you want.

Yip and Gruda offer a useful sequence: Regulate, Relate, Reason. When someone’s nervous system is activated, logic doesn’t land. You must stabilize the system before you can connect, and connect before you can problem-solve. That ordering matters – and it applies to you as much as to them.

However, the deeper question isn’t how to manage a single dysregulated leader. The question is whether your organization has any infrastructure to detect this before it cascades. Most don’t. Most organizations promote people into leadership roles faster than they develop the emotional architecture to sustain them, then wonder why the system breaks under strain.

A leader with an insecure attachment style isn’t a broken person. They’re a predictable outcome of a system that rewards technical performance, ignores relational capacity, and treats leadership as a title rather than a design challenge.

The better question isn’t “How do I survive this person?” It’s “What does this environment need from me right now?” That reframe shifts you from a reactive to a diagnostic approach. From enduring to designing.

Two questions worth sitting with:

When your leader’s insecurity shows up, do you accommodate, withdraw, or confront – and is that pattern producing the results you want? I chose confront. I thought proving the gap would fix the system. It didn’t.

Are you regulating yourself before trying to regulate the relationship? I’ve been on the wrong side of this equation myself. You can’t steady someone else’s nervous system if yours is already hijacked.

The leaders who learn to read dysregulation with precision instead of frustration don’t just survive difficult bosses. They build the kind of relational infrastructure that makes entire teams more resilient.

That’s not managing up. That’s leading from wherever you sit.
