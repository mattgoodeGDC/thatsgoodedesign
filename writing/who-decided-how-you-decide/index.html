Who Decided How You Decide?

Matt Goode12 February 20267 min read Get new pieces by email

# 6C

ChatGPT can synthesize 100 pages of market research in 30 seconds. It can draft a strategy memo, model three scenarios, and rank them by risk – before you’ve finished your coffee.

What it can’t do is walk into your Monday meeting and say: “This decision needs everyone’s input. But that one? I’m calling it, and here’s why. And that third one? It’s yours – I trust you to run with it.”

AI can process information faster than any leader alive. But the competitive advantage was never about processing information. It’s about knowing which type of decision you’re facing – and choosing the right approach before the conversation starts.

Most leaders never make that choice. They just decide the way they’ve always decided.

Every team has a default decision-making mode. Almost nobody chose it deliberately. It just emerged – from the founder’s personality, the company’s early crises, the habits that calcified before anyone noticed.

Some teams default to consensus. Every decision becomes a group conversation. It feels inclusive. It feels democratic. And it grinds to a halt on the decisions that needed someone to just call it. Meetings multiply. Timelines stretch. The pursuit of alignment becomes the enemy of momentum.

Some teams default to the leader making all decisions. It’s fast. It’s clear. And it creates a bottleneck that scales exactly as far as one person’s calendar allows. The team stops developing judgment because their judgment never gets tested. They wait. They defer. They learn that initiative is risky and real authority is rare.

Some teams default to collaboration – talking everything through, workshopping every choice, co-creating every plan. It builds camaraderie. It also burns hours on decisions that one person with the right expertise could have resolved in five minutes.

None of these modes is wrong. Every one of them is right – in the right context. The problem isn’t the mode. It’s using the same mode for everything.

Here’s what I’ve seen working with teams across government agencies, school districts, and startups: the most dangerous decision-making failure isn’t choosing wrong. It’s using the wrong process.

A consensus approach to an urgent call. A unilateral decision on something that needed buy-in. A collaborative workshop for a question that the subject-matter expert should have owned. The what of the decision might be fine. The how is what erodes trust.

Because every decision is also a message. It tells your team who matters, what’s expected, and whether they can act with clarity or need to wait for permission.

When a leader says “let’s discuss” but has already made up their mind, people learn not to trust the invitation. When a leader says “this is your call” but second-guesses every move, people learn that autonomy is theater. When a leader pulls rank on a technical question that the expert should own, the expert stops contributing – not out of spite, but out of logic.

The gap between what you say and how you actually decide is where trust quietly erodes.

There are really six modes available to you. Not one. Not two. Six – and each one answers a different question.

Command: Who must act, right now? “I’m making this call. Here’s my reasoning. Questions after, but the direction is set.” That’s not authoritarian. That’s clarity in a moment that requires it.

Consultative: Whose insight will sharpen my call? “I’m deciding this, but I want your perspective first. I’ll explain how your input shaped my thinking.” That’s genuine consultation – but only if you actually let the input land.

Collaborative: Where will co-creation build a better solution? “We’re building this together. I need everyone’s fingerprints on it because the implementation depends on shared ownership.” That’s not slow. That’s strategic.

Consensus: When is alignment more important than speed? “We need broad agreement before we move. Let’s work toward a direction everyone can support.” This is the right call when the decision won’t stick without genuine buy-in – and the wrong call for almost everything else.

Core Expert: Who’s best positioned to decide this? “You know this domain better than I do. This is your call. I’ll support whatever you decide.” That’s not abdication. That’s trust grounded in competence.

Choice: What should I fully release? “Here are the guardrails. Inside them, you own everything. I’ll check in on Thursday, but this is yours.” That’s not disengagement. That’s development.

Six modes. Six different messages. And your team hears every one of them – whether you name them or not. The difference is whether they hear clarity or have to decode it.

But naming the mode is only half the equation. Each mode also has a depth – a level of autonomy you’re actually granting inside it.

Saying “this is collaborative” doesn’t tell your team much if you haven’t decided whether that means “I invite your input but hold final approval” or “we co-create this, then you carry it forward, and I step back.” Both are collaborative. They’re not the same thing.

Michael Hyatt mapped five levels of delegation, from “do exactly what I say” to “own this completely.” Lay those levels across the six decision modes, and you get a grid – and that grid is where the real diagnostic lives. Every cell is a different combination of what kind of decision this is and how much authority you’re actually releasing.

Most leaders don’t default to a single mode. They default to one cell – and camp there for every decision, every person, every context. The grid makes that visible.

And that’s where the real damage happens – not from picking the wrong mode, but from saying one thing and doing another.

But here’s where most leaders do real damage – not by picking the wrong mode, but by saying one thing and doing another.

“This is your call... but run everything by me first.” The words say autonomy. The behavior says control. People learn not to trust the words.

“Let’s build this together... then bring me what you’ve come up with and I’ll decide.” That’s not collaboration. That’s consultation dressed up as collaboration. People feel misled.

“I want your input on this.” But you’ve already decided, and everyone in the room can tell. Now “input” is theater – and next time you ask for it, no one will bother giving you their real thinking.

These mismatches don’t blow up in the moment. They erode trust silently. People stop listening to your words and start reading your behavior. They decode what you actually mean rather than what you say. And once a team learns to decode instead of trust, you’ve lost something that takes months to rebuild.

The AI parallel matters because it reveals what’s actually scarce. In a world drowning in information, the bottleneck isn’t analysis. It’s decision architecture – the ability to look at a situation and know not just what to decide, but how to decide it, who should be involved, and what kind of authority the moment requires.

That’s a human skill. And most leaders have never been taught it.

They’ve been taught to make good decisions. They’ve never been taught to design the decision process itself. So they default to one mode – the one that matches their personality – and wonder why some decisions land beautifully, and others generate confusion, resentment, or paralysis.

The fix isn’t a new framework on the wall. It’s a new habit: before the conversation starts, name the mode out loud.

“This is my call, and here’s why.” “I need your input before I decide.” “We’re deciding this together.” “We need alignment before we move.” “You’re the expert. This is yours.” “Here are the guardrails. You own the rest.”

Twelve words, in the right combination, that prevent twelve weeks of confusion. Try it once this week. Watch what happens in the room when people know – before anyone speaks – exactly what kind of conversation they’re in.
