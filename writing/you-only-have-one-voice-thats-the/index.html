You Only Have One Voice (That’s the Problem)

Matt Goode9 February 20264 min read Get new pieces by email

# VOICES

A guitarist who only plays power chords can fill a room. For about three songs. After that, everything starts to sound the same – loud, urgent, impressive in a narrow way. The audience doesn’t leave because the chords are bad. They leave because the range never changes.

Most leaders have the same problem. Not with volume. With voice.

Somewhere along the way – probably during the stretch that earned you the promotion, the reputation, the team – you developed a default mode. A way of showing up that worked so well it became automatic. Maybe you’re the person who brings structure to chaos. Maybe you’re the one who paints the vision and gets people fired up. Maybe you’re the steady hand who makes everyone feel safe.

That mode isn’t wrong. It’s your power chord. The problem is when it’s the only one you play.

I’ve spent twenty years watching this pattern up close. In classrooms, in boardrooms, in coaching sessions where a leader sits across from me, genuinely confused about why their team is stuck. The answer, almost always, is that the leader is bringing the same presence to every situation – and some situations need something entirely different.

The leader who’s brilliant at execution wonders why her team won’t take initiative. She’s so good at organizing the work that she’s taken ownership away from them. The leader who’s a natural encourager wonders why standards keep slipping. He’s so focused on making people feel safe that he’s never challenged them. The leader who inspires with vision wonders why nothing gets finished. She’s painted such a compelling picture of the future that no one’s managing the present.

Each of them is operating from a real strength. And each of them is stuck in a single gear.

Leadership isn’t one long stretch of smooth highway. The terrain changes. The weather changes. The load you’re carrying changes. And your team feels every shift – whether you adjust or not.

When the work is steady and the team is capable, what’s needed is space. Step back. Trust. Let them drive. But when deadlines compress and stakes rise, that same hands-off approach feels like an absence. Suddenly, the moment calls for structure, clarity, and decisiveness.

When morale dips and confidence wavers, people don’t need a project plan. They need to feel seen. But when good work plateaus and standards drift, warmth without honesty becomes a ceiling.

The leaders who navigate this well aren’t shapeshifters. They’re not performing. They’ve simply built a wider range – the ability to sense what the moment requires and respond with the right presence, not just their most comfortable one.

Think of it like a musician who’s mastered more than one key. They don’t abandon their signature sound. They expand it. The power chord is still there when the song calls for it. But so is the fingerpicking. So is the silence between notes.

The hard part isn’t learning new modes of presence. It’s recognizing that your default – the thing that got you here – might be the thing that’s limiting what comes next.

Not because it’s a weakness. Because it’s a strength you’ve overused.

The leader who’s all vision and no execution isn’t dreaming too big. She’s playing one chord. The leader who’s all structure and no warmth isn’t heartless. He’s playing one chord. The leader who makes everyone feel great but never pushes them isn’t soft. She’s playing one chord.

Range isn’t about being someone else. It’s about being more of yourself – in more situations, for more people, when it matters most.

A quick diagnostic. Six scenarios. Notice which ones feel natural and which ones make you flinch:

  1. Your team has lost sight of why the work matters. Do you re-cast the vision – or does that feel too abstract?

  2. A project is slipping. Do you bring structure and clarity – or does that feel like micromanaging?

  3. A strong performer is waiting for permission they don’t need. Do you step back – or does the silence make you nervous?

  4. Someone is stuck, repeating the same mistakes. Do you ask questions that help them see it – or do you just give the answer?

  5. Morale is low, and fear is creeping in. Do you name what you’re noticing – or do you push past it to the work?

  6. Good work has plateaued. Do you name the gap between where it is and where it could be – or do you let it slide?

The ones that made you uncomfortable? Those are your underdeveloped voices. Not weaknesses. Untapped range.

Your power chord got you here. Your range is what gets you – and your team – where you’re going next.
