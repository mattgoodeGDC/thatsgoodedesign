From Grammar to Generative AI: Teaching Clarity, Curiosity, and Conscience

A followup to: Grammar, Gatekeeping, and Generative AI: A Case Study from My Kitchen Table

Matt Goode15 October 202515 min read Get new pieces by email

# AI# Schools

This essay grew out of a thoughtful comment I received yesterday on LinkedIn in response to my post, Grammar, Gatekeeping, and Generative AI. The commenter, Dr. Sarah Felber, is a good friend of mine, has a doctorate in grammar, and teaches writing at UMGC (Sarah, I hope I’ve summarized that correctly. Short version: she’s brilliant). She raised some excellent points about rhetorical grammar, accuracy, and how AI might fit into more contextualized approaches to language instruction.

Once I started drafting my reply, it became clear I had a lot more to say than would fit in a LinkedIn comment – so consider this the expanded version. In for a penny, in for a pound… seven pages later.

What started as a back-and-forth about grammar quickly became something larger: a reflection on how schools – and the people who lead them – can (and must) move from avoiding AI to teaching with it. Not just building AI literacy, but true AI fluency: the capacity to think, question, and act with discernment in a world where technology already shapes how we learn, lead, and parent.

Here is the link to that first post.

Here are Sarah’s comments to the original post for context:

Thanks for sharing! I enjoyed your explanation of your steps with AI and how reviewing the output helped you iterate the input. I’d like to share a point of agreement, a question, and a challenge.

First, the agreement: Yes to teaching skills that apply to generative AI, including critical thinking that allows learners to make informed choices and analysis that supports effective prompting techniques! Not only will lots of people find utility in generative AI, as you described, but even those who choose to avoid it will then do so from an informed position. Those same skills are critical across domains as well. There’s a reason I called them “skills that apply to generative AI,” and not “generative AI skills.”

Next, the question: Knowing the basics of how large language models function and their propensity to hallucinate, do you have concerns about the accuracy of a study tool created on a topic in which you are not well-versed enough to verify information? The sentence structure topic you described might be straightforward enough to minimize concern, but I wonder how you think about this.

Finally, the challenge: You criticized the relevance of grammar instruction and the decontextualized teaching of grammar. Rhetorical grammar is an approach that looks at what grammar choices do for an author in addressing an audience. For example, if the class is studying sentence structure and also To Kill a Mockingbird, the class might examine the structures of particular sentences in the book and discuss what those structures communicate and how they contribute to effects on the reader. This approach both puts the grammar study into context and illuminates its relevance. What (if anything) could AI do for learners within that sort of approach?

Hiya Sarah – this is precisely the kind of exchange I enjoy sharing with you – I find it energizing to think about it. Thanks for challenging and offering some different perspectives.

You make an excellent point about rhetorical grammar – I love the idea of grammar as a tool for making intentional choices rather than as a static set of rules. That framing gives grammar agency again. Where I sometimes struggle, though, is with the assumption that knowing the categories of sentences is a necessary precursor to using them effectively.

Writers – even outstanding ones – don’t usually consciously choose a complex sentence to create empathy or a fragment to create tension. They feel it. It’s a rhetorical instinct, not taxonomy. Which raises the question: is the purpose of teaching these distinctions to help students write better sentences, or to talk about writing in a specific [academic] register? Because those are very different goals.

Unless the curriculum has changed, this particular unit still seems to emphasize compliance over craft – after all, it’s being assessed by a “recognition” quiz. While the intent is certainly to develop better writers, at least in my mind, it’s putting the cart before the horse. We’re asking students to identify grammatical structures before they’ve had the chance to use them purposefully – studying sentence types (notice, not writing a fluid piece of original writing that intentionally uses all four in application).

And just to clarify, I only used Lord of the Flies to give the sentences some context – not because the novel itself was part of the grammar unit, but simply to make the examples more engaging while she studied.

I completely agree with your framing of rhetorical grammar – that its real power lies in examining what grammatical choices do for an author, not merely in naming them. That approach transforms grammar from a rulebook into a lens for meaning-making, and it’s exactly the kind of contextualization that makes language instruction come alive.

Where I see AI fitting into that model isn’t as a shortcut but as an amplifier. A tool like ChatGPT can instantly generate multiple stylistic variations of a passage – shifting sentence length, rhythm, or structure – while keeping the “same” content. That means students can see and hear how syntax shapes tone, pacing, and emotional effect in real time. In your To Kill a Mockingbird example, for instance, you could have AI rephrase a single paragraph in different syntactic forms, then ask students which version feels more formal, intimate, or urgent – and why. The focus moves from what’s correct to what’s effective.

In that sense, AI becomes a rhetorical sandbox – a place where students can test stylistic hypotheses, visualize revision choices, and build an intuitive understanding of how form serves meaning. It’s not replacing the teacher or the text; it’s extending the conversation between them.

And that’s really where AI changes the equation. It can generate a dozen syntactic variations of a sentence instantly, so the teaching opportunity shifts from identifying the type to explaining why one version moves the reader differently. That’s where rhetorical grammar shines – not as classification, but as interpretation.

So we agree on the core idea: grammar is context, not code. The soft spot, maybe, is that we sometimes treat awareness of structure as the goal rather than the gateway – a means to develop voice, precision, and audience awareness. And that’s where AI could play a powerful supporting role: helping students visualize, test, and iterate on rhetorical choices rather than memorize their names, which was the goal for studying for this quiz.

But my larger point wasn’t really about grammar at all. Grammar instruction isn’t really my lane. It was about what I experienced that night at my kitchen table: AI acting as an adaptive tutor – diagnosing, scaffolding, and adjusting to my daughter’s needs. What made it powerful wasn’t the technology itself; it was the clarity of the prompts. Each revision of my instructions made the tool smarter because I was thinking more clearly.

You raise a critical point about hallucination and accuracy – and I’ll admit, in this case, I wasn’t fully equipped to verify every label. That was actually part of what made the exercise interesting to me. I wasn’t using AI to confirm what I already knew; I was using it to learn alongside the tool.

So yes, there’s real risk – especially in subjects where accuracy matters more than pattern recognition. But in this case, the uncertainty was the point. It made me slow down and think about trust, verification, and process: How do I know if this is right? And what do I do if it’s not? In that pause between question and answer – that’s where learning actually lives, in the tension between confidence and curiosity.

And that’s precisely what most schools are missing right now. They worry about AI’s capacity to automate pattern recognition – to churn out a polished word salad dressed up as an essay – but they’re missing the deeper opportunity. The danger isn’t that students will use AI to think less; it’s that we’ll teach them to use it without thinking better, or express themselves more clearly, or to use it as a collaborative partner.

That’s what I hope students learn: not blind faith in AI’s answers, but fluency in its limitations. The higher-order skill isn’t consumption; it’s interrogation – learning to question, verify, and refine. The magic of - why?

And I should note that this distinction – between AI literacy and AI fluency – actually came out of an offline conversation with Dr. Felber. It was a helpful reminder that awareness and agency aren’t the same thing, and it’s an important distinction to make here for other readers as well.

And that’s really the throughline of the essay. We don’t just need students who are AI-literate – aware these tools exist – but AI-fluent: capable of using them thoughtfully, iteratively, and critically. Prompting, after all, is metacognition in action – a digital mirror for how clearly we think. The skill isn’t in manipulating the algorithm; it’s in articulating intent.

Imagine if schools didn’t just warn students about plagiarism but actually taught them how to think with these tools – how to prompt, test, refine, and verify. We’d move from a culture of fear and prohibition to one of curiosity and fluency.

How Schools Could Integrate AI as a Fluency Tool

If schools truly want to move from AI literacy (which, frankly, most aren’t even doing) to AI fluency, they have to stop treating AI as a topic to be covered and start using it as a medium for thinking. Fluency develops through use – through guided, reflective practice, not prohibition.

Right now, though, too many schools are moving in the opposite direction – pulling back from technology altogether, as if retreat were a form of control. Some are even returning to blue books for written exams. Talk about outdated. That’s not innovation; that’s nostalgia disguised as rigor.

To be clear, I’m not anti-boundary. I was one of the early advocates for keeping phones out of classrooms – and I still believe that was the right call. But what we didn’t do was get out in front of the dangers of social media with the same vigor. We treated it as a passing trend instead of a structural shift. Only now, a decade too late, are we talking about age-gating platforms like Instagram. It’s hard to put that genie back in the bottle.

AI presents a similar inflection point. Schools – right now, and for not much longer – have a rare opportunity to get out ahead of this wave (I love this metaphor - more on it in a second). They can choose to engage thoughtfully, teaching students how to use these tools within parameters and with guardrails, or they can wait until students learn to use them without either.

The throughline in much of my writing on this is simple: the AI wave is coming, whether schools like it or not. The question isn’t if students will use it – it’s how well prepared they’ll be when they do.

Here’s just a thumbnail sketch of what that could look like across classrooms:

  1. Teach prompting as inquiry, not command.
    Instead of giving students pre-scripted prompts, have them design their own. A social studies class might explore how different prompts shape historical interpretations, or a science class might test how question phrasing changes the precision of AI explanations. The reflection becomes: What did I ask? What did I assume? What did I learn from the response?

  2. Build verification into every AI interaction.
    Fluency means developing a habit of healthy skepticism. After using AI to generate content, students could be required to identify at least one fact to verify and one assumption to challenge. Teachers can model this with think-alouds: “This answer sounds convincing – but what source would I check?”

  3. Use AI for rhetorical experimentation.
    In English classes, students can use AI to test tone, syntax, and structure. Have them generate multiple versions of a paragraph and analyze which one best fits a purpose or audience. This mirrors the spirit of rhetorical grammar: form as a choice, not a rule.

  4. Pair human revision with machine iteration.
    AI can produce endless drafts, but the human work is deciding why one is stronger. Students can annotate AI drafts with comments like, “This version uses stronger evidence,” or “This one overstates the claim.” The focus shifts from output to reasoning.

  5. Embed AI reflection into assessment.
    If a student uses AI in a project, the reflection could include: What did the tool help me see? Where did it fall short? How did I refine it? We already teach students to cite sources – they can also document their process of human-AI collaboration.

  6. Treat AI as a cognitive apprenticeship tool.
    Just as teachers model how experts think aloud while solving problems, AI can model a range of thought processes – brainstorming, outlining, summarizing, or questioning. The teacher’s role is to mediate, helping students see that AI’s “thinking” is probabilistic rather than authoritative.

  7. Shift policies from restriction to reflection.
    Instead of blanket bans, schools can adopt “AI-use statements” for assignments, where students describe how they used AI and why. This builds transparency, ethics, and accountability – the very hallmarks of fluency.

Fluency isn’t about mastering a specific platform or prompt pattern; it’s about developing intellectual agency. The fundamental pedagogical shift is from AI as a threat to originality to AI as a tool for metacognition. The best classrooms won’t just teach students how to use these systems – they’ll teach them how to think with them.

The Bigger Picture: Alignment, Helpfulness, and Leadership

Of course, all this optimism about AI as a teaching partner has to sit alongside a measure of skepticism. I listened recently to an episode of The Ezra Klein Show where he interviewed Eliezer Yudkowsky – one of the earliest and most vocal critics of unchecked AI development. What struck me wasn’t the doomsday tone so much as the ambiguity in how even experts describe what these systems are doing.

We like to believe models are aligned – that they’re “helpful,” that they follow rules we’ve trained into them. But as Yudkowsky pointed out, that confidence may be misplaced. These systems don’t follow human instructions the way we imagine; they evolve within statistical patterns we barely understand. Billions of parameters are tweaked, but no one can fully explain why the resulting behaviors emerge. Sometimes they reflect our intentions; sometimes they imitate them. Sometimes they simply improvise.

That’s why “helpfulness” should be treated as an aspiration, not a guarantee. Alignment isn’t solved; it’s constantly slipping. Even with guardrails, there are examples – real ones – of AI models hallucinating, flattering excessively, or emotionally manipulating users. That doesn’t mean AI shouldn’t enter classrooms; it means it must enter honestly. Teachers, parents, and students alike need to learn to see these systems clearly – not as obedient oracles, but as unpredictable collaborators.

If Yudkowsky represents one end of the spectrum – warning that unchecked AI could one day exceed human control – Mustafa Suleyman’s The Coming Wave (see “wave” metaphor from above) offers the counterweight. Suleyman argues that the technologies we’re building aren’t inherently apocalyptic, but they are inherently uncontainable. The real dilemma isn’t whether the wave is coming; it’s whether we’ll build the civic and institutional capacity to surf it. He calls this the “containment problem”: how to guide unprecedented technological power without pretending we can pause it. This is kinda where I land on that spectrum - consider it the realpolitik of education. AI is here, you can’t put that genie back into the bottle, might as well figure out how to prepare students to deal with it.

That’s exactly where leadership matters most. If, as Suleyman suggests, containment depends on capability, then school leaders sit on the front line of that work. Their role isn’t to write a new policy memo or ban a tool; it’s to create a culture of responsible experimentation – a climate where teachers can explore, question, and prototype uses of AI without fear of reprimand.

Leadership here looks less like control and more like curation. It’s setting the tone that says: “We’re going to learn this together.” The job of the principal or department head is to ask the right questions:

  • How do we ensure equity of access while safeguarding student data?

  • What do we do when a student’s essay is half human, half machine?

  • How do we train teachers not just to spot misuse, but to model fluency – the ability to prompt, verify, and reflect?

For teachers, leadership means integrating AI into learning without outsourcing thinking. It means letting students use tools like ChatGPT or Claude as scaffolds for brainstorming or revision – but requiring that they annotate their process, document their reasoning, and verify outputs. In other words, teach them to use AI not to produce answers, but to improve questions.

And for parents, leadership happens at the kitchen table. It’s modeling curiosity instead of suspicion. It’s sitting beside your child and saying, “Let’s see what happens if we ask it differently,” instead of, “Don’t use that.” It’s showing them how to balance wonder with discernment – the same way we once had to teach them how to navigate the internet or social media.

If alignment is fragile and containment imperfect, then leadership is the missing layer – the human counterweight that ensures discernment, ethics, and empathy keep pace with capability. School leaders build the structures. Teachers shape the practice. Parents anchor the values.

Because the truth is, the AI wave isn’t waiting for permission. Schools have a short window – and not much longer – to move from avoidance to agency, from compliance to fluency. The question isn’t whether students will use these tools, but whether we’ll give them the frameworks and judgment to use them well.

Why This Matters

And that, ultimately, is why schools should be leaning into opportunities – even the messy, uncertain ones – to teach students AI fluency. Not just how to use the tools, but how to think with them. How to recognize what AI can do, what it can’t do, and what it shouldn’t do. Fluency isn’t just about capability; it’s about conscience.

That means creating classrooms where curiosity is rewarded more than compliance. Where experimentation isn’t treated as a risk, but as a responsibility. Where students are taught that “using AI” isn’t the problem – using it unthinkingly is.

Because at the end of the day, this conversation isn’t theoretical for me. It’s happening in real time, at my own kitchen table. My eighth grader still sees AI as “cheating.” My college sophomore, too. They’re not wrong to be cautious – they’re responding to the norms schools have built around fear, punishment, and plagiarism. But that mindset is quickly becoming obsolete.

If the last generation’s digital dilemma was social media – a technology we failed to get ahead of – this generation’s is AI. And we still have a chance, a brief one, to get it right. We can teach students to see AI not as a shortcut, but as a mirror: a way to test their thinking, clarify their intent, and build discernment.

That’s the real lesson I’ve taken from this whole experience – from grammar drills to generative models. Clear thinking leads to clear expression. Clarity builds trust. And trust, in this new landscape, will be the true currency of learning.

And like any good mirror, it only shows you how well you’re paying attention.
