1. Why This Series Exists: EQ in the Age of AI

Essay 1 of 7

Matt Goode3 November 20256 min read Get new pieces by email

# EQ# AI

It Started With a Paradox

I spend my days helping leaders design systems that make human work work better.

As a solopreneur, I rely on AI the way some rely on teams – as a silent, tireless partner that helps me move faster, think clearer, and ship awork that would otherwise stay stuck in notebooks. It isn’t just a tool. It’s a multiplier.

But AI isn’t just part of how I build. It’s part of how I think.

Before consulting, I spent years in and around education – designing systems, coaching leaders, and trying to bridge the gap between theory and practice. Though I’ve moved beyond that sector professionally, I haven’t moved on from it intellectually. I still think often about learning – how we grow, how we teach, and what education looks like in a world where machines can now teach themselves.

Much of my writing lives at that intersection: between AI and emotional intelligence, systems and soul, the future of work and the future of learning. It’s where my curiosity loops back, again and again.

Because no matter how fast the tools get, the deeper questions remain:
What kind of humans are we designing for? And how do we make sure they’re not left behind?

Lately, I’ve noticed something strange. The more we automate, the more people ache for the parts of leadership that can’t be automated – clarity, empathy, meaning.

Every dashboard, every KPI, every leadership algorithm kept leading me back to one realization: we’ve become better at managing systems than we have at managing ourselves.

And that’s where this series began – with the quiet recognition that the next competitive advantage isn’t artificial intelligence.

It’s applied emotional intelligence.

Yes, AI will transform. It will disrupt. In some cases, it will even destroy systems, roles, and entire ways of working. But that’s only half the story.

Because just as much as AI will dismantle, it will also create – new tools, new workflows, new forms of value we haven’t yet imagined. It’s both demolition and design. Not a clean upgrade, but a messy re-architecture of how we think, lead, and build.

The leaders I work with aren’t just asking, “How do we keep up?”
They’re asking, “What do we want to build in the rubble?”

That’s the real opportunity – not just to brace for the future, but to shape it. Intentionally. Ethically. With enough emotional intelligence to remember what all this technology is ultimately for: better human outcomes.

A few weeks ago, a senior executive asked me, “How do I lead when I’m surrounded by people – and machines – that can think faster than I can?”

That question landed hard. Because under it was another: What’s left for us when the thinking is outsourced?

We’re entering an era that won’t just test our intellect. It will test our humanity.

And that’s why I’m writing this series – to help leaders navigate that threshold with both rigor and soul. To remember that while IQ might be the engine’s horsepower, EQ is the skill of learning to drive stick – the awareness in motion that tells you when to ease off the clutch, when to shift, and when to let the engine breathe simply.

Why Washington

As I worked through the early drafts of this series, I kept coming back to one unlikely case study: George Washington.

As a decidedly apolitical figure, he occupies a rare place in American memory – known well enough that most people can sketch the broad contours of his life, yet distant enough to remain almost mythic. He’s less a politician than a symbol: of restraint, integrity, and a kind of leadership shaped more by character than charisma.

That’s part of what makes him such a useful mirror in the age of AI. In a world defined by velocity and noise, he reminds us that stillness is also a form of strength – that clarity and presence still matter more than cleverness or volume. Leadership isn’t always about being the loudest voice; sometimes it’s about being the quietest listener.

He lived long before algorithms or machine learning. Yet, his leadership reads like a modern manual on emotional intelligence – grounded in self-awareness, impulse control, empathy, ethical foresight, and a steady commitment to mentoring others into leadership. –

Washington was both systemic and soulful – a founder who didn’t rely on personality to move people but on design that outlasted him. He modeled what I often describe as a balanced engine: six cylinders of culture, trust, execution, adaptability, teaching, and ethics working in unison beneath the surface – CREATE before its time.

And when he did act, he shifted gears with discernment. He knew when to advance, when to hold, and when to yield – a living example of VOICES long before the framework had a name.

He didn’t build an empire; he built a culture. And that distinction is as relevant to AI-era organizations as it was to a fledgling democracy.

Because the challenge hasn’t changed – only the speed.

Why Emotional Intelligence Now

We’re living through the fastest redefinition of work in human history. AI, automation, and analytics are rewriting what competence even means.

Yet no matter how advanced the tools become, people still need to feel seen, safe, and significant. That doesn’t change with technology – it becomes more urgent because of it.

Emotional intelligence isn’t a counterweight to AI. It’s the interface that makes intelligence usable, sustainable, and humane. Without it, intelligence becomes extraction – data without discernment, output without empathy. With it, intelligence becomes design – systems that listen, adapt, and serve.

Because AI may amplify horsepower, but only EQ teaches us how to drive it.

It’s the awareness in motion – the quiet listening that tells us when to shift, when to slow, and when to let the engine breathe.

Why Me

I’ve spent two decades at the intersection of education, leadership, and system design – from classrooms to federal agencies to executive boardrooms.

I’m not a futurist; I’m a facilitator. My work lives in the space between data and dignity – helping people see that culture isn’t an outcome. It’s architecture.

And like any good structure, it must be designed to adapt – to shifting weight, new tools, and faster systems.

This series is my way of mapping that architecture for an age when the boundaries between human and machine are blurring. It’s both an argument and a field guide – and, more than anything, an act of hope.

Because emotional intelligence isn’t disappearing. It’s evolving – becoming the adaptive framework that keeps human leadership steady as everything else accelerates.

EQ is what helps the structure breathe. It’s what keeps the cylinders aligned and the gears responsive, reminding us that even in an age of automation, the system still runs best when it listens.

Why This Series

Each essay in this series is a different lens on the same truth: the leaders who will thrive in the next decade will be those who can think deeply, feel clearly, and act ethically – all at once.

What began as scattered notes – half reflections, half experiments – eventually crystallized into seven essays.

Each one explores a different gear in the system, a different way emotional intelligence translates from awareness into action.

  1. The New Leadership Divide – balancing head and heart in the age of automation.

  2. The Five Facets of EQ AI Can’t Replicate – translating empathy into execution.

  3. Learning to Learn in the Machine Age – emotional intelligence as adaptive muscle.

  4. Coaching the Human Back into Leadership – mentorship as system design.

  5. The Ethical Compass in an Algorithmic World – slowing down when speed tempts us to forget.

  6. Emotional Intelligence as Strategy – EQ as infrastructure, not inspiration.

  7. The EQ Flywheel – how awareness becomes alignment, action, and renewal.

Together, these essays form a map – not for managing machines, but for designing the humanness that makes systems work.

Because culture isn’t code.

It’s chemistry.

And when leaders tune into that frequency – the quiet hum between intellect and intuition – they build something AI still can’t: cultures that feel alive.

A Gentle Invitation

If this series resonates, it’s probably because you already feel the same tension I do – that quiet pull between speed and soul, data and discernment, information and wisdom.

You’ve likely sensed it in your own work – the hum beneath the dashboards, the ache for meaning that no metric quite measures.

I don’t claim to have all, or even any, of the answers.

This series is simply my attempt to map the questions – to get them out of my head and into the marketplace of ideas, where they can be challenged, reshaped, and refined.

I hope you’ll join that dialogue. Comment, repost, disagree, expand – that’s how intelligence evolves. After all, who doesn’t love a good Hegelian dialectic?

Because the point isn’t to resist technology. It’s to reclaim humanity – not as nostalgia, but as strategy.

To remember that leadership, like driving, is still a felt art: you listen for the engine, sense the timing, and shift with care.

Let’s build that future – intentionally, emotionally, and together.
