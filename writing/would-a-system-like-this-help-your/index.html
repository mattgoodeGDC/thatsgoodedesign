Would a system like this help your practice?

The path from stranger to conversation, and the three choices that make it work

Matt Goode2 October 20265 min read Get new pieces by email

# Growing faster than it's getting clearer

I mean that as a real question. If you run a practice of one or a few, as a consultant, coach, or founder, and you’ve noticed that everything in the business waits on you, I’d like to know whether what I’m about to describe would be useful to you. I’m not selling anything. I’m trying to find out whether this is worth building for anyone other than me.

Here’s why I’m asking.

Winging it

Most of my working week goes into helping founders get the work out of their heads. Write down the decision. Name the owner. Build the thing that runs when you’re not in the room.

This spring, I admitted I’d always been winging it.

It wasn’t one moment. It was a slow realization. I sell systems thinking, and I hadn’t applied it to my own business. When I had a few clients, I could keep it all in my head. Then the business grew, and the exact thing I help founders through happened to me. I outgrew my own head.

So I built a system, essentially an operating system for the business. I call it GOs, short for Goode Operating System, and because I can’t resist a clever name, it “goes.” It isn’t one new app. It’s a small set of decisions about how the work moves, held together by tools I already used. I see it as a complete ecosystem.

The shape of it

Picture a map. On the left, the audience comes in: people who read something I wrote, see a post, hear me speak. On the right, delivery and money go out: coaching, client work, invoices. In the middle sits a hub, one shared list that every part of the system reads from and writes to.

That’s the whole layout. Everything else is detail about how a person gets from left to right.

Here’s what the two routes below run on:

  • Where people find me. LinkedIn, this newsletter, and my website.

  • Turning interest into contacts. Jotform, Calendly, and Kit.

  • Moving data between them. Zapier.

  • Tasks and records. Airtable as the CRM and Todoist as the one shared list, with Gmail, Google Drive, and Google Calendar alongside.

  • Having the conversation. Google Meet.

Another thirty or so handle delivery, publishing, the websites, and getting paid. That’s about forty tools in all. The tool count is the least interesting thing about the system. It’s also the reason the system needed rules: forty tools with nothing deciding how they connect is just a bigger head.

Two routes, from stranger to conversation

The map has a lot of routes. Two of them matter for this question because together they show how someone goes from never having heard of me to talking with me.

Route one: quiz to CRM. It starts with a LinkedIn post. It includes a link to my leadership quiz, built in Jotform. When someone finishes the quiz, Zapier carries their result into DALE, the CRM I built myself in Airtable. An email sequence in Kit starts. And the lead is scored, so I can see at a glance who is curious and who might want to talk.

My part is the writing. The plumbing does the rest.

Route two: discovery call. Someone books a call through Calendly. A short prep form goes out, so we both arrive knowing why we’re there. The call lands on my Google Calendar with a Meet link. We talk. Afterward, I update the CRM so the conversation doesn’t live only in my memory.

Put the two routes end to end and you have the path from stranger to conversation. Neither route is clever. What makes them work is three choices underneath.

A system is three choices, not a stack of tools

When people hear “system,” they picture software. The software was the easy part. The hard part was deciding three things.

  1. Every task gets a permanent name. Not “that thing for the newsletter.” A name that never changes, so I can point at a job from anywhere and know exactly which one I mean.

  2. Every job has one home. Each kind of work gets done in one place. If two places could own a job, nothing owns it.

  3. The parts only talk to each other through one shared list. No side channels. If a job needs to hand something on, it goes on the list, and the next part picks it up from there.

Notice there isn’t a tool in that list. You could run all three on paper.

This is delegation, pointed at yourself

In CLUTCH I wrote about DELEGATE, my framework for handing work over so it stays handed over. The heart of it is the difference between the Title and the License. You keep the Title: you’re still accountable. You hand over the License: the right to run the work day-to-day, inside guardrails you’ve made clear.

Most solo practitioners never delegate, because there’s no one to delegate to. But you can delegate to a system. The routes above hold the License for the routine work: the follow-up email, the calendar invite, the update to the record. I keep the Title. I decide who I talk to and what I say.

The system is the team member who never forgets.

On AI, since people ask: AI handles the organizing and automating. I do the writing.

What I outgrew

What broke first was me. As a solopreneur, I felt like I was drinking from a firehose. Everything arrived at once, and it all came to me because I was the only place it could go.

The tools followed the same pattern. My CRM started life in Google Sheets, held together with a little JavaScript I wrote myself. That worked until the sheet got more complex than a sheet should be, so I moved it to Airtable. My bookkeeping went the same way. Sheets was my QuickBooks for a while, until I outgrew that too and built a small accounting app in Airtable.

What surprised me was that the bigger fix wasn’t more effort or better apps. It was those three choices. Once every task had a name and a home, the firehose became a list.

So, back to you

Most coaches, consultants, and founders I know run their business the way I ran mine: well, from memory, and a little too late at night. The client work is good. The back office is a person.

So: would a system like this help your practice? Not my exact setup, which is shaped around my odd mix of work. Yours is built on the same three choices you own and run.

If you haven’t built one yet, and you’ve got two minutes, tell me. Six questions. I read every answer, and I’ll only follow up if you say I can.
