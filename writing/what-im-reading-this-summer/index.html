What I’m Reading This Summer

Matt Goode19 May 20268 min read Get new pieces by email

# Off the clock

Apologies, reader. It’s been a busy few weeks since I got back from Boston. I haven’t had much time for writing, with business projects booming and (ironically) two books getting ready for release. Plus, my son is home briefly before starting his co-op…

My dad actually emailed me the other day to ask if I had fallen and couldn’t get up, because he hadn’t seen a Substack post in a while. OK, Dad, I get it, neglecting this forum.

So, here’s my first post in a while:

Every summer I build a stack. Seven to ten books. Not a TBR, not aspirational – the books I’m actually going to read between Memorial Day and Labor Day, in roughly the order I’ll read them.

The tradition started inside curriculum redesign. One year I switched grades and inherited an Ancient Egypt unit. I prefer thematic teaching to geographic teaching, and I brought that approach to life in year two. But in year one, I wanted to read everything about Egypt. Toby Wilkinson’s The Last Dynasty. Kara Cooney’s When Women Ruled the World. That set the pattern.

Other summers got their own obsessions. One was World War One – Tuchman’s Guns of August, which I’d already read in isolation. But that summer, rather than serially read one book at a time, I organized my list, jumping from book to book, chapter by chapter, building the war chronologically across writers. Another summer it was Iliad retellings: Madeline Miller’s Circe and The Song of Achilles, Natalie Haynes’s A Thousand Ships, Caroline Alexander’s The War That Killed Achilles.

Those two summers eventually fused into a book of my own, The Wrath of Archie Ellis, currently being proofread. I’m aiming for an autumn 2026 release for that book, my third. It’s a structural retelling of the Iliad set at the Third Battle of Ypres in 1917 – Achilles and Hector rewritten as a British captain and a German Hauptmann. Twenty-four chapters, one for each book of Homer. I wanted to know what the rage of Achilles looks like without the elegy – and what kleos looks like in the age of modern war.

2016 was election books, naturally. Mayer’s Dark Money. Cramer’s classic about the 1988 race, What It Takes. And some prophetic doom-reading: Roth’s The Plot Against America and Lewis’s It Can’t Happen Here back to back. Lessig’s Republic, Lost for the campaign finance rot underneath all of it.

The Covid summer, when no one was going anywhere, was travelogues only. William Heat-Moon’s Blue Highways, Mayle’s Provence, Theroux’s The Great Railway Bazaar, Troost’s The Sex Lives of Cannibals, Oxenreider’s At Home in the World. That one bled into a longer arc – Frankopan, Weatherford, Harl, Anthony’s The Horse, the Wheel, and Language – on how the Silk Road and the steppe quietly built the modern world while everyone was looking the other way.

This year’s stack is heavier on nonfiction than usual, lighter on fiction, and built around a single question that’s been sitting with me since February of last year: What does it mean to build something durable in a moment when so much is being dismantled?

I run a small leadership coaching and consulting practice. Before that, nearly two decades in independent school education, and a stint at USAID’s Bureau of Humanitarian Assistance before the 2025 RIF. The throughline across all three institutions is the same one – how they form people, how they fail people, how they get built and broken. Most of what’s on this list is, one way or another, about that.

Here’s the stack.

1. Into the Wood Chipper: A Whistleblower’s Account of How the Trump Administration Shredded USAID – Nicholas Enrich. I have to start here. Enrich was USAID’s acting assistant administrator for global health when he was placed on administrative leave in March 2025 for telling the truth about what was happening to the agency. I lived a version of this story from a different seat, and I owe it to my former colleagues to read the version that becomes the record. Atul Gawande wrote the foreword. This is the book I’ve been putting off because I’m not sure I want to reread a lived experience. I think I’m ready now. It goes first.

2. One Day, Everyone Will Have Always Been Against This – Omar El Akkad. El Akkad’s title is the whole argument: institutions – and the people inside them – tend to discover their moral courage retroactively. The gap between stated values and revealed values is where most of the damage happens. The book is built around Gaza, and around what El Akkad calls the gap between the West's lofty ideals and its bloodstained reality. He's writing for Western centrists and liberals who didn't speak. I read it from the other side of that gap. At the time of the RIF, USAID's humanitarian response was being constrained by the Biden administration for the sake of the alliance with Israel – meaning the agency I worked for was being dismantled at exactly the moment it was already being prevented from doing the work it existed to do. Two institutional failures running at the same time. Reading this after Enrich isn't a pairing I planned so much as one that became inevitable.

3. Crisis Engineering: Time-Tested Tools for Turning Chaos into Clarity – Marina Nitze, Matthew Weaver, and Mikey Dickerson. This is the constructive inverse of the first two. Dickerson founded USDS after the healthcare.gov rescue; Nitze was CTO of the VA. Between them, they've spent a decade teaching federal agencies how to function under pressure. The book's opening question is When the system breaks, what do you do? – which, frankly, is most of what I do in my business. If the first two books are about how government capacity gets destroyed, this one is about how it gets built. I expect to mark it up heavily – there's real material here for the consulting work I do with mission-driven organizations.

4. The Coming Storm: Power, Conflict, and Warnings from History – Odd Arne Westad. Westad is a serious historian writing at the height of his powers. Most of what I read about the current moment is hot-take journalism that ages badly within weeks. Westad operates at a different time-scale, and I want my sense of scale recalibrated before the fall. What captured my attention was his appearance on the podcast Hidden Forces, where he frames his argument around the premise that 2026 looks a lot like 1914. See my interest in WW1, and its similarities with the Bronze Age Collapse and the end of the Edwardian World.

5. How Great Ideas Happen: The Hidden Steps Behind Breakthrough Success – George Newman. A cognitive scientist on the empirical mechanics of breakthrough thinking. I’ve spent years building operational frameworks for clients – CREATE, VOICES, the 6C Decision Compass – and I want to read someone with research credentials writing seriously about how good ideas actually emerge. I’ll be reading this with a pen in hand and probably arguing with it on every other page.

6. Strong Ground: The Lessons of Daring Leadership, the Tenacity of Paradox, and the Wisdom of the Human Spirit – Brené Brown. Brown’s argument is that bluster and cruelty are increasingly being framed as strength. She’s writing into the same cultural moment I write into, with a much bigger platform. I want to read her not as a fan but as a colleague – to see where her packaging is sharper than mine, where mine is sharper than hers, and what she’s missing that I should be saying louder.

7. The Power of Mattering – Zach Mercurio. The premise: showing people they matter is the most underrated lever in leadership. This sits squarely inside my EQ-i 2.0 and CliftonStrengths work, and I suspect at least one client conversation and one essay will come out of it. Short, well-researched, and immediately applicable.

8. Primal Intelligence: You Are Smarter Than You Know – Angus Fletcher. Fletcher draws on case studies of visionary thinkers and U.S. Army Special Operators to argue there’s a trainable form of intelligence that outperforms AI under volatility and uncertainty. I’ve written publicly about AI as a tool rather than a shortcut, and I want to read someone making a serious, non-hyped argument for what humans still uniquely contribute. This is the wildcard on the list – the one I expect to surprise me.

9. Open Socrates – Agnes Callard. Callard is one of the few living philosophers writing real philosophy for general readers. Her argument is that the examined life – the actual, rigorous Socratic version – looks almost nothing like what most of us call self-reflection. The Socratic method is the spine of what I do as a coach. Callard is going to press on whether I’m really doing it or just gesturing at it. This is the book I expect to change how I work.

10. The Death of Ivan Ilyich – Leo Tolstoy. The one classic on the list, and the one I should have read years ago. A successful judge confronts the meaninglessness of a life spent climbing institutional ladders without ever asking what he actually valued. I’m reading this at exactly the right moment – post-RIF, mid-pivot, building something I hope is the answer to the question Ivan Ilyich never quite asked himself in time. Short enough for two evenings. I expect it’ll stay with me for years.

That’s the stack. Witness and world, practice and philosophy. I’ll report back in September.

If you’re putting together your own list, I’d love to hear what’s on it. Reply or drop me a line.

– MG

Two of my own, since you’re here.

If you’re looking to put together your own summer reading list…

Balance – out now. My debut novel. Ellie Beckett is a CRISPR scientist in Kendall Square, thirteen years into a relationship with Alex, when her CEO starts paying a different kind of attention. Four POVs, no villains, no easy answers. The Ethical Slut explains ethical non-monogamy. An Atlantic piece argues about it. Balance lets you feel it – chosen family, female mentorship in STEM, the slow honesty of loving more than one person well. Find it on Amazon or on my author website: www.mkirkwrites.com .

CLUTCH – July 2026. The nonfiction one. I’m looking for a few more advance readers. If you want a free copy of the manuscript in exchange for an honest review at launch, click here to sign up for the ARC team. The argument: organizations grow people into leadership roles faster than they grow leadership capability, and the gap is where most of the damage happens. CLUTCH is the system I built to close it – an engine, a gearbox, and a GPS for founders and operators trying to lead under volatility without burning out the humans around them. If you’ve ever inherited a team you didn’t build, promoted someone before they were ready because no one else was, or watched a “good” decision-making process produce a bad decision in slow motion, the book is for you.
