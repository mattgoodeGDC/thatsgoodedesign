You Didn’t Delegate. You Dumped.

Matt Goode11 February 20263 min read Get new pieces by email

# DELEGATE

Three words. Total confidence from the leader. Total confusion for everyone else.

They handed over the frame. They forgot the picture.

What does “it” look like when it’s done? What’s in bounds and what’s off limits? When should they check in – or should they not bother you at all? None of that was said. None of that was asked. The leader walked away feeling productive. The person left holding the work walked away guessing.

This is the most common delegation failure in organizations, and it has nothing to do with trust or control. It’s a clarity problem. The leader knows what they mean. They just didn’t say it.

Here’s what makes this invisible: from the leader’s perspective, the handoff felt generous. They gave someone ownership. They showed trust. They got out of the way.

But “getting out of the way” without setting the frame isn’t empowerment. It’s abandonment with accountability attached.

I learned this in classrooms long before I saw it in boardrooms. No teacher would hand a student a textbook and say, “learn chapter seven” without context, scaffolding, or success criteria. You’d never say “write an essay” without explaining what a good one looks like, what the boundaries are, and when it’s due. That’s not teaching. That’s hoping.

And yet leaders do this every day. They hand off a project with a vague ask and a tight deadline, then act surprised when the result doesn’t match the picture in their head – the picture they never shared.

Most delegation failures occur within the first 30 seconds. Not because the person receiving the work isn’t capable. Because three things were never made explicit:

What does done look like?

Not the task – the outcome. “Update the client” is a task. “The client knows X, Y, and Z, feels confident about the timeline, and has a single point of contact for questions” is a picture of done. One invites guesswork. The other invites execution.

Where are the boundaries?

Every handoff has invisible edges. Budget limits, stakeholder sensitivities, and decisions that require a check-in before action. When those boundaries aren’t named, capable people will cross them – not out of recklessness, but out of ignorance. And then trust takes a hit that was entirely preventable.

When do we check in?

Not “let me know if you need anything” – that puts the burden on the person with the least context. A real checkpoint sounds like: “Let’s sync on Thursday. Bring me where you are, what’s working, and where you’re stuck.” That’s not micromanaging. That’s making sure no one drives off a cliff in silence.

The teaching parallel matters because delegation is a teaching act, not a management act. Every time you hand something off, you’re either developing someone’s judgment or undermining it. You’re either building a driver or creating a passenger who’ll need you in the car next time, too.

The two-minute version: before your next handoff, answer three questions out loud – to the person, not just in your head.

  • What does done look like?

  • Where are the edges?

  • When do we sync?

That’s it. Three questions. Maybe ninety seconds of your time. The difference between a handoff that builds capability and one that just redistributes stress.

Delegation isn't handing someone a frame. It's making sure there's a picture inside it.

And the leaders who get this right don’t just free up their own calendars – they build teams that don’t need them in the room to make good decisions.

That’s not losing control. That’s the whole point.
